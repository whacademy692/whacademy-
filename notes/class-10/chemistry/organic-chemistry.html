Organic chemistry is the study of carbon-based compounds — the compounds found in living things, fuels, medicines, plastics, foods and perfumes. Every carbon atom forms four bonds, so carbon atoms can join into chains and make a huge number of different compounds. In this unit you learn three big skills: (1) how to draw an organic compound as a displayed formula and as a condensed (normal) structural formula; (2) how an IUPAC name is built from a stem (how many carbons) and a suffix (which family); and (3) how an alcohol and a carboxylic acid join to make a fruity-smelling ester. The six families you must master are alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, alcohols, carboxylic acids and esters (Figure 8.1).

TIPS

Learning Tips & Memory Tricks

Tip: Stems 1 – 4: remember “Mice Eat Peanut Butter” = Meth, Eth, Prop, But (1, 2, 3, 4 carbons). These four come from old names, so they must be learnt by heart.
Tip: Stems 5 – 10: they are the Greek numbers you already know from shapes — pentagon (5), hexagon (6), heptagon (7), octagon (8), nonagon (9), decagon (10).
Tip: Bond-type endings go in alphabetical order a – e – y = 1 – 2 – 3 bonds: -ane = single, -ene = double, -yne = triple. Also, the letter Y has three tips → triple bond.
Tip: Four-bond check (the most important habit): C = 4 lines, O = 2 lines, H = 1 line. After drawing any formula, count the lines on every carbon. Missing or extra hydrogens are the number-one marks-loser in this chapter.
Tip: Numbering rule: number the chain from the end that is closer to the action (double bond, triple bond or –OH) so that the action gets the smaller number. For acids, the –COOH carbon is always C-1, so no number is written.
Tip: Drawing recipe (name → formula), 4 steps: ① stem → write that many C atoms; ② suffix → decide the family; ③ number the carbons and place the double bond / triple bond / –OH / –COOH; ④ add hydrogens until every carbon has four bonds.
Tip: Esterification in one line: “the ACID gives –OH, the ALCOHOL gives H.” Together they leave as water (H₂O) and the two pieces join through an O atom to make the ester link –COO–.
Tip: Naming an ester: start from the single-bonded O. The group attached to that O (from the alcohol) is named first (methyl, ethyl …). The C=O side (from the acid) is named second and ends in -oate. Example: CH₃COOCH₃ = methyl (on the O) + ethanoate (the CH₃–C=O side).
Tip: Practice plan: every day draw 10 formulae from memory, once from the name and once from the formula; cover the answer and check. Use a red pen to box the functional group (C=C, C≡C, –OH, –COOH, –COO–) — the box tells you the family at a glance.
8.0

Organic Chemistry — The Big Picture

CHEM-01

What is organic chemistry?

Organic chemistry is the study of carbon-based compounds, which are fundamental to life and have diverse applications in various industries. Understanding the structures, properties and reactions of organic molecules is crucial for fields such as medicine, materials science and environmental science.

Hydrocarbons and functional groups

A hydrocarbon contains carbon and hydrogen only. Alkanes (all C–C single bonds), alkenes (at least one C=C) and alkynes (at least one C≡C) are hydrocarbons. Other organic compounds contain a functional group — an atom or group of atoms that decides the class of the compound. All compounds with the same functional group belong to the same class. In this chapter: –OH (alcohols), –COOH (carboxylic acids) and –COO– (esters).

8.1

Structural Formula

CHEM-01

What is a structural formula?

A structural formula shows how the different atoms are joined up or arranged in a molecule or compound. It can be drawn in two ways: as a displayed formula or as a condensed (normal) structural formula. The molecular formula (for example C₃H₈) only counts atoms; it does not show how they are joined.

Displayed formula

  • A displayed formula is a full structural formula that shows all the bonds between the atoms as individual lines.
  • One line = single bond, two lines = double bond, three lines = triple bond. More lines means a stronger bond.
  • It is always drawn with the molecule straightened out and flattened, and it shows exactly how all the atoms are joined.
  • It gives as much information as possible, but it is very large and time-consuming to draw.

The four-bond rule

In every organic molecule each carbon atom has four bonds, each oxygen atom has two and each hydrogen atom has one. A double bond counts as two of the four, and a triple bond counts as three.

Common Mistake: Drawing a carbon with only three bonds (a missing H) or with five bonds (an extra H). Always count: 4 lines on every carbon.
Note: The 90° angles of a displayed formula are only a drawing convention. Real molecules are not flat; the book says displayed formulae are “straightened out and flattened”.

Condensed (normal) structural formula

  • It is a simplified version that omits some or all bonds and groups the atoms joined to the same carbon.
  • List the main-chain carbon atoms and the hydrogen atoms attached to them in the sequence they appear, e.g. CH₃ or CH₂ instead of showing every C–H bond.
  • Methane CH₄; ethane CH₃CH₃; propane CH₃CH₂CH₃. The group -CH₃ is called methyl and -CH₂- is called methylene.

CHEM-02

What are alkanes?

Alkanes are hydrocarbons in which all the carbon atoms are joined by single covalent bonds. Their names end in -ane. Methane is the simplest alkane; ethane, propane and butane follow.

Tip: Pattern: each next alkane has one more CH₂. The H count follows CₙH₂ₙ₊₂: C5 → 12, C6 → 14, C7 → 16, C8 → 18, C9 → 20, C10 → 22. If your molecular formula does not fit the pattern, a hydrogen is missing or extra.
Tip: Counting H in an unbranched alkane: each end carbon carries 3 H and each middle carbon carries 2 H. A shorter way to write a long chain: CH₃(CH₂)₃CH₃ means CH₃, three CH₂ groups, then CH₃ (pentane).

Example 8.1 Give the displayed and normal structural formula for pentane

Solution:

  • Step 1: The stem pent means five carbon atoms.
  • Step 2: The ending -ane indicates an alkane (all single bonds).
  • Step 3: Write a string (chain) of five carbon atoms: C – C – C – C – C.
  • Step 4: Attach hydrogen atoms so that every carbon has four bonds: three H on each end carbon and two H on each middle carbon.
  • Step 5: For the condensed formula, write each carbon with its set of H atoms: CH₃-CH₂-CH₂-CH₂-CH₃ or CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂CH₃ or CH₃(CH₂)₃CH₃.

Structural Formulae for Alkenes

CHEM-03

What are alkenes?

Alkenes are hydrocarbons that have one or more double bonds between carbon atoms; their general formula is CₙH₂ₙ. When two carbon atoms share two pairs of electrons, they form a double covalent bond. A doubly bonded carbon atom forms a single bond with two other atoms (4 = 2 for the double bond + 1 + 1).

NameMolecular formulaCondensed formulaC=C is between
EtheneC₂H₄CH₂=CH₂C1 and C2
PropeneC₃H₆CH₃CH=CH₂C1 and C2
But-1-eneC₄H₈CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃C1 and C2
But-2-eneC₄H₈CH₃CH=CHCH₃C2 and C3
Note: But-1-ene and but-2-ene have the same molecular formula (C₄H₈) but different structures (the double bond is in a different place), so they are different compounds. The number in the name tells you where the double bond starts.
Common Mistake: Writing propene as CH₃CH₂=CH₂. The middle carbon would then have five bonds (1 + 2 + 2). The correct formula is CH₃CH=CH₂. (This slip is even printed once in the textbook — see the print-errors box later.)

Example 8.2 Draw structural and condensed formulas for (a) But-1-ene (b) But-2-ene

Solution:

  • Step 1: But- means four carbon atoms.
  • Step 2: The ending -ene indicates an alkene (a C=C double bond).
  • Step 3: Write a string of four carbon atoms and give each a number from one side: C1 – C2 – C3 – C4.
  • Step 4: The number in the name shows where the double bond starts. Make the double bond between that carbon and the next one.
  • Step 5: Attach enough hydrogens to give every carbon four bonds.
  • Step 6: For the condensed formula write each carbon with its set of hydrogens.
  • (a) But-1-ene: double bond between C-1 and C-2 → C=C–C–C → CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH₃ (C1 has 2 H, C2 has 1 H, C3 has 2 H, C4 has 3 H).
  • (b) But-2-ene: double bond between C-2 and C-3 → C–C=C–C → CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃ (C1 has 3 H, C2 has 1 H, C3 has 1 H, C4 has 3 H).

Structural Formulae for Alkynes

CHEM-04

What are alkynes?

Alkynes are hydrocarbons with at least one triple bond between carbon atoms. Those with one triple bond have the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂. Ethyne, also called acetylene, is the simplest alkyne: the two carbons share three pairs of electrons and are joined by a triple bond. Each triply bonded carbon can bond with only one hydrogen atom (3 + 1 = 4 bonds): H–C≡C–H.

NameMolecular formulaCondensed formulaC≡C is between
Ethyne (acetylene)C₂H₂CH≡CHC1 and C2
PropyneC₃H₄CH₃C≡CHC1 and C2 (count from the ≡CH end)
But-1-yneC₄H₆CH≡CCH₂CH₃C1 and C2
But-2-yneC₄H₆CH₃C≡CCH₃C2 and C3
Common Mistake: Giving a triply bonded carbon two hydrogens, e.g. CH₂≡CCH₂CH₃. That carbon would have five bonds. A carbon at the end of a triple bond has only one H: CH≡CCH₂CH₃.

Example 8.3 Draw structural and condensed formulas for (a) But-1-yne (b) But-2-yne

Solution:

  • Step 1: But- means four carbon atoms.
  • Step 2: The ending -yne indicates an alkyne (a C≡C triple bond).
  • Step 3: Write a string of four carbon atoms and number them from one side.
  • Step 4: The number in the name shows the position of the triple bond. Make a triple bond between that carbon and the next one.
  • Step 5: Attach the required number of hydrogens so that every carbon has four bonds.
  • Step 6: For the condensed formula write each carbon with its hydrogens.
  • (a) But-1-yne: C1≡C2–C3–C4 → H–C≡C–CH2–CH3 → CH≡C–CH₂–CH₃ (C1 has 1 H, C2 has none, C3 has 2 H, C4 has 3 H).
  • (b) But-2-yne: C1–C2≡C3–C4 → CH₃–C≡C–CH₃ (C1 has 3 H, C2 and C3 have none, C4 has 3 H).

Naming Organic Compounds (iupac System)

CHEM-02

Why do we use IUPAC names?

IUPAC (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) gives a standard set of rules for naming organic molecules from their structure. It helps chemists everywhere identify and tell compounds apart regardless of language or region, and it avoids the confusion of common (trivial) names, which vary widely and lack specificity. An IUPAC name tells you the type and position of the functional group, the length of the carbon chain and whether there are double or triple bonds.

Two parts of every name: STEM + SUFFIX

  • The stem tells how many carbon atoms are in the longest chain.
  • The suffix (the ending) tells the functional group, i.e. the family. It is placed after the stem.
  • Example: stem meth = one carbon and ending -ane = alkane, so one-carbon alkane = methane, CH₄.

8.2.1

Naming Alkanes

CHEM-02

Alkanes are hydrocarbons in which all the carbon atoms are joined by single covalent bonds. Their names are coded with the ending -ane; adding the stem before -ane gives the name. One-carbon alkane = methane CH₄; two-carbon = ethane CH₃CH₃; three-carbon = propane CH₃CH₂CH₃; four-carbon = butane CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃, and so on.

Q62 How are the names of alkanes formed?
Alkane names end in -ane; the stem for the number of carbons is added before -ane, e.g. meth + ane = methane, prop + ane = propane.

Butane, CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ (C₄H₁₀).
8.2.2

Naming Alkenes

CHEM-03

Alkenes contain a carbon–carbon double bond, shown in the name by the ending -ene. A 2-carbon chain with a double bond is ethene CH₂=CH₂; a 3-carbon chain is propene CH₃CH=CH₂. With larger chains the position of the double bond is shown by a number: the chain is numbered from the end nearest to the double bond, and the number of the carbon where the double bond starts is written before “-ene”. A 4-carbon chain with the double bond starting on C-1 is but-1-ene (CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃); if it starts on C-2 it is but-2-ene (CH₃CH=CHCH₃).

Note: How do you know from which end to number? Remember the rule: numbering is started from the end which gives the smaller number to the name. The same rule is used for alkynes and alcohols.
Common Mistake: Naming from the wrong end, e.g. writing pent-3-ene or but-3-ene. Always check both directions and write the smaller number: CH₃CH₂CH=CHCH₃ is pent-2-ene, not pent-3-ene.
Tip: Ethene and propene need no number because the double bond can be in only one place. From four carbons (but-) onwards the position must be written. A double bond needs two carbons, so there is no “methene”.

What are alcohols?

Alcohols are characterised by the hydroxyl group (–OH) attached to a hydrocarbon chain. This is shown by the ending -ol. To name an alcohol, take the name of the alkane with the same number of carbons and replace the final “e” by “ol”: methane → methanol, ethane → ethanol.

CHEM-06

What are carboxylic acids?

Carboxylic acids are characterised by the carboxyl group –COOH attached to a hydrocarbon chain. This is shown by the ending -oic acid. To name an acid, take the alkane with the same number of carbons and replace the final “e” by “oic acid”. In the carboxyl group one carbon is double-bonded to an oxygen (C=O) and single-bonded to an –OH group; that carbon is part of the chain.

The position of the carbonyl carbon (the C of –COOH) is not mentioned in the name because it is always carbon number 1: numbering always starts from the carbonyl carbon in carboxylic acids. So there is no “propan-1-oic acid”, just propanoic acid.

Alcohol –OH or acid –COOH?

Common Mistake: Forgetting that the –COOH carbon counts as one of the carbon atoms. Ethanoic acid CH₃COOH has two carbons (CH₃ and COOH); butanoic acid CH₃CH₂CH₂COOH has four.

8.3

Esters

CHEM-08

What are esters?

Esters are organic compounds derived from carboxylic acids and alcohols. A carboxylic acid has the –COOH group (general formula RCOOH). When the –OH of the carboxyl group is replaced by an –OR′ group from an alcohol, an ester is formed, with the general formula RCOOR′. R and R′ may be the same or different. Esters have a fruity odour.

Esterification

The reaction between a carboxylic acid and an alcohol to form an ester and water is called esterification. It is carried out in the presence of a few drops of sulphuric acid as a catalyst (with heating).

RCOOH + R′OH → RCOOR′ + H₂O CH₃COOH + CH₃OH → CH₃COOCH₃ + H₂O ethanoic acid + methanol → methyl ethanoate + water
Note: Your textbook says the catalyst is “dilute sulphuric acid”. Many other books say “concentrated sulphuric acid”. In the exam write what your book says: “a few drops of sulphuric acid as a catalyst”.

Naming an ester: two parts

  • First word: the alkyl group attached to the single-bonded O — it comes from the alcohol (methanol → methyl, ethanol → ethyl, propan-1-ol → propyl, butan-1-ol → butyl).
  • Second word: the name of the carboxylic acid with “-ic acid” replaced by “-ate” (ethanoic acid → ethanoate, propanoic acid → propanoate).
  • Example: methyl ethanoate gets “methyl” from methanol and “ethanoate” from ethanoic acid.

Ten rules to remember

  • Carbon forms 4 bonds, oxygen 2, hydrogen 1.
  • Name = stem (number of C) + suffix (family).
  • a – e – y = single – double – triple bond (-ane, -ene, -yne).
  • Number from the end that gives the smaller number to C=C, C≡C or –OH.
  • Alcohol: change “e” to “ol”. Acid: change “e” to “oic acid”.
  • –COOH carbon is always C-1, so no number is written.
  • Esterification: acid + alcohol → ester + water (few drops of sulphuric acid, heat).
  • The acid gives –OH, the alcohol gives H.
  • Ester name: alkyl (from alcohol) first, -oate (from acid) second.
  • Same molecular formula ≠ same compound (check the structure).

2. Short Answer Questions

Short i · CHEM-01Q141 Define the term displayed structural formula in organic chemistry.
A displayed structural formula is a full structural formula that shows all the atoms and all the bonds between them as individual lines (one line for a single bond, two for a double bond, three for a triple bond). It shows exactly how all the atoms in the molecule are joined.
Short ii · CHEM-02Q142 Explain the difference between an alkane and an alkene.
Alkane: a hydrocarbon in which all carbon atoms are joined by single bonds only; name ends in -ane; general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂; e.g. ethane CH₃CH₃.
Alkene: a hydrocarbon with at least one C=C double bond; name ends in -ene; general formula CₙH₂ₙ; e.g. ethene CH₂=CH₂. An alkene has two hydrogens fewer than the alkane with the same number of carbons.
Short iii · CHEM-07Q143 How can you distinguish between an alcohol and a carboxylic acid by their structural formulae?
An alcohol has an –OH group attached to a carbon chain (C–O–H), e.g. CH₃CH₂OH; its name ends in -ol.
A carboxylic acid has the –COOH group, i.e. C=O and –OH on the same carbon, e.g. CH₃COOH; its name ends in -oic acid. The acid therefore has the extra double-bonded oxygen.
Short iv · CHEM-05Q144 Write the name for CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–OH.
Five carbons (pent) with an –OH on the end carbon (alcohol, -ol; numbering from the –OH end): pentan-1-ol.
Short v · CHEM-08Q145 Describe the process of esterification.
Esterification is the reaction of a carboxylic acid with an alcohol, carried out in the presence of a few drops of sulphuric acid as a catalyst (with heating). The –OH of the carboxyl group of the acid is replaced by the –OR′ group of the alcohol; the –OH from the acid and the H from the alcohol form water.
Carboxylic acid + alcohol → ester + water: RCOOH + R′OH → RCOOR′ + H₂O. Example: CH₃COOH + CH₃OH → CH₃COOCH₃ + H₂O (methyl ethanoate, which has a fruity smell).
Short vi · CHEM-02Q146 Given the condensed structural formula CH₃CH₂CH₃, what is the displayed structural formula?
CH₃CH₂CH₃ is propane: three carbons in a row; each end carbon has 3 H and the middle carbon has 2 H (2 C–C bonds + 8 C–H bonds), as shown in Figure 8.24.

(ii) Ethanoic acid CH₃COOH: CH₃ (3 H) joined to a carbon that is double-bonded to O and single-bonded to O–H. Both are drawn in Figure 8.16 (earlier).

3. Long Questions

Long i · CHEM-01Q155 Discuss the significance of functional groups in organic chemistry and provide examples.
A functional group is an atom or group of atoms in a molecule that decides the class of the compound.
1. Classification: all compounds with the same functional group belong to the same class (all compounds with C=C are alkenes; with –OH, alcohols).
2. Naming: the functional group gives the suffix of the IUPAC name (-ene, -yne, -ol, -oic acid, -yl … -oate) and decides how the chain is numbered.
3. Recognition: by looking for the group in a formula we can tell the family and expect similar behaviour from its members. The same carbon chain with a different group gives a different compound: ethane CH₃CH₃, ethanol CH₃CH₂OH and ethanoic acid CH₃COOH.

Long iii · CHEM-08Q157 Explain the reaction between an alcohol and a carboxylic acid to form an ester.
An alcohol (R′OH) reacts with a carboxylic acid (RCOOH) when they are heated with a few drops of sulphuric acid, which acts as a catalyst. This reaction is called esterification.
The –OH of the carboxyl group of the acid is replaced by the –OR′ group of the alcohol. The –OH from the acid and the H from the alcohol combine to form water, and the remaining parts join through an oxygen atom as the ester group –COO–.

Condensed (normal) structural formulaA simplified formula that omits some or all of the bonds and groups the atoms that are joined to the same carbon atom, e.g. CH₃CH₃.
AlkanesHydrocarbons in which all the carbon atoms are joined to each other with single covalent bonds.
AlkenesHydrocarbons that have at least one carbon–carbon double bond (C=C).
AlkynesHydrocarbons which have at least one triple bond between carbon atoms.
AlcoholsOrganic compounds characterised by the hydroxyl group (–OH) attached to a hydrocarbon chain.
Carboxylic acidsOrganic compounds characterised by the carboxyl group (–COOH) attached to a hydrocarbon chain.
EstersOrganic compounds derived from carboxylic acids and alcohols; general formula RCOOR′.
IUPACInternational Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry.
StemThe part of an organic name that indicates the number of carbon atoms in the longest chain.
SuffixThe ending of the name, placed after the stem, which indicates the functional group (family).
EsterificationThe reaction between a carboxylic acid and an alcohol to form an ester and water, in the presence of a few drops of sulphuric acid as catalyst.
