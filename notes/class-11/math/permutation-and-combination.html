Chapter Psychology

What this unit is really testing, the three decisions to ask, how to pick the right tool, and where students lose marks.

The one idea behind this unit

1. What is this whole unit really testing?
• What is the idea of the chapter?• How should I study Permutation and Combination?

Every problem is the same question in a different costume: “In how many ways…?” The examiner does not test whether you can multiply. He tests whether you can read the situation and choose the right counting tool.

In simple words

Think of three switches. Switch 1: does the order matter? Switch 2: are some objects alike? Switch 3: is there a condition (together, never together, even, first digit not 0)? Set the switches first, then pick the formula.

Exam tip

After every question ask yourself: which switch did this one test? Examiners reuse the same few ideas with new stories (digits, letters, books, flags, seats, committees, points on a circle).

4. Why do students lose marks here?
• Common errors in Permutation and Combination
  • Permutation vs combination mix-up. The formulas are easy; picking the wrong one is the usual loss. Say decision 1 aloud.
  • Forgetting the zero rule in digit problems.
  • Cancelling across + and − in factorial fractions: \(4!-2!\) is not \(2!\).
  • Writing \(n!\) when the question needs \({}^{n}P_{r}\) (arranging only some of the objects).
  • Not rejecting a negative \(n\). A factorial equation can give \(n=-3\); reject it and say why.
  • Circular vs line. In a line the first seat is special; in a circle nobody is.

Part 1 M11-06-01

Rule of Product

Section 6.1 — the counting engine behind every formula of this unit. AND means multiply, OR means add.

Key points
  • Rule of product: \(m\times n\times p\cdots\) for events that all happen one after another.
  • AND → multiply, OR → add.
  • Without repetition the number of choices shrinks by one at each place.

Counting without listing

1. What is the Rule of Product (Fundamental Counting Principle)?
• State the fundamental principle of counting• Define the multiplication rule of counting

Counting is a basic tool: we compute the number of outcomes without listing them. A coin has 2 outcomes and a die has 6. Tossing both gives the outcomes (H,1), (H,2), …, (T,6) — twelve of them — and we can find 12 without listing:

\(2\times6=12\)

Rule of Product. If an event A can happen in \(m\) ways and an event B in \(n\) ways, then the pair (A, B) can happen in \(m\times n\) ways. For three events A, B, C in \(m\), \(n\), \(p\) ways, the triplet can happen in \(m\times n\times p\) ways.

In simple words

3 shirts and 2 pairs of trousers: each shirt goes with either pair, so \(3\times2=6\) outfits. “This and that” → multiply. “This or that” → add.

2. When do I multiply and when do I add?
• Difference between the rule of sum and the rule of product
Key fact

Use the rule of product when events happen one after another and all must happen (AND → multiply). Use the rule of sum when only one of several separate cases happens (OR → add). Case-by-case counting such as “at most 2 women” uses add.

Common mistake

Adding when you should multiply. Ask: do I need event 1 and event 2? Multiply. Event 1 or event 2 (not both)? Add.

3. Worked picture: how choices shrink
• Repetition allowed and not allowed

A 3-digit code from the digits 1 to 5: with repetition allowed each of the 3 places has 5 choices, without repetition the choices shrink:

\(\text{with repetition: }5\times5\times5=125\)
\(\text{no repetition: }5\times4\times3=60\)
📝 The shrinking choices 5, 4, 3 are the seed of permutations (Section 6.3).
Part 2 M11-06-01

Factorial Notation

Section 6.2 — a short way to write long products, how to simplify them, and how to solve factorial equations.

Key points
  • \(n!=n(n-1)\cdots2\cdot1\) and \(0!=1\).
  • \(n!=n\,(n-1)!\) opens any factorial one step.
  • To simplify: write the larger factorial down to the smaller and cancel.
  • In factorial equations never expand — and always reject a negative \(n\).

Factorial notation

1. What is a factorial? Define \(n!\).
• Define factorial notation• What does 5! mean?

Factorial notation was introduced by the French mathematician Christian Kramp in 1808. The factorial of a positive integer \(n\) is the product of all the positive integers from \(n\) down to 1:

\(n!=n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots3\times2\times1\)
\(n\)0123456
\(n!\)112624120720
In simple words

\(n!\) = the number of ways to line up \(n\) different things. 3 friends can stand in a line in \(3!=6\) ways.

📝 Scientific calculators have a factorial key (above the \(x^{-1}\) key, used with SHIFT). For example \(4!=24\).
2. Why is \(0!=1\)?
• Prove that 0! = 1• Value of 0 factorial

From \((n+1)!=(n+1)\,n!\) put \(n=0\):

\(1!=(0+1)\,0!\;\Rightarrow\;1=1\times0!\;\Rightarrow\;0!=1\)
In simple words

There is exactly one way to arrange nothing: do nothing. It is also needed so that \({}^{n}C_{n}=\frac{n!}{n!\,0!}=1\) works.

Common mistake

Writing \(0!=0\). It is the most common factorial error — remember \(0!=1\).

3. Recursive rule: cut a big factorial down to a smaller one
• How do I simplify a ratio of factorials?

\(n!=n\,(n-1)!\), so a factorial can always be opened one step (or more):

\(10!=10\times9\times8!\)
\((n+2)!=(n+2)(n+1)\,n!\)
Exam tip

To simplify a ratio of factorials write the larger factorial down to the smaller one and cancel: \(\frac{8!}{5!}=\frac{8\times7\times6\times5!}{5!}=336\).

4. Two warnings: \((m+n)!\) and \((mn)!\)
• Is (m + n)! = m! + n!?

Point to be noted (textbook): \(2!\times4!=(2\cdot1)(4\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1)=48\) but \((2\times4)!=8!=40320\). In general

\((m\times n)!\ne m!\times n!\quad\text{and}\quad(m+n)!\ne m!+n!\)
Common mistake

Thinking \((2n)!=2\times n!\). Check with \(n=3\): \(6!=720\) but \(2\times3!=12\).

Common mistake

“Cancelling” across + or −: \(\frac{4!-2!}{3!+5!}\) is not \(\frac{2!}{8!}\). Evaluate each factorial first: \(\frac{4!-2!}{3!+5!}=\frac{24-2}{6+120}=\frac{22}{126}=\frac{11}{63}\).

Book examples and extra examples

5. Book Example 1 — Simplify the following (p. 163)Basic
• Simplify 8!/5!• Simplify (n+1)!/(n−2)!

Question. (i) \(\frac{8!}{5!}\) (ii) \(\frac{1}{4!\times3!}+\frac{7}{5!\times2!}\) (iii) \(\frac{(n+1)!}{(n-2)!}\)

Solution.

(i) \(\frac{8!}{5!}=\frac{8\cdot7\cdot6\cdot5!}{5!}=8\cdot7\cdot6=336\)
(ii) \(\frac{1}{4!\,3!}+\frac{7}{5!\,2!}=\frac{5}{5!\,3!}+\frac{21}{5!\,3!}=\frac{26}{5!\,3!}=\frac{26}{720}=\frac{13}{360}\)
(iii) \(\frac{(n+1)!}{(n-2)!}=\frac{(n+1)\,n\,(n-1)\,(n-2)!}{(n-2)!}=(n+1)\,n\,(n-1)=n^{3}-n\)
Exam tip

In (ii) make both denominators the same factorial first, then add the numerators — never add the denominators.

6. Book Example 2 — Write the following in factorial form (p. 163)Intermediate
• Express in factorial form

Question. (i) \(\frac{13\cdot12\cdot11\cdot9}{6\cdot5\cdot4}\) (ii) \(\frac{(n-4)(n-3)(n-1)}{n(n-1)}\) (as printed in the book)

Solution.

(i) \(\frac{13\cdot12\cdot11\cdot9}{6\cdot5\cdot4}=\frac{13\cdot12\cdot11\cdot10!\cdot9\cdot8!}{6\cdot5\cdot4\cdot3!}\times\frac{3!}{10!\,8!}=\frac{13!\,9!\,3!}{10!\,8!\,6!}\)
(ii) \(\frac{(n-4)(n-3)(n-2)}{n(n-1)}=\frac{(n-2)(n-3)(n-4)\,(n-5)!}{n(n-1)\,(n-2)!}\times\frac{(n-2)!}{(n-5)!}=\frac{[(n-2)!]^{2}}{n!\,(n-5)!}\)
⚑ The printed question has \((n-1)\) as the last numerator factor, but the book's solution uses \((n-2)\). The solution above follows the book's solution; please confirm with your teacher.
7. Extra Example A — Evaluate \(\frac{10!}{8!\times2!}\)Basic
\(\frac{10!}{8!\,2!}=\frac{10\times9\times8!}{8!\times2}=\frac{10\times9}{2}=45\)
8. Extra Example B — Simplify \(\frac{(n+2)!}{n!}-\frac{(n+1)!}{(n-1)!}\)Intermediate
\(\frac{(n+2)!}{n!}-\frac{(n+1)!}{(n-1)!}=(n+2)(n+1)-(n+1)\,n=(n+1)[(n+2)-n]=2(n+1)\)
\(\text{Check, }n=3:\ \frac{5!}{3!}-\frac{4!}{2!}=20-12=8=2\times4\ \checkmark\)
9. Extra Example C — Highest power of 2 dividing \(8!\)Advance
\(8!=8\times7\times6\times5\times4\times3\times2\times1\)
\(\text{powers of }2:\ 8\to3,\ 6\to1,\ 4\to2,\ 2\to1\ \Rightarrow\ 3+1+2+1=7\)
\(\text{Check: }8!=40320=2^{7}\times315\ \checkmark\)

Factorial equations

10. How do I solve a factorial equation like \((n+2)!=132\cdot n!\)?Intermediate
• Find n: (n+2)! = 132·n!
Short trick — “write the big one in terms of the small one”
  • Never expand. Write \((n+2)!=(n+2)(n+1)\,n!\).
  • Cancel \(n!\) from both sides.
  • Solve the small quadratic and reject the negative root.
\((n+2)!=132\,n!\;\Rightarrow\;(n+2)(n+1)\,n!=132\,n!\;\Rightarrow\;(n+2)(n+1)=132\)
\(n^{2}+3n-130=0\;\Rightarrow\;(n-10)(n+13)=0\;\Rightarrow\;n=10\ (\text{reject }n=-13)\)
Exam tip

Check by putting \(n=10\) back: \(12!=132\times10!\) because \(12\times11=132\).

Part 3 M11-06-02

Permutation: Order Matters

Section 6.3 — arrangements of distinct objects, alike objects, circles and key chains, and the cryptography application.

Key points
  • \({}^{n}P_{r}=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\) — arrange \(r\) of \(n\) different objects (order matters).
  • \({}^{n}P_{n}=n!\) and \(0!=1\).
  • Alike objects: divide by the factorial of each repeat — \(\frac{n!}{n_{1}!\,n_{2}!\cdots}\).
  • Circle: \((n-1)!\). Circle that flips: \(\frac{(n-1)!}{2}\).

Permutation of distinct objects

1. What is a permutation? Define \({}^{n}P_{r}\).
• Define permutation• What is meant by nPr?

The number of ways in which \(r\) objects out of \(n\) objects (\(0\le r\le n\)) can be arranged in a definite order is called a permutation. It is written \({}^{n}P_{r}\) and read “\(n\)-permutation-\(r\)”.

In simple words

The word to watch for is order. If changing the order gives a different result (ABC ≠ CBA, 123 ≠ 321, seat 1 ≠ seat 2) it is a permutation.

2. Derive the formula \({}^{n}P_{r}=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\)
• Prove nPr = n!/(n−r)!• Derive the permutation formula

Fill \(r\) places one by one:

  1. 1st object: \(n\) choices
  2. 2nd object: \(n-1\) choices
  3. 3rd object: \(n-2\) choices
  4. … and so on …
  5. \(r\)-th object: \(n-(r-1)=n-r+1\) choices

Multiply, then multiply and divide by \((n-r)!\):

\({}^{n}P_{r}=n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-r+1)=\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-r+1)\,(n-r)!}{(n-r)!}=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\)
\({}^{n}P_{r}=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\)
In simple words

Imagine \(r\) empty seats and \(n\) people. 1st seat: \(n\) choices; 2nd seat: one person is gone, so \(n-1\); and so on. The formula says: arrange everybody (\(n!\)), then throw away the order of the \(n-r\) people who never got a seat (divide by \((n-r)!\)).

3. Deductions: \({}^{n}P_{n}=n!\) and \(0!=1\)
• Show that nPn = n!

Put \(r=n\):

\({}^{n}P_{n}=n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots1=n!\quad\text{and also}\quad{}^{n}P_{n}=\frac{n!}{(n-n)!}=\frac{n!}{0!}\)

Since \({}^{n}P_{n}=n!\), we get \(n!=\frac{n!}{0!}\), so \(0!=\frac{n!}{n!}=1\).

\(0!=1\quad\text{and}\quad{}^{n}P_{n}=n!\)
4. Quick way to compute \({}^{n}P_{r}\)
• How to calculate nPr quickly
Exam tip

Write \(r\) factors starting from \(n\) and going down. You never need to expand \(n!\) fully.

\({}^{8}P_{6}=8\times7\times6\times5\times4\times3=20160\quad(6\text{ factors})\)
Common mistake

Taking the wrong number of factors. \({}^{10}P_{3}=10\times9\times8\) (three factors), not \(10\times9\times8\times7\).

5. Book Example 3 — Vehicle number plates (p. 165)Basic

Question. A vehicle number plate consists of four digits. Count the different plates using distinct digits from 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.

  1. 1st digit: 6 choices
  2. 2nd digit: 5 choices (the chosen digit cannot be chosen again)

6. Book Example 4 — Books on a shelf (p. 165)Basic

Question. A student has four different subjects' books and wishes to arrange them on a shelf. In how many ways can he arrange the books?

\(4\times3\times2\times1=24=4!\)

In general, \(n\) distinct objects can be arranged in \(n!\) ways.

7. Book Example 5 — Seating in a row (p. 166)Basic

Question. In how many ways can 6 persons out of 8 be seated in a row?

\({}^{8}P_{6}=\frac{8!}{(8-6)!}=\frac{8!}{2!}=\frac{8\cdot7\cdot6\cdot5\cdot4\cdot3\cdot2!}{2!}=20160\)

Objects that are alike

8. Permutation when some objects are alike
• Arrangements when some objects are alike• Permutation of repeated letters

Let \(n\) objects be not all distinct: \(n_{1}\) alike of the 1st kind, \(n_{2}\) of the 2nd kind, …, \(n_{k}\) of the \(k\)-th kind, so \(n=n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{k}\). Let \(X\) be the number of arrangements. If the alike objects were distinct we would get \(X\times n_{1}!\,n_{2}!\cdots n_{k}!\) arrangements, but all-distinct arrangements are \(n!\), so

\(X\times n_{1}!\,n_{2}!\cdots n_{k}!=n!\;\Rightarrow\;X=\frac{n!}{n_{1}!\,n_{2}!\cdots n_{k}!}\)
\(\frac{n!}{n_{1}!\,n_{2}!\cdots n_{k}!}\)
In simple words

If two letters are identical, swapping them changes nothing. \(n!\) counts every swap as new, so divide the useless swaps away: \(2!\) for two alike, \(3!\) for three alike. Example, the word AAB: \(3!=6\) would list AAB, AAB, ABA, ABA, BAA, BAA, but only 3 are different: \(\frac{3!}{2!}=3\).

Common mistake

Forgetting to divide for repeated letters. STATESMAN has 9 letters but S, T and A each appear twice: divide by \(2!\) three times.

Exam tip

Before counting the arrangements of a word, write each distinct letter and how many times it appears in a small table.

9. Book Example 6 — Arrangements of EVENING (p. 166)Basic

Question. How many different arrangements of the letters used in the word EVENING can be made by using all the letters?

EVENING has 7 letters; E is repeated 2 times and N is repeated 2 times.

\(\frac{7!}{2!\times2!}=\frac{7\cdot6\cdot5\cdot4\cdot3\cdot2!}{2!\times2\cdot1}=1260\)
10. Extra Example F — Arrangements of MISSISSIPPIIntermediate

11 letters: M × 1, I × 4, S × 4, P × 2.

\(\frac{11!}{1!\,4!\,4!\,2!}=\frac{39916800}{24\times24\times2}=34650\)

Circular arrangements

11. Circular permutation: why \((n-1)!\)?
• Circular permutation formula• Arrangements around a round table

The four line arrangements abcd, bcda, cdab, dabc are all different along a line but all the same along a circle, because the position of each object with respect to the others is the same. So the circular arrangements of 4 objects are \(\frac{4!}{4}=\frac{4\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1}{4}=6\). For \(n\) objects:

\(\frac{n!}{n}=\frac{n\,(n-1)!}{n}=(n-1)!\)
\((n-1)!\)
In simple words

Seat 4 friends at a round table. If everybody moves one seat clockwise, the picture is the same. In a row those 4 rotations look like 4 different lines, so a circle has 4 times fewer arrangements. Shortcut: fix one person in one seat (that removes the rotation) and arrange the other \(n-1\) people: \((n-1)!\).

Key fact

Why divide by \(n\)? Each circular arrangement can be “cut open” at \(n\) different places and written as \(n\) different lines.

Common mistake

Using \(n!\) for a round table. In a circle there is no first seat, so rotations of the same arrangement count once: \((n-1)!\).

12. Book Example 7 — Circular table (p. 167)Basic

Question. In how many ways can 8 people sit around a circular table for dinner?

\((8-1)!=7!=5040\)
13. Book Example 8 — Men and women at a round table (p. 167)Intermediate

Question. There are 5 men and 5 women in a party. Find the number of ways in which they can be seated at a round table if (i) anyone can occupy any seat, (ii) men and women have alternate seats.

(i) \((10-1)!=9!=362880\)
(ii) \((5-1)!\times5!=4!\times5!=24\times120=2880\)

In (ii) seat the 5 men round the table first: \((5-1)!=4!\) ways. That creates 5 gaps (one between each pair of neighbouring men), and the 5 women fill the 5 gaps in \(5!\) ways.

14. Book Check Point 1 — OPTICAL, vowels always together (p. 167)Intermediate

Question. In how many different ways can the letters of the word OPTICAL be arranged so that the vowels always come together?

  1. OPTICAL has 7 different letters; vowels O, I, A (3), consonants P, T, C, L (4)
  2. Treat the 3 vowels as one block: 4 consonants + 1 block = 5 objects
  3. Arrange the 5 objects: \(5!=120\)
  4. Arrange the vowels inside the block: \(3!=6\)
\(5!\times3!=120\times6=720\)
15. Key chain, necklace, garland: the flip-over rule
• Arrangements on a necklace

When objects are arranged in a circle but the whole arrangement can be flipped over, the anticlockwise arrangement turns into the clockwise one after flipping and they count as the same. So

\(\frac{(n-1)!}{2}\)
\(\text{4 objects: }\frac{(4-1)!}{2}=\frac{3!}{2}=\frac{6}{2}=3\)
In simple words

A necklace can be turned over. Turning it over reads the beads in the opposite direction, but it is the same necklace. Every necklace is counted twice in \((n-1)!\), so divide by 2.

Exam tip

Key chain, necklace, bracelet, garland (anything you can turn over) → \(\frac{(n-1)!}{2}\). Round table with people (cannot flip) → \((n-1)!\).

16. Extra Example I — Six different beads on a necklaceBasic
\(\frac{(6-1)!}{2}=\frac{120}{2}=60\)
17. Extra Example H — Round table, two people NOT togetherAdvance

7 people sit at a round table. In how many ways can two particular people A and B not sit together?

\(\text{all: }(7-1)!=720\)
\(\text{together: }(6-1)!\times2!=120\times2=240\)
\(\text{not together}=720-240=480\)

Cryptography

18. Use of permutation in cryptography (p. 168)
• Encrypt and decrypt a word using a permutation• Application of permutation in cryptography

A permutation can be used as a secret code. To encrypt the word PAK label P as 1, A as 2, K as 3. There are \(3!=6\) encrypted words:

The six codes of PAK
Encrypted wordPermutation code
PAK(1 2 3)
PKA(1 3 2)
AKP(2 3 1)
APK(2 1 3)
KAP(3 2 1)
KPA(3 1 2)

If we want it encrypted as APK, i.e. code (2 1 3), it is decrypted by putting the labels back in the order 1 2 3, which gives PAK.

Key fact

Encrypt: label the letters 1, 2, 3, … and write the letters in the order the code tells you. Decrypt: each letter of the coded word carries the label the code gives it; rewrite the letters in the order of the labels 1, 2, 3, ….

In simple words

Think of a seating chart. Each letter has a number tag (its label). The code is just the order in which you read the tags.

Part 4 M11-06-03

Combination: Order Does Not Matter

Section 6.4 — selections, the formula and its proofs, deductions and Pascal's rule.

Key points
  • \({}^{n}C_{r}=\frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}\) and \({}^{n}C_{r}\times r!={}^{n}P_{r}\).
  • \({}^{n}C_{n}={}^{n}C_{0}=1\), \({}^{n}C_{1}=n\), \({}^{n}C_{r}={}^{n}C_{n-r}\).
  • Pascal: \({}^{n}C_{r}+{}^{n}C_{r-1}={}^{n+1}C_{r}\).
  • \({}^{n}C_{p}={}^{n}C_{q}\) gives \(p=q\) or \(p+q=n\).

Combinations: order does not matter

1. What is a combination? Define \({}^{n}C_{r}\).
• Define combination• Difference between permutation and combination

In permutation we arrange objects in a definite order. If order is not important, the selection is called a combination. Take three objects a, b, c: abc, acb, bac, bca, cab, cba are six different permutations, but as a combination they are all the same single selection. The number of combinations of \(r\) objects out of \(n\) distinct objects is written \({}^{n}C_{r}\) or \(\binom{n}{r}\).

In simple words

Choosing a team of 3 from 10 students: the team {A, B, C} is the same team however you list the names. In \({}^{10}P_{3}\) every team is counted \(3!=6\) times (ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA), so \({}^{10}C_{3}=\frac{{}^{10}P_{3}}{3!}\).

2. Prove that \({}^{n}C_{r}=\frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}\)
• Prove nCr = n!/(r!(n−r)!)• Derive the combination formula

Let \(X\) be the number of combinations of \(r\) objects out of \(n\) distinct objects. Taking these \(r\) objects in some order can be done in \(r!\) ways, but in combinations all those \(r!\) ways count as one. So \(r!\text{ permutations}=1\text{ combination}\). The total number of permutations of \(r\) out of \(n\) is \({}^{n}P_{r}\), hence

\(X\times r!={}^{n}P_{r}\;\Rightarrow\;X=\frac{1}{r!}\times\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}=\frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}\)
\({}^{n}C_{r}=\frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}\qquad{}^{n}C_{r}\times r!={}^{n}P_{r}\)
Key fact

\({}^{n}P_{r}={}^{n}C_{r}\times r!\) — choose first (\({}^{n}C_{r}\)), then arrange the chosen \(r\) objects (\(r!\)). This one line joins the two halves of the unit.

3. Deductions: \({}^{n}C_{n}={}^{n}C_{0}=1\), \({}^{n}C_{r}={}^{n}C_{n-r}\), \({}^{n}C_{1}=n\)
• Properties of nCr• Show that nCn = nC0 = 1

(i) \({}^{n}C_{n}={}^{n}C_{0}=1\)

\({}^{n}C_{n}=\frac{n!}{n!\,(n-n)!}=\frac{n!}{n!\,0!}=\frac{1}{0!}=1\)
\({}^{n}C_{0}=\frac{n!}{0!\,(n-0)!}=\frac{n!}{1\times n!}=1\)

(ii) \({}^{n}C_{r}={}^{n}C_{n-r}\) — replace \(r\) by \(n-r\):

\({}^{n}C_{n-r}=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!\,[n-(n-r)]!}=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!\,r!}={}^{n}C_{r}\)

(iii) \({}^{n}C_{1}={}^{n}C_{n-1}=n\)

\({}^{n}C_{1}=\frac{n!}{1!\,(n-1)!}=\frac{n\,(n-1)!}{(n-1)!}=n\)
\({}^{n}C_{n-1}=\frac{n!}{(n-1)!\,1!}=n\)
In simple words

Choosing 3 students to go on a trip is the same decision as choosing the 7 who stay: \({}^{10}C_{3}={}^{10}C_{7}\). This is why \({}^{n}C_{p}={}^{n}C_{q}\) also happens when \(p+q=n\).

Exam tip

Use \({}^{n}C_{r}={}^{n}C_{n-r}\) to shorten the work: \({}^{16}C_{11}={}^{16}C_{5}\) (only 5 factors, not 11).

4. Pascal's rule: \({}^{n}C_{r}+{}^{n}C_{r-1}={}^{n+1}C_{r}\)
• Prove Pascal's rule for combinations

Write both terms over a common denominator and add:

\({}^{n}C_{r}+{}^{n}C_{r-1}=\frac{n!}{r\,(r-1)!\,(n-r)!}+\frac{n!}{(r-1)!\,(n-r+1)\,(n-r)!}=\frac{n!}{(r-1)!\,(n-r)!}\left[\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{n-r+1}\right]\)
\(=\frac{n!}{(r-1)!\,(n-r)!}\times\frac{n+1}{r\,(n-r+1)}=\frac{(n+1)\,n!}{r!\,(n-r+1)!}=\frac{(n+1)!}{r!\,(n+1-r)!}={}^{n+1}C_{r}\)
In simple words

In a group of \(n+1\) people there is one special person X. A team of \(r\) either includes X (choose \(r-1\) more from the other \(n\): \({}^{n}C_{r-1}\)) or excludes X (choose all \(r\) from the other \(n\): \({}^{n}C_{r}\)). Together: \({}^{n+1}C_{r}\).

5. When is \({}^{n}C_{p}={}^{n}C_{q}\)?
• Find n if nC5 = nC8
\({}^{n}C_{p}={}^{n}C_{q}\;\Rightarrow\;p=q\ \text{ or }\ p+q=n\)
\({}^{n}C_{5}={}^{n}C_{8}\;\Rightarrow\;n=5+8=13\)
Common mistake

Writing “\({}^{n}C_{p}={}^{n}C_{q}\) ⇒ \(p=q\)” only. The other possibility \(p+q=n\) is the one the exercises test.

2. Handling conditions: together, never together, gap idea
• Arrangements when two persons are always together• No two persons together
In simple words

Glue idea: if some people must sit together, glue them into one “super-person”, arrange the super-persons, then shuffle the people inside the glue.

Gap idea: “no two together” — seat one group first, then drop the others into the gaps between them (\(n\) objects in a line make \(n+1\) gaps; in a circle they make \(n\) gaps).

\(\text{together: }(n-k+1)!\times k!\)

Exam tip

For a restricted place (an even number, a number ending in 5) fill that place first; for a number with the digit 0 in the list, the first place cannot be 0.

Exam tips
  • Ask first: does the order matter? Order matters, use \({}^{n}P_{r}\); only the group matters, use \({}^{n}C_{r}\).
  • Write \(\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\) first, then cancel. Open only the larger factorial and let the smaller one cancel.
  • Remember \(0!=1\). It is the most common factorial mistake.
  • For "always together" tie the group into one block and multiply by the arrangements inside it. For "never together" subtract the together count from the total.
  • In a restricted-place question, fill the restricted place first (units digit of an even number, first digit of a number with a zero).
  • "At least one" is easiest as total minus none. Use \({}^{n}C_{r}={}^{n}C_{n-r}\) when \(r\) is more than half of \(n\).

A triangle is fixed by any 3 points and order does not matter. No three points of a circle are on a line.


\({}^{10}C_{3}=\frac{10\times9\times8}{3\times2\times1}=\frac{720}{6}=120\)

Answer: 120

Answer: \(r=5\)

Uses \(\frac{{}^{n}C_{r}}{{}^{n}C_{r-1}}=\frac{n-r+1}{r}\).

Ex 6.3 · Q7 (i) — A committee of 5 members is formed out of 6 men and 4 women. In how many ways can it be done if it has exactly 2 women?Intermediate
Question:

A committee of 5 members is formed out of 6 men and 4 women. In how many ways can it be done if it has exactly 2 women?

Exactly 2 women means 2 women and 3 men.


\({}^{4}C_{2}\times{}^{6}C_{3}=6\times20=120\)

Answer: 120

Ex 6.3 · Q2 (v) — Find \(n\): \({}^{n}C_{6}:{}^{n-3}C_{3}=33:4\)Advance
Question:

Find \(n\): \({}^{n}C_{6}:{}^{n-3}C_{3}=33:4\)

\(\frac{{}^{n}C_{6}}{{}^{n-3}C_{3}}=\frac{n!}{6!\,(n-6)!}\times\frac{3!\,(n-6)!}{(n-3)!}=\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{120}=\frac{33}{4}\)
\(n(n-1)(n-2)=990=11\times10\times9\;\Rightarrow\;n=11\)

Answer: \(n=11\)

Ex 6.3 · Q4 (i) — Find \(n\) and \(r\): \({}^{n}C_{r-1}:{}^{n}C_{r}:{}^{n}C_{r+1}=6:14:21\)Advance
Question:

Find \(n\) and \(r\): \({}^{n}C_{r-1}:{}^{n}C_{r}:{}^{n}C_{r+1}=6:14:21\)

\(\frac{{}^{n}C_{r}}{{}^{n}C_{r-1}}=\frac{n-r+1}{r}=\frac{14}{6}=\frac{7}{3}\;\Rightarrow\;3n=10r-3\qquad(1)\)
\(\frac{{}^{n}C_{r+1}}{{}^{n}C_{r}}=\frac{n-r}{r+1}=\frac{21}{14}=\frac{3}{2}\;\Rightarrow\;2n=5r+3\qquad(2)\)
\(\text{from (2) }n=\frac{5r+3}{2};\ \text{in (1): }\frac{3(5r+3)}{2}=10r-3\;\Rightarrow\;15r+9=20r-6\;\Rightarrow\;r=3,\ \ n=\frac{15+3}{2}=9\)

Answer: \(n=9,\ r=3\)
