Unit Overview

What This Unit Is About

Some physical quantities (mass, time, length) are fully described by a number and a unit — these are scalars. Others (displacement, force, acceleration, torque, momentum) need a direction as well — these are vectors. A road signboard is the book's opening example: knowing only the distance (a scalar) is not enough to reach a place; the signboard also gives the direction, so it really gives a displacement (a vector).

This unit builds two big tools on top of the grade-9 idea of vectors:

  1. Vector resolution — breaking one vector into two perpendicular (rectangular) components Ax and Ay, and putting the components back together to get the magnitude and direction.
  2. Products of vectors — multiplying two vectors in two different ways:
    • the scalar (dot) product A · B = AB cosθ → answer is a number (work, power, electric flux);
    • the vector (cross) product A × B = AB sinθ n̂ → answer is a vector (torque, angular momentum, area of a parallelogram).

Everything in the unit is connected by one triangle (the right-angled triangle of a vector and its components) and two functions (cosθ for the dot product, sinθ for the cross product).

Note (scope): Addition of vectors (head-to-tail rule) was studied in grade 9 and is not re-taught in this unit. Section 2.0 gives a two-line recall only (W.H.-added).

Rectangular Components in Full Detail

Section 2.1 of the textbook, explained slowly: why we resolve a vector, where Ax = A cosθ and Ay = A sinθ come from, how to rebuild a vector, and how to avoid the five usual mistakes.

1. Why do we split a vector at all?

A slanting vector is awkward to work with because it points in a direction that is neither horizontal nor vertical. Two perpendicular vectors, however, do not affect each other: what happens along the x-axis is independent of what happens along the y-axis. So we replace one slanting vector by two simple ones, one along each axis, that together do exactly the same job. Think of a man pushing a lawn roller with a handle slanting upward: only the horizontal part of his push moves the roller forward; the vertical part just presses it into the ground. Resolution separates these two effects.

2. Building the components, one step at a time

  1. Draw A from the origin O so that it makes an angle θ with the +x axis. Its head is the point P.
  2. Drop two perpendiculars from P: one to the x-axis (foot Q) and one to the y-axis (foot S).
  3. Read the lengths. OQ is the projection of A on the x-axis, so OQ = Ax. OS is the projection on the y-axis, so OS = Ay. Because OQPS is a rectangle, QP = OS = Ay.
  4. Write the vector sum. A = Ax + Ay as vectors; with unit vectors, A = Ax î + Ay ĵ. The unit vectors only supply direction; the numbers Ax and Ay supply the sizes.
  5. Use the right-angled triangle OPQ (hypotenuse OP = A, base OQ = Ax, height QP = Ay): sinθ = height/hypotenuse = Ay/A, so Ay = A sinθ; cosθ = base/hypotenuse = Ax/A, so Ax = A cosθ.
Memory trick: the component next to the angle (adjacent) uses cos; the component opposite the angle uses sin. At θ = 0° the vector lies along x, so Ax = A cos0° = A and Ay = A sin0° = 0. At θ = 90° it lies along y, so Ax = 0 and Ay = A. If your answer breaks these two checks, you have swapped sin and cos.

3. The angle must be measured from the x-axis

The formulae Ax = A cosθ and Ay = A sinθ are true only when θ is measured from the horizontal (x) axis. If a problem gives the angle from the vertical, the roles of sine and cosine swap, because the angle is now next to the y-component. Never memorise blindly; draw the triangle and label the sides.

5. Rebuilding a vector from its components

Resolution and rebuilding are opposite jobs. Going back, the right-angled triangle gives Pythagoras for the size and the tangent for the direction:

(OP)² = (OQ)² + (QP)²   and QP = OS

tanθ = Ay / Ax   →   θ = tan⁻¹(Ay / Ax)        (Eq 2.8)

6. Which quadrant? (the step that saves marks)

A calculator gives tan⁻¹ only between −90° and +90°. So first look at the signs of Ax and Ay to decide the quadrant, then convert to the angle from the +x axis. If Ax is negative (quadrants II and III), the calculator angle is off by 180°: add 180° to it.

7. A five-step method for every problem

  1. Sketch the vector and mark the angle given.
  2. Decide whether the angle is from the x-axis (use cos for Ax) or from the y-axis (use sin for Ax).
  3. Write Ax and Ay with the correct formulae and put the numbers in.
  4. Fix the signs from the quadrant (a component pointing left or down is negative).
  5. Check that √(Ax² + Ay²) returns the original magnitude.

9. Five common mistakes

  • Swapping sin and cos when the angle is given from the vertical. Draw the triangle.
  • Adding components as plain numbers (12 + 5 = 17). Perpendicular vectors combine by Pythagoras: √(12² + 5²) = 13.
  • Writing θ = tan⁻¹(Ax/Ay). It is Ay over Ax.
  • Ignoring the quadrant and quoting the raw calculator angle.
  • Calculator in RAD mode. Use DEG when angles are in degrees.

The Scalar (Dot) Product in Full Detail

Section 2.2.1 explained from the idea to the exam answer: what the product measures, why cosθ appears, the component form, the angle between vectors, projections, and work, power and flux.

1. The idea in plain words

The dot product answers the question “how much of one vector lies along the other, multiplied by the size of the other?” Pulling a sled with a rope: only the part of the pull along the ground moves the sled, and the work done is that part times the distance. The answer is just a number (a scalar), which is why the product is called the scalar product.

2. Definition and its three equal forms

A · B = AB cosθ            θ = angle between A and B (tails joined)
      = (A cosθ) B          = (component of A along B) × B
      = A (B cosθ)          = A × (component of B along A)        (Eq 2.9–2.10)

The component of A along B is the “shadow” of A on B, which is A cosθ (Fig. C). Read the picture as: shadow of A × length of B. Because AB cosθ is also A (B cosθ), the order of the two vectors does not matter: A · B = B · A.

6. Three jobs the dot product does

  1. Angle between two vectors: find A · B from components, find A and B, then cosθ = (A · B)/(AB) and take cos⁻¹.
  2. Projection (component) of A along B: A cosθ = (A · B)/B.
  3. Perpendicular test: if A · B = 0 and neither vector is null, the vectors are perpendicular.

9. Common mistakes

  • Giving a vector as the answer. The dot product is a scalar; no î, ĵ or k̂ in the result.
  • Using sinθ. A · B always uses cosθ; sinθ belongs to the cross product.
  • Forgetting the sign. An angle above 90° makes the product negative.
  • Taking the angle between head and tail. θ is the angle when the two vectors start from the same point.
  • Using the dot symbol for work in cross form (W = F × d). Work is a scalar, so it is F · d.

The Vector (Cross) Product in Full Detail

Section 2.2.2 explained from the idea to the exam answer: magnitude, the right-hand rule, the unit-vector pattern, the determinant method step by step, and torque, angular momentum and area.

1. The idea in plain words

Where the dot product picks the part of A along B, the cross product picks the part across B (perpendicular to it). That sideways part is what produces turning and area, which is why torque and the area of a parallelogram come from it. The answer is a vector, and it points in a new direction: perpendicular to both A and B.

2. Definition: magnitude and direction

A × B = AB sinθ  n̂                                          (Eq 2.15)
|A × B| = AB sinθ     θ = smaller angle between A and B
n̂ = unit vector perpendicular to the plane of A and B  (right-hand rule)

The magnitude is the size of the “across” part: A (B sinθ). It is largest, AB, at θ = 90° and zero at 0° and 180°.

3. The right-hand rule, step by step

  1. Place the tails of A and B together and find the plane they make.
  2. Point the fingers of your right hand along the first vector A.
  3. Curl the fingers toward the second vector B through the smaller angle (less than 180°).
  4. Keep the thumb erect. The thumb points along A × B.
  5. To get B × A, curl the fingers the other way: the thumb now points opposite. So B × A = −(A × B).
Another way to say the same rule: a right-handed screw turned from A toward B moves forward along A × B. Both forms give the same direction.

6. The determinant method, step by step

For A = Ax î + Ay ĵ + Az k̂ and B = Bx î + By ĵ + Bz k̂:

         | î    ĵ    k̂  |
A × B =  | Ax   Ay   Az |       (Eq 2.16)

  1. î term: cover the î column; take (Ay Bz − Az By).
  2. ĵ term: cover the ĵ column; take (Ax Bz − Az Bx) and put a minus sign in front.
  3. k̂ term: cover the k̂ column; take (Ax By − Ay Bx).

2.1 Vector Resolution

What are components?

Components are the effective parts of a vector in different directions. In 2-D we use two: the x-component (horizontal) and the y-component (vertical). They are perpendicular to each other, so they are called rectangular components.

Definition (book): The components of a vector which are mutually perpendicular are called rectangular components.

Figure 2.1 (described): vector A starts at the origin O and makes angle θ with the +x axis. Drop perpendiculars from its head P to the x-axis (foot Q) and to the y-axis (foot S). Then OQ = Ax (projection on the x-axis) and OS = Ay (projection on the y-axis). OQPS is a rectangle, so QP = OS.

Written as vectors:

A = Ax + Ay            (2.1)
A = Ax î + Ay ĵ        (2.2)

Here î and ĵ are the unit vectors along +x and +y. They only supply direction (magnitude 1); Ax and Ay supply the sizes.

2.1.1 Finding the magnitude of the rectangular components

sinθ = perpendicular / hypotenuse = QP / OP = OS / OP = Ay / A
        →  Ay = A sinθ          (2.3)

cosθ = base / hypotenuse = OQ / OP = Ax / A
        →  Ax = A cosθ          (2.4)

If Ax and Ay are given, the magnitude A and direction θ follow from the same triangle OPQ.

2.2.1 Scalar Product Or Dot Product

Definition (book): When the product of two vector quantities gives a scalar quantity, the product is called the scalar product.

It is written by putting a dot (·) between the two vectors, so it is also called the dot product.

Definition and formula

Figure 2.2 (described): two vectors A and B drawn from a common point with angle θ between them. Then:

A · B = A B cosθ          (2.9)

A and B are the magnitudes. So the scalar product is the product of the magnitudes multiplied by the cosine of the angle between them.

Meaning of A cosθ and B cosθ

Rewrite Eq 2.9 in two ways:

A · B = (A cosθ) B                      (2.10)
A · B = (component of A along B) × B    (2.11)

A · B = A (B cosθ)
A · B = A × (component of B along A)    (2.12)

Figure 2.3 (described; printed one page after it is first cited): drop a perpendicular from the head of A on the line of B. The length cut on B's line is A cosθ — the component of A in the direction of B.

Definition (book): The scalar product is the product of the magnitude of either vector with the component of the other vector in the direction of the first vector.
Component (projection) of A along B  =  A cosθ  =  (A · B) / B
Component (projection) of B along A  =  B cosθ  =  (A · B) / A

2.2.2 Vector Product Or Cross Product

Definition (book): When the product of two vector quantities gives a vector quantity, the product is called the vector product.

It is written by putting a cross (×) between the symbols of the two vectors, so it is also called the cross product.

Definition and formula

Figure 2.4 (described): vectors A and B from a common point with angle θ between them. Their cross product is

A × B = A B sinθ n̂          (2.15)

A and B are the magnitudes. The magnitude of the vector product is the product of the magnitudes multiplied by the sine of the angle between them: |A × B| = AB sinθ.

n̂ is a unit vector giving the direction of the product. It is always perpendicular to the plane containing A and B.

Right-hand rule (direction of n̂) — Fig 2.5

Rule (book): Rotate the fingers of your right hand from the first vector to the second vector through the smaller of the two possible angles, with the thumb erect. The direction of the product is along the erect thumb.

Steps: (1) point the fingers along the first vector; (2) curl them toward the second vector through the smaller angle; (3) the thumb gives the direction of A × B.

Memory aid for î, ĵ, k̂

        î ──► ĵ ──► k̂ ──► î        (cyclic order → plus sign)
   î × ĵ = +k̂      ĵ × k̂ = +î      k̂ × î = +ĵ
   ĵ × î = −k̂      k̂ × ĵ = −î      î × k̂ = −ĵ      (reverse order → minus sign)

Cross product in components

A × B = (Ax î + Ay ĵ + Az k̂) × (Bx î + By ĵ + Bz k̂)

Multiply term by term. The 3 same-direction terms vanish (î×î = 0 etc.). The 6 mixed terms use the table above, e.g. Ax By (î × ĵ) = Ax By k̂, Ay Bx (ĵ × î) = −Ay Bx k̂. Collecting the coefficients of î, ĵ, k̂ gives the determinant:

              | î    ĵ    k̂  |
   A × B  =   | Ax   Ay   Az |                                   (2.16)
              | Bx   By   Bz |

   A × B = (Ay Bz − Az By) î + (Az Bx − Ax Bz) ĵ + (Ax By − Ay Bx) k̂      (2.17)
Tip (W.H.-added): for two vectors lying in the x-y plane (Az = Bz = 0) only the k̂ term survives: A × B = (Ax By − Ay Bx) k̂. Example: A = (3, 2), B = (1, 4) ⇒ A × B = (12 − 2) k̂ = 10 k̂.
Common Mistake: forgetting the minus sign in front of the ĵ term when expanding the determinant along the first row.

Physical significance of the vector product (book)

The vector product gives us the area of a plane figure. If two vectors A and B represent adjacent sides of a plane figure (rectangle or parallelogram) as in Fig 2.7, then

Area of parallelogram = |A × B| = A B sinθ

Why: the base is A and the perpendicular height is B sinθ, so area = base × height = A × B sinθ.

The unit vector n̂ gives the direction of that area, normal to the plane, found by the right-hand rule (Fig 2.5).

Extension (W.H.-added): the triangle formed by A and B has half that area: ½ |A × B|.

Q120. Work and torque both have the unit N m. Why is work a scalar but torque a vector?

Work is the dot product F · d (a number); torque is the cross product r × F (it has a direction along the axis of rotation). Same unit in form, different physical nature. (the joule is reserved for energy and work.)

SLO PHY-15
Q121. Can the dot product be negative? Can the magnitude of the cross product be negative?

The dot product can be negative (θ > 90°, cosθ < 0). The magnitude AB sinθ is never negative for 0° ≤ θ ≤ 180°; reversing the direction (−n̂) is how the cross product shows "opposite".

SLO PHY-15
Q122. Show that the components of A = Ax î + Ay ĵ can be written as Ax = A · î and Ay = A · ĵ.

A · î = (Axî + Ayĵ) · î = Ax(î·î) + Ay(ĵ·î) = Ax(1) + Ay(0) = Ax. Likewise A · ĵ = Ay. This is the same as Ax = A cosθ because A · î = A(1)cosθ — resolution is a dot product with a unit vector.

SLO PHY-16
Q123. For a 2-D vector A with angle θ from the x-axis, show that |A × î| gives the magnitude of the y-component.

|A × î| = A(1) sinθ = A sinθ = |Ay|. So cosθ (dot with î) gives the x-component and sinθ (cross with î) gives the y-component. (W.H.-derived)

SLO PHY-16
Q124. How is the idea of a component (resolution) used in defining the dot product?

A · B = (A cosθ) B: the dot product is the component of A along B multiplied by the magnitude of B (Eq 2.11), so it is resolution followed by an ordinary multiplication.

SLO PHY-16
Q125. Show that the formula A = √(Ax² + Ay²) follows from the dot product.

A · A = A² (property of dot product) and A · A = (Axî + Ayĵ)·(Axî + Ayĵ) = Ax² + Ay² (mixed terms are 0). So A² = Ax² + Ay², i.e. A = √(Ax² + Ay²). (W.H.-derived)

SLO PHY-16
Q126. Using the dot product, show that the magnitude of the position vector r = aî + bĵ is √(a² + b²).

r · r = r² = a² + b² ⇒ r = √(a² + b²).

SLO PHY-16
Q127. Find the angle between A = î and B = î + ĵ using (a) the dot product and (b) the cross product. Do they agree?

(a) A·B = 1, A = 1, B = √2 ⇒ cosθ = 1/√2 ⇒ θ = 45°. (b) A × B = (1)(1)k̂ − 0 = k̂, so |A × B| = 1 = (1)(√2) sinθ ⇒ sinθ = 1/√2 ⇒ θ = 45°. Both agree: 45°.

SLO PHY-16
Q128. Show that tanθ = |A × B| / (A · B). Verify for A = 3, B = 4, θ = 60°.

Divide AB sinθ by AB cosθ: tanθ = |A × B|/(A · B). Check: A · B = 12 × 0.5 = 6; |A × B| = 12 × 0.866 = 10.39; ratio = 1.732 = tan60° ✓. (W.H.-derived)

SLO PHY-16
Q129. Show that (A · B)² + |A × B|² = A²B² and verify it for A = î + 2ĵ + 3k̂, B = 4î + 5ĵ + 6k̂.

(AB cosθ)² + (AB sinθ)² = A²B²(cos²θ + sin²θ) = A²B². Check: A · B = 32 ⇒ 1024; |A × B|² = 54; sum = 1078 = A²B² = 14 × 77 ✓. (W.H.-derived)

SLO PHY-16
Q130. Verify that A × B is perpendicular to A for A = î + 2ĵ + 3k̂ and B = 4î + 5ĵ + 6k̂.

A × B = −3î + 6ĵ − 3k̂. A · (A × B) = −3 + 12 − 9 = 0, so they are perpendicular — consistent with n̂ being normal to the plane of A and B.

SLO PHY-16
Q131. Show with the determinant that î × ĵ = k̂ agrees with the right-hand rule.

For A = î (1, 0, 0) and B = ĵ (0, 1, 0): k̂ coefficient = AxBy − AyBx = 1·1 − 0·0 = 1, others 0 ⇒ î × ĵ = +k̂. The right-hand rule (fingers from x to y, thumb along +z) gives the same.

SLO PHY-16
Q132. In Example 2.2, how does resolution help to understand P = F · v = 24 − 8?

Each axis contributes separately: along x, Fx vx = 8 × 3 = 24 W (force and velocity in the same direction); along y, Fy vy = (−2) × 4 = −8 W (opposite directions). Net P = 16 W.

SLO PHY-16
Q133. A force of 50 N acts at 30° above the road. Name the concept used to (a) find the part of the force that pushes the trolley forward, (b) find the work when the trolley moves 10 m, (c) find the torque about a point.

(a) Resolution: Fx = 50 cos30° = 43.3 N. (b) Dot product: W = F d cos30° = 50 × 10 × 0.866 = 433 J (same as Fx × d). (c) Cross product: τ = r × F.

SLO PHY-16
Q134. In one sentence each, state how resolution, dot product and cross product are connected.

Resolution splits one vector into perpendicular parts with cosθ and sinθ. The dot product multiplies the along part (cosθ) and gives a scalar. The cross product multiplies the across part (sinθ) and gives a vector perpendicular to both.

Vocabulary

Unit 2 Key Terms

TermMeaning
ScalarA physical quantity described completely by a magnitude (a number with a unit), for example mass, time, work.
VectorA physical quantity that has both magnitude and direction, for example force, displacement, torque.
Unit vectorA vector of magnitude one with no unit that shows direction only; Â = A/A, and î, ĵ, k̂ along the axes.
Null vectorA vector of zero magnitude and arbitrary direction.
Position vectorThe vector drawn from the origin to a point P(x, y): r = x î + y ĵ.
Rectangular componentsThe two mutually perpendicular vectors Ax î and Ay ĵ into which a vector can be resolved.
Resolution of a vectorSplitting a vector into two perpendicular components: Ax = A cosθ and Ay = A sinθ.
Magnitude of a vectorA = √(Ax² + Ay²) for a vector with rectangular components Ax and Ay.
Direction of a vectorThe angle θ with the +x axis found from tanθ = Ay/Ax.
Scalar (dot) productA · B = AB cosθ; the result is a scalar.
WorkThe scalar product of force and displacement, W = F · d = F d cosθ (unit: joule).
PowerThe scalar product of force and velocity, P = F · v = F v cosθ (unit: watt).
Electric fluxThe scalar product of the electric field and the area vector, Φ = E · A = E A cosθ.
Projection of A on BThe component of A along B, equal to A cosθ = (A · B)/B.
Vector (cross) productA × B = AB sinθ n̂; the result is a vector perpendicular to both A and B.
Right-hand ruleCurl the fingers of the right hand from the first vector toward the second through the smaller angle; the thumb shows the direction of A × B.
TorqueThe vector product of position vector and force, τ = r × F, with magnitude rF sinθ (unit: N m).
Angular momentumThe vector product of position vector and linear momentum, L = r × p.
Commutative propertyThe order does not matter: A · B = B · A holds for the dot product but not for the cross product.
Anti-commutative propertyReversing the order reverses the sign: A × B = −(B × A).
Parallelogram areaThe area of a parallelogram with adjacent sides A and B is |A × B| = AB sinθ.
Perpendicular vectorsTwo non-null vectors at 90° to each other; their scalar product is zero.
Parallel vectorsTwo non-null vectors at 0° or 180° to each other; their vector product is zero.
