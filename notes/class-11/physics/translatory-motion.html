Unit Overview

What This Unit Is About

Everything that moves can be described by three ideas: where it is (displacement), how fast it changes position (velocity) and how fast the velocity changes (acceleration). The branch of mechanics that studies motion in this way, without asking what causes it, is kinematics. This unit studies translational motion: a body moving from one place to another, first along a straight line and then along the curved path of a projectile.

The unit has three big ideas:

  1. Three equations of motion for constant acceleration, derived from the v–t graph, which solve every straight-line problem and also free fall (just put a = g).
  2. Projectile motion: gravity acts only downward, so the horizontal and vertical motions are independent. Solve them separately and join them with the time.
  3. Conservation of momentum: when no net external force acts, the total momentum stays the same in every collision, while kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic ones.

The link between all three is one repeated skill: choose a direction as positive, write the known quantities, pick the equation that has no unwanted quantity.

Note (scope): g is taken as 9.8 m s⁻² and air resistance is neglected unless the question says otherwise. The coefficient of restitution and impulse are not part of this unit.

Equations of Motion in Full Detail

Sections 3.1 to 3.1.3 explained from the idea to the exam answer: the five quantities, the graph derivation, the three kinds of v–t line, the s–t and a–t graphs and worked examples.

1. The five quantities and which equation to pick

Every straight-line problem with constant acceleration uses five quantities: initial velocity vi, final velocity vf, acceleration a, time t and distance S. Three equations connect them. Each equation leaves one quantity out, and that is how you choose it: write down the three quantities you know, the one you want, and pick the equation that does not contain the quantity you are neither given nor asked for.

EquationMissing quantityUse it when you do not have
vf = vi + at (3.2)Sthe distance
S = vit + ½at² (3.3)vfthe final velocity
2aS = vf² − vi² (3.4)tthe time
Conditions: the equations hold only for a straight line and uniform (constant) acceleration. If the acceleration changes, the v–t graph is not a straight line and these equations cannot be used.

2. Deriving them from the v–t graph (Fig. 3.1)

Take a body whose velocity rises from vi (point A) to vf (point B) in time t (OD = t). Draw AC parallel to the time axis, so CD = vi, BD = vf and BC = vf − vi.

  1. First equation (slope). Acceleration is the slope of AB: a = BC/AC = (vf − vi)/t, so vf = vi + at.
  2. Second equation (area). Distance is the area of trapezium OABD: S = ½(OA + BD) × OD = ½(vi + vf)t. Put vf = vi + at: S = vit + ½at².
  3. Third equation (remove t). From the first, t = (vf − vi)/a. Put it into S = ½(vi + vf)t: S = (vf² − vi²)/2a, i.e. 2aS = vf² − vi².
Without the graph (algebraic route): with uniform acceleration the average velocity is (vi + vf)/2, and by definition distance = average velocity × time. This gives the same second equation directly.

3. Three kinds of motion on one picture

A flat line (a = 0) is uniform motion and the area is a plain rectangle, S = vt. A rising line has positive acceleration. A falling line has negative acceleration (retardation): the body slows down and, if the line crosses the axis, turns back. The area under the line is always the distance (area below the axis counts as motion in the opposite direction).

6. Worked examples

E1Speeding upBASIC

A car moving at 12 m s⁻¹ accelerates uniformly at 2 m s⁻² for 8 s. Find its final velocity and the distance covered.

  • No distance is needed for the velocity, so use the first equation: vf = 12 + 2(8) = 28 m s⁻¹
  • Distance: S = vit + ½at² = 12(8) + ½(2)(8)² = 96 + 64 = 160 m
  • Answer: vf = 28 m s⁻¹, S = 160 m
  • Check: Third equation: (28² − 12²)/(2×2) = (784 − 144)/4 = 160 m ✓
E2Stopping distance (no time given)BASIC

A car moving at 20 m s⁻¹ is brought to rest by brakes giving a retardation of 4 m s⁻². How far does it travel before stopping?

  • Time is neither given nor asked, so use 2aS = vf² − vi² with vf = 0 and a = −4.
  • 2(−4)S = 0 − 20² ⇒ −8S = −400 ⇒ S = 50 m
  • Time taken (first equation): 0 = 20 − 4t ⇒ t = 5 s
  • Answer: S = 50 m (in 5 s)
  • Check: S = average velocity × t = 10 × 5 = 50 m ✓
E3Acceleration from distanceINTERMEDIATE

A train starts from rest and reaches 30 m s⁻¹ after covering 450 m. Find its acceleration and the time taken, assuming uniform acceleration.

  • vi = 0, so 2aS = vf² ⇒ a = 30²/(2×450) = 900/900 = 1 m s⁻²
  • Time: vf = at ⇒ t = 30/1 = 30 s
  • Answer: a = 1 m s⁻², t = 30 s
  • Check: S = ½at² = ½(1)(30)² = 450 m ✓
E4Reading a v–t graphBASIC

A v–t graph is a straight line from 4 m s⁻¹ at t = 0 to 16 m s⁻¹ at t = 6 s. Find the acceleration and the distance.

  • a = slope = (16 − 4)/6 = 2 m s⁻²
  • S = area of the trapezium = ½(4 + 16) × 6 = 60 m
  • Answer: a = 2 m s⁻², S = 60 m
  • Check: S = 4(6) + ½(2)(36) = 24 + 36 = 60 m ✓

7. Five common mistakes

  • Using the equations when a is not constant. A curved v–t line needs other methods.
  • Forgetting the sign of a. Retardation is negative when the positive direction is the direction of motion.
  • Mixing units. Convert km h⁻¹ to m s⁻¹ (divide by 3.6) before substituting.
  • Using the third equation to find time directly. It has no t in it; use the first equation afterwards.
  • Confusing distance with displacement when the body turns back. The formulas give displacement; add the two parts of the area for total distance.

Free Fall in Full Detail

Section 3.1.4 explained from the idea to the exam answer: signs, the up-and-down motion, graphs and worked examples.

1. Free fall is just constant acceleration

Near the Earth’s surface, and when air resistance is ignored, every falling body, heavy or light, gains velocity at the same rate: a = g = 9.8 m s⁻² (the book quotes 9.81), directed towards the centre of the Earth. So free fall needs no new equations: take the three equations of motion and put a = g, writing the distance as h.

vf = vi + gt
h   = vi t + ½ g t²
2gh = vf² − vi²

2. Choose a sign convention and keep it

Pick one direction as positive and stay with it for the whole problem. Taking downward positive for a dropped body gives a = +g. Taking upward positive for a ball thrown up gives a = −g, with vi positive. The physics is identical; only the signs change.

Distances in equal seconds from rest are in the ratio 1 : 3 : 5 : 7 (4.9, 14.7, 24.5, 34.3 m), because the total distance grows as t². The v–t graph is a straight line through the origin with slope g; the h–t graph is a parabola.

3. Up and down: the same speeds at the same heights

  • At the top the velocity is zero for an instant, but the acceleration is still g downward. (Acceleration zero would mean the ball could hang there.)
  • Time to rise = time to fall back to the starting level: tup = vi/g, T = 2vi/g.
  • Maximum height: from the third equation with vf = 0, H = vi²/2g.
  • It returns to the starting point with the same speed vi (now directed downward).

4. Worked examples

FF1Dropped from a heightBASIC

A stone is dropped from the top of a 45 m building. How long does it take to reach the ground and with what speed does it strike? (g = 9.8 m s⁻²)

  • vi = 0, h = 45 m, a = +g. From h = ½gt²: t = √(2h/g) = √(90/9.8) = 3.03 s
  • vf = gt = 9.8 × 3.03 = 29.7 m s⁻¹
  • Answer: t = 3.03 s, v = 29.7 m s⁻¹
  • Check: Third equation: v² = 2gh = 2(9.8)(45) = 882 ⇒ v = 29.7 m s⁻¹ ✓
FF2Thrown straight upINTERMEDIATE

A ball is thrown vertically upward at 24.5 m s⁻¹. Find the maximum height, the time to reach it and the total time of flight.

  • At the top vf = 0, a = −g. Time up: t = vi/g = 24.5/9.8 = 2.5 s
  • Height: H = vi²/2g = (24.5)²/(2×9.8) = 600.25/19.6 = 30.62 m
  • Total time = 2 × 2.5 = 5 s; it comes back with 24.5 m s⁻¹ downward.
  • Answer: H = 30.62 m, t up = 2.5 s, T = 5 s
  • Check: H = ½gt² for the fall: ½(9.8)(2.5)² = 30.6 m ✓
FF3Thrown downwardADVANCE

A stone is thrown vertically downward at 5 m s⁻¹ from a bridge 50 m above the water. Find the time to reach the water and the speed on arrival.

  • Take downward positive: vi = 5, a = +9.8, h = 50.
  • Speed first (no time): vf² = vi² + 2gh = 25 + 980 = 1005 ⇒ vf = 31.7 m s⁻¹
  • Time: t = (vf − vi)/g = (31.7 − 5)/9.8 = 2.72 s
  • Answer: vf = 31.7 m s⁻¹, t = 2.72 s
  • Check: h = 5(2.72) + 4.9(2.72)² = 50.0 m ✓

5. Common mistakes

  • Saying the acceleration is zero at the top. Only the velocity is zero; a = g all the way.
  • Believing heavier bodies fall faster. Without air resistance all bodies have the same a = g.
  • Changing the sign convention half-way. Choose upward or downward positive once and keep it.
  • Forgetting that v is negative on the way down (upward positive). Squaring hides the sign, so state the direction in words.

Projectile Motion in Full Detail

Sections 3.2 to 3.2.2 explained from the idea to the exam answer: the two independent motions, horizontal and angled launches, the height, time and range formulas, complementary angles and air resistance.

1. What a projectile is, and the one big idea

A projectile is a body thrown into the air that then moves only under gravity (air resistance neglected). Its path is a parabola. The one big idea: gravity acts only downward, so the horizontal and vertical motions are independent and can be solved separately.

Horizontal (x)Vertical (y)
Accelerationax = 0ay = −g (upward positive)
Velocityconstantchanges by g every second
Motion typeuniform motionfree fall

2. Launched horizontally (Section 3.2.1)

Here vix = vi and viy = 0. After time t:

vfx = vi                 (never changes)
vfy = g t                 (downward)
vf  = √(vfx² + vfy²)     θ = tan⁻¹(vfy / vfx) below the horizontal
X = vi t     Y = ½ g t²   (3.11, 3.12)

Time to hit the ground from height h: t = √(2h/g), which depends only on h. A ball thrown hard and a ball dropped from the same height land at the same moment; only the horizontal distance differs.

P1Ball thrown from a cliffBASIC

A ball is thrown horizontally at 15 m s⁻¹ from the top of a 20 m cliff. Find the time of flight, the horizontal distance, and the speed and direction on landing.

  • t = √(2h/g) = √(40/9.8) = 2.02 s
  • X = vit = 15 × 2.02 = 30.3 m
  • vfy = gt = 19.8 m s⁻¹; vf = √(15² + 19.8²) = 24.8 m s⁻¹
  • θ = tan⁻¹(19.8/15) = 52.9° below the horizontal
  • Answer: t = 2.02 s, X = 30.3 m, v = 24.8 m s⁻¹ at 52.9° below horizontal

3. Launched at an angle (Section 3.2.2)

Resolve the launch velocity: vix = vi cosθ and viy = vi sinθ. Then:

vfx = vi cosθ                       (constant)
vfy = vi sinθ − g t
x = (vi cosθ) t       y = (vi sinθ) t − ½ g t²   (3.13)

4. Height, time of flight and range — where the formulas come from

  1. Maximum height H. At the top vfy = 0. The third equation for the vertical motion gives 2(−g)H = 0 − (vi sinθ)², so H = vi² sin²θ / 2g (3.15).
  2. Time to the top T′. 0 = vi sinθ − gT′, so T′ = vi sinθ / g (3.18).
  3. Time of flight T. The way down takes as long as the way up (same level), so T = 2T′ = 2vi sinθ / g (3.17).
  4. Range R. Horizontal speed is constant, so R = (vi cosθ)T = vi²(2 sinθ cosθ)/g, and with 2 sinθ cosθ = sin2θ: R = vi² sin2θ / g (3.19).

5. Angle for maximum range, and complementary angles

Since R ∝ sin2θ and sin2θ is largest (= 1) when 2θ = 90°, the range is greatest at θ = 45°: Rmax = vi²/g (3.20). Because sin(180° − 2θ) = sin2θ, the angles θ and (90° − θ) give the same range, though the height and time of flight differ (the steeper path goes higher and stays up longer). At 45°, R = 4H.

6. Air resistance (qualitative)

Air resistance acts like friction and removes energy, so it slows the projectile: both the horizontal and the vertical velocity components decrease. The result is a smaller maximum height and a smaller range, and the trajectory is no longer a symmetric parabola. The formulas of this unit apply only when air resistance is neglected.

9. Common mistakes

  • Using vi instead of vi cosθ for the horizontal speed. Always resolve first.
  • Saying the velocity is zero at the top. Only the vertical component is zero; the horizontal one is still vi cosθ.
  • Using the range formula when the landing level differs from the launch level. R = v² sin2θ/g needs the same level; otherwise use x and y separately.
  • Forgetting that the time of flight depends only on the vertical motion. A horizontal launch from height h takes √(2h/g) whatever the speed.
  • Thinking acceleration is zero at the top. a = g downward throughout.

Momentum and Collisions in Full Detail

Sections 3.3 to 3.5 explained from the idea to the exam answer: momentum, conservation, elastic and inelastic collisions, explosions, 2-D collisions and applications.

1. Momentum and Newton’s second law

Linear momentum is mass times velocity: p = mv. It is a vector (direction of v) with unit kg m s⁻¹ (= N s). A heavier or faster body has more momentum. Newton’s second law in its general form says the net force is the rate of change of momentum: F = Δp/Δt. For constant mass this reduces to F = ma.

If no net external force acts, Δp = 0, so the total momentum stays the same. That is the law of conservation of momentum: for an isolated system, m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁′ + m₂v₂′ (the primes mean after).

2. Collisions: what is and is not conserved

TypeMomentumKinetic energyBodies after collision
Elasticconservedconservedseparate
InelasticconservedNOT conserved (some becomes heat, sound, deformation)usually separate
Perfectly inelasticconservedmaximum lossstick together
Key point: momentum is conserved in every collision of an isolated system. Only kinetic energy distinguishes elastic from inelastic.

3. One-dimensional elastic collision

Writing both conservation laws and solving for the final velocities gives (for m₁ moving at v₁ and m₂ at v₂):

v₁′ = [(m₁ − m₂)/(m₁ + m₂)] v₁ + [2m₂/(m₁ + m₂)] v₂
v₂′ = [2m₁/(m₁ + m₂)] v₁ + [(m₂ − m₁)/(m₁ + m₂)] v₂

Useful special cases: equal masses simply exchange velocities; a light body hitting a very heavy body at rest bounces back with almost the same speed while the heavy body hardly moves; a heavy body hitting a light one at rest carries on, and the light body shoots off at nearly twice the heavy body’s speed. In an elastic collision the relative speed of approach equals the relative speed of separation.

4. Perfectly inelastic collision

The bodies stick, so they move with one common velocity. From conservation of momentum: v = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂)/(m₁ + m₂). The kinetic energy lost is ΔK = ½ [m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂)] (v₁ − v₂)², which is always positive.

5. Explosions and recoil

A body at rest that splits apart (a gun firing, a rocket, a nucleus decaying) has zero total momentum before, so it must have zero after: m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = 0, i.e. the pieces move in opposite directions and the lighter piece moves faster. Here the kinetic energy increases (stored chemical or elastic energy is released) while momentum is still conserved.

6. Two-dimensional collisions

Momentum is a vector, so conserve it separately along x and along y. For a target at rest hit by a body moving along x: m₁v₁ = m₁v₁′ cosθ₁ + m₂v₂′ cosθ₂ and 0 = m₁v₁′ sinθ₁ − m₂v₂′ sinθ₂. For an elastic 2-D collision add the kinetic-energy equation as well.

  • y: 0 = 1(3)sin53° + 1(vy) ⇒ vy = −3(0.8) = −2.4 m s⁻¹
  • v = √(3.2² + 2.4²) = √16 = 4 m s⁻¹ at θ = tan⁻¹(2.4/3.2) = 36.9° below the x-axis
  • Answer: Second ball: 4 m s⁻¹ at 36.9° below the x-axis
  • Check: K before = 12.5 J; K after = ½(9) + ½(16) = 12.5 J ✓ (so the collision was elastic; the angle between the two paths is 90°)

10. Common mistakes

  • Saying kinetic energy is conserved in every collision. Only in elastic collisions; momentum is always conserved.
  • Forgetting the direction of velocity. Choose a positive direction; a body moving the other way has negative velocity.
  • Adding momenta as magnitudes in 2-D. Resolve into x and y components first.
  • Using the elastic formulas for a sticking collision. If the bodies stick, use the common-velocity formula.
  • Mixing units (g and kg). Convert to kg and m s⁻¹ first.

Dynamics is the branch of mechanics that deals with the study of the forces that cause objects and systems to move. It includes momentum and the associated law of conservation.

Q10. Define displacement.
Also asked as: Differentiate between distance and displacement.

Also asked as: Differentiate between speed and velocity.

Q13. When is a motion called uniformly accelerated motion?
Also asked as: What does "constant acceleration" mean?

When the acceleration stays constant, the velocity changes by equal amounts in equal intervals of time. This is the condition (assumption) under which the three equations of motion are valid.

Q14. Define momentum and write its SI unit.

Momentum is the product of mass and velocity, p = mv. It is a vector in the direction of the velocity. Its SI unit is kg m s⁻¹.

3.1 Equations of Uniformly Accelerated Motion

Q16. How many equations of motion are there? What do they show?
Also asked as: What do the equations of motion describe and predict?

There are three equations of motion. They show the relation between initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration, displacement and time, and they describe and predict the motion of objects under constant acceleration.

SLO PHY-01
Q17. State the assumptions made in deriving the equations of motion.
Also asked as: Under what conditions are the three equations of motion valid?

(1) The object moves along a straight line. (2) The acceleration is constant. (3) Only magnitudes of displacement, velocity and acceleration are considered. (4) The direction of the initial velocity, and all quantities in that direction, are taken as positive.

SLO PHY-01
Q18. What are the conditions for using the equations of motion?
Also asked as: Can the equations of motion be used when the acceleration changes with time?

No. They are valid only for straight-line motion with constant acceleration. If the acceleration changes, the equations cannot be applied to the whole motion.

SLO PHY-02
Q19. Why are the equations of motion derived using only the magnitudes of vectors?
Also asked as: What does it mean that the direction of initial velocity is taken as positive?

In a straight line there are only two possible directions, so direction can be handled with a plus or minus sign. The direction of vi is called positive; a quantity in the opposite direction (for example the acceleration of a slowing body) gets a negative sign.

SLO PHY-01
Q20. Write the three equations of motion and name every symbol.
Also asked as: State the equations of motion for uniform acceleration.

vf = vi + at; S = vi t + ½at²; 2aS = vf² − vi². Here vi = initial velocity, vf = final velocity, a = acceleration, t = time, S = displacement.

SLO PHY-01
Q21. What relation does the first equation of motion represent?

The first equation, vf = vi + at, represents the relation between initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration and time.

SLO PHY-01
Q22. What relation does the second equation of motion represent?

The second equation, S = vi t + ½at², represents the relation between initial velocity, acceleration, distance and time.

SLO PHY-01
Q23. What relation does the third equation of motion represent?

The third equation, 2aS = vf² − vi², represents the relation between initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration and distance.

SLO PHY-01
Q24. Which equation of motion should be used if the time is not given?
Also asked as: How do you choose the correct equation of motion for a numerical?

Choose the equation that contains the three known quantities and the one wanted, and does not contain the missing quantity: S not involved → vf = vi + at; vf not involved → S = vi t + ½at²; t not involved → 2aS = vf² − vi².

Also asked as: How is acceleration found from a velocity-time graph?

Q27. What does the area under a velocity-time graph give?
Also asked as: How is the distance travelled found from a velocity-time graph?

The total distance S travelled by the body is equal to the total area under the velocity-time graph (here, the area of the trapezium OABD).

SLO PHY-01
Q28. Why is the velocity-time graph of a body with constant acceleration a straight line?

Constant acceleration means the velocity changes by equal amounts in equal times. The slope (= acceleration) is therefore the same at every point, which is possible only for a straight line.

SLO PHY-01
Q29. In Fig. 3.1, what do BD, CD and OD represent?
Also asked as: Which lines of the graph represent the final velocity, the initial velocity and the time?

BD represents the final velocity vf, CD represents the initial velocity vi (CD = OA), and OD represents the time t.

SLO PHY-01
Q30. What do the areas of the rectangle OACD and the triangle ABC in Fig. 3.1 represent?
Also asked as: Show that the total area under the graph equals vi t + ½at².

Rectangle OACD = vi × t = vi t: the distance the body would cover at its initial velocity. Triangle ABC = ½ × base × height = ½(t)(at) = ½at²: the extra distance covered because of the gain in speed. Their sum gives the total distance S = vi t + ½at².

SLO PHY-01
Q31. What does the bracket "Gain in speed, vf − vi = at" in Fig. 3.1 mean?

BC is the increase in velocity during time t. Since the acceleration is a, the gain in speed in time t is at, so vf − vi = at.

SLO PHY-01
Q32. Derive the first equation of motion graphically.
Also asked as: Using the velocity-time graph, prove that vf = vi + at.
  • Consider a body moving in a straight line with initial velocity vi and constant acceleration a. Its velocity becomes vf after time t (Fig. 3.1).
  • Acceleration = slope of line AB, so a = BC/AC, i.e. a = (BD − CD)/OD ______ (3.1)
  • From the graph BD = vf, CD = vi and OD = t. Hence a = (vf − vi)/t.
  • So at = vf − vi, and therefore vf = vi + at ______ (3.2)
  • This is the first equation of motion: it relates the initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration and time.

3.1.2 Second Equation of Motion

Q33. Why is the distance travelled equal to the area of the trapezium OABD?
Also asked as: Which figure on the velocity-time graph gives the total distance, and why?

The distance travelled is the area under the velocity-time graph. Under line AB, between the velocity axis and the vertical line BD, this area is the trapezium OABD.

SLO PHY-01
Q34. Write the formula for the area of a trapezium and identify its parallel sides in Fig. 3.1.

Area = ½ (sum of parallel sides) × (distance between the parallel sides). In Fig. 3.1 the parallel sides are OA and BD (both vertical), and OD is the distance between them.

SLO PHY-01
Q35. Derive the second equation of motion S = vi t + ½at².
Also asked as: Using the velocity-time graph, prove the relation between distance, initial velocity, acceleration and time.
  • Total distance S = area of trapezium OABD = ½ (OA + BD) × OD.
  • Putting OA = vi, BD = vf and OD = t: S = ½ (vi + vf) × t.
  • Using the first equation vf = vi + at: S = ½ (vi + vi + at) × t = ½ (2vi + at) × t = ½ (2vi t + at²).
  • Finally S = vi t + ½ at² ______ (3.3)
  • This is the second equation of motion; it relates initial velocity, acceleration, distance and time.
SLO PHY-01
Q36. What is the average velocity of a body moving with constant acceleration?
Also asked as: Show that S = ½(vi + vf)t means distance = average velocity × time.

From S = ½(vi + vf) × t we can write S = [(vi + vf)/2] × t. The quantity (vi + vf)/2 is the average velocity. This simple average is valid only when the acceleration is constant, because then the velocity changes uniformly (the graph is a straight line).

SLO PHY-01

3.1.3 Third Equation of Motion

Q37. Derive the third equation of motion 2aS = vf² − vi² graphically.
Also asked as: Using the velocity-time graph, prove the relation between initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration and distance.
  • Total distance S = area of trapezium OABD = ½ (OA + BD) × OD, or 2S = (OA + BD) × OD.
  • Multiply both sides by BC/OD: 2S × BC/OD = (OA + BD) × OD × BC/OD.
  • As BC/OD = a (slope of the graph): 2S × a = (OA + BD) × BC.
  • Putting OA = vi, BD = vf and BC = vf − vi: 2aS = (vi + vf) × (vf − vi).
  • So 2aS = vf² − vi² ______ (3.4)
  • This is the third equation of motion; it relates initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration and distance.
SLO PHY-01
Q38. Why is the equation 2S = (OA + BD) × OD multiplied by BC/OD in the derivation of the third equation?

BC/OD is the slope of the graph, which equals the acceleration a. Multiplying by it brings the acceleration into the equation and turns BC into (vf − vi), so the product (vf + vi)(vf − vi) = vf² − vi² appears and time t is eliminated.

SLO PHY-01
Q39. Derive the third equation of motion by eliminating time from the first two equations.

From vf = vi + at: t = (vf − vi)/a. Put this in S = ½(vi + vf) t: S = ½(vi + vf)(vf − vi)/a, so 2aS = (vf + vi)(vf − vi) = vf² − vi².

SLO PHY-01
Q40. Derive the three equations of motion from the definitions of velocity and acceleration (algebraic method).
Also asked as: The SLO asks for a derivation "from the definitions of velocity and acceleration". Show it without using the graph.
  • First equation: by definition a = (vf − vi)/t, so at = vf − vi, i.e. vf = vi + at.
  • Second equation: with uniform acceleration the average velocity is vav = (vi + vf)/2. By definition of velocity, S = vav × t = (vi + vf)t/2. Put vf = vi + at: S = vi t + ½at².
  • Third equation: from the first, t = (vf − vi)/a. Put it in S = (vi + vf)t/2 to get 2aS = vf² − vi².
SLO PHY-01
Q41. Why does the third equation not contain time? When is it most useful?

Time was eliminated during its derivation. It is the best choice when the time is neither given nor asked, for example stopping distance from a known speed and retardation.

3.1.4 Free-Fall Motion

Q53. What is free-fall motion?
Also asked as: Give an example of uniformly accelerated motion.

Free fall is the motion of a body falling freely near the Earth's surface in the absence of air resistance. It is an example of uniformly accelerated motion, because its acceleration g is constant.

SLO PHY-03
Q54. Define acceleration due to gravity. What is its value and direction?
Also asked as: What is "g"? Write its value and state in which direction it acts.

The constant acceleration with which all freely falling bodies move near the Earth's surface is called acceleration due to gravity, denoted by g. Its value is 9.81 m s⁻² (taken as 9.8 m s⁻² in the book's examples) and it is directed towards the centre of the Earth.

SLO PHY-03
Q55. In the absence of air resistance, do heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones?
Also asked as: Why do a lighter and a heavier object reach the ground together when dropped from the same height without air?

No. In the absence of air resistance all objects, lighter or heavier, fall with the same acceleration g, independent of their masses, so they take the same time to reach the ground.

SLO PHY-03
Q56. How are the equations of motion for free fall obtained from the three general equations?
Also asked as: What substitutions are made to write the equations for a freely falling body?

Put a = g (acceleration) and S = h (vertical distance, height) in the three equations of motion.

SLO PHY-03
Q58. What sign is given to g in free-fall problems?
Also asked as: When is g taken positive and when negative?

g is positive when the body is falling downward (motion along g) and negative when the body is moving upward (motion against g). In Example 3.3, g = −9.8 m s⁻² is used for the upward motion of the kangaroo.

Q82. What are the initial horizontal and vertical components of velocity for a projectile launched horizontally?

Also asked as: Write ax and ay for a horizontal projectile.

ax = 0 (no horizontal acceleration) and ay = g (the acceleration due to gravity).

SLO PHY-06
Q84. When is g taken positive and when negative in projectile motion?

g is positive when the ball is falling downward and negative when it is moving upward.

SLO PHY-06
Q85. How does the horizontal component of velocity of a horizontally thrown projectile change with time?
Also asked as: Why does the horizontal component of velocity remain constant?

It remains constant: vfx = vix = vi at every instant, because there is no horizontal acceleration (ax = 0) once the projectile has left the thrower.

SLO PHY-05
Q86. How does the vertical component of velocity of a horizontally thrown projectile change with time?
Also asked as: Write the expression for the vertical component of velocity at time t.

It increases uniformly with time: vfy = viy + ay t = 0 + gt, so vfy = gt.

SLO PHY-06

Also asked as: How is the speed of a projectile at time t found from its two components?

SLO PHY-08

Also asked as: Write the formula for the angle θ made by the velocity with the horizontal.

θ = tan⁻¹(vfy / vfx) (Eq. 3.10): the angle whose tangent is vertical component divided by horizontal component.

SLO PHY-08
Q89. Write the expressions for the horizontal and the vertical displacements of a horizontally launched projectile at time t.
Also asked as: How far has the projectile moved horizontally and how far has it fallen after time t?

Horizontal displacement X = vfx t = vi t (Eq. 3.11). Vertical displacement (downward) Y = ½gt² (Eq. 3.12).

SLO PHY-08

SLO PHY-08
Q91. Show that the path (trajectory) of a horizontally launched projectile is a parabola.

From X = vi t, t = X/vi. Put it in Y = ½gt²: Y = (g / 2vi²) X². This has the form Y = kX² (k constant), which is the equation of a parabola; so the projectile follows a parabolic path.

  • y = (vi sinθ)t − ½gt² = 20 × 0.5 × 1 − ½(9.8)(1) = 10 − 4.9 = 5.1 m

From x = (vi cosθ)t, t = x/(vi cosθ). Substitute in Eq. 3.13: y = x tanθ − [g / (2vi² cos²θ)] x². This is of the form y = ax − bx² (a, b constants), the equation of a parabola.

SLO PHY-08
Q105. What is the velocity of a projectile at the highest point of its path?

At the top the vertical component is zero (vfy = 0) but the horizontal component vi cosθ is still there, so the velocity is horizontal with magnitude vi cosθ and the speed is the smallest of the whole flight. The velocity is zero at the top only for a vertical throw (θ = 90°).

Height of the projectile H

Q106. Define the height (maximum height) of a projectile.
Also asked as: What is meant by H in projectile motion? What is it denoted by?

The height of the projectile is the maximum vertical distance attained during the projectile motion. It is denoted by H (Fig. 3.5).

SLO PHY-08
Q107. Why is the vertical component of velocity zero at the maximum height?
Also asked as: Which condition is used to find the height of a projectile?

At the maximum height the body momentarily comes to rest in the vertical direction, so vfy = 0. This condition is put in the third equation of motion to find H.

SLO PHY-08
Q108. Derive the expression for the maximum height of a projectile.
Also asked as: Prove that H = vi² sin²θ / 2g.

  • At maximum height vfy = 0; for upward motion ay = −g. So 2(−g)H = 0 − viy².
  • Since viy = vi sinθ: −2gH = 0 − vi² sin²θ.
  • By arranging: H = vi² sin²θ / 2g ______ (3.15)
  • If the magnitude and direction of the initial velocity are known, this equation gives the height.
SLO PHY-08
Q109. On what factors does the maximum height of a projectile depend?

H = vi² sin²θ/2g depends on the initial speed (H ∝ vi²: doubling vi makes H four times), the launch angle (H ∝ sin²θ, largest for θ = 90°, where H = vi²/2g) and g. It does not depend on the mass of the projectile.

Also asked as: What is the time taken by a projectile from the point of projection to the point where it hits the ground at the same level called?

The time taken by the projectile from the point of projection to the point where it hits the ground at the same level is called the time of flight. It is denoted by T.

Effect of Air Resistance on Projectile Motion

Q129. How does air resistance affect projectile motion?
Also asked as: Predict qualitatively the effect of air resistance on a projectile.

Air resistance acts like friction and decreases the energy and velocity of the projectile. Both the horizontal and vertical components of velocity decrease, so the range and the maximum height are reduced and the trajectory is changed.

SLO PHY-10
Q130. How is the horizontal component of velocity affected by air resistance?
Also asked as: Is the horizontal velocity of a projectile still constant when air resistance acts?

No. In the ideal case vx is constant, but with air resistance a retarding force acts opposite to the motion, so the horizontal component of velocity decreases continuously.

SLO PHY-10
Q131. How is the vertical component of velocity affected by air resistance?

The air resistance also reduces the vertical component of the velocity, so the projectile rises to a smaller height than in the ideal case.

SLO PHY-10
Q132. Why does air resistance reduce the range of a projectile?
Also asked as: Predict qualitatively how air resistance changes the range of a projectile.

The horizontal component of velocity keeps decreasing during the flight, so less horizontal distance is covered in the same time; the reduced vertical component also lowers the height reached. Hence the range is reduced.

SLO PHY-10
Q133. Why does air resistance reduce the energy of the projectile?

Air resistance is a friction-like force. Work done against it takes kinetic energy away from the projectile (it appears as heat in the air and the projectile), so the particle's energy decreases.

SLO PHY-10
Q134. Describe Fig. 3.6(b).
Also asked as: Compare the trajectories with and without air resistance in Fig. 3.6(b).

Both arches start from the same point. Without air resistance the path is a symmetric parabola with large height and range. With air resistance the arch is lower, shorter and steeper on the way down, with a much smaller range.

SLO PHY-10
Q135. Is the path of a projectile still a parabola when air resistance is present?

No. The parabolic path is the result of a constant horizontal velocity and a constant vertical acceleration g. With air resistance neither is constant, so the path is no longer a perfect parabola (it is lower, asymmetric and falls more steeply).

SLO PHY-07

3.3 Law of Conservation of Momentum

Q145. What is an isolated system?
Also asked as: When is a system said to be isolated?

A system is said to be isolated if and only if the net external force acting on it (such as the gravitational force or friction) is zero.

SLO PHY-11
Q146. Is there any ideally isolated system in the universe?
Also asked as: How is an isolated system treated in physics problems?

No ideally isolated system exists in the universe. We consider an isolated system as one that does not interact with its environment, which is a very good approximation when the external forces are negligible.

SLO PHY-11
Q147. How is the total momentum of a system of many particles found?
Also asked as: If the particles of a system move with different velocities, what is the total momentum?

The total momentum of the system is the sum of the momenta of the individual particles (vector sum, since momentum is a vector): p = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ + …

SLO PHY-11
Q148. State the law of conservation of momentum.
Also asked as: What is conserved in an isolated system of interacting particles?

The total momentum of an isolated system of interacting particles is conserved.

SLO PHY-11
Q149. Express the law of conservation of momentum mathematically (Eq. 3.21).
Also asked as: Write the relation between the initial and the final momenta of an isolated system.

pf = pi, or pf − pi = 0, or Δp = 0 (Eq. 3.21). Here pi and pf are the initial and final total momenta of the isolated system.

SLO PHY-11
Q150. Why is the law of conservation of momentum useful in collision problems?
Also asked as: What happens to the momentum of each particle and to the total momentum during a collision?

During a collision the internal forces change the momentum of each particle, but these forces do not contribute to the net external force, which remains zero. Hence the total momentum of the system does not change with time, and this lets us find unknown velocities after the collision.

SLO PHY-11
Q151. Is the law of conservation of momentum applicable to explosions?

Yes. The law is also applicable in explosions, where the internal forces break one body into fragments.

SLO PHY-11
Q152. Why is the total momentum of two colliding bodies conserved even though each body's momentum changes?

The two bodies exert equal and opposite forces on each other for the same time (Newton's third law). So the momentum gained by one equals the momentum lost by the other: Δp₁ = −Δp₂, and Δp₁ + Δp₂ = 0.

SLO PHY-11
Q153. What is the condition for conservation of momentum?

The net external force on the system must be zero (isolated system). If an external force acts, the momentum of the system changes.

An elastic collision is a collision in which both the momentum and the kinetic energy of the system are conserved.

Also asked as: What happens to the shapes and the kinetic energy of two objects in an elastic collision?

Collisions between atomic and subatomic particles are elastic. The two objects collide and return to their original shapes with no loss of total kinetic energy; the kinetic energy does not change into other types of energy.

Q181. Define an inelastic collision.
Also asked as: In which collision is momentum conserved but kinetic energy not conserved?

An inelastic collision is a collision in which the momentum of the system is conserved but the kinetic energy is not conserved.

SLO PHY-14
Q182. Give an example of an inelastic collision.
Also asked as: Into which forms of energy is kinetic energy transformed in an inelastic collision?

Example: a meteorite falling on the Earth. During inelastic collisions the kinetic energy is transformed into other forms of energy such as heat energy, sound energy and the energy of material deformation.

SLO PHY-14
Q183. Why is the kinetic energy of the system not conserved in an inelastic collision although the momentum is?

Part of the kinetic energy is converted into heat, sound and deformation of the bodies. Momentum is a vector conserved by the internal action–reaction forces, but kinetic energy is energy of motion and can be converted into other forms (see the section on why KE changes below).

Collisions in Two Dimensions and Why Kinetic Energy Changes

Q210. How is momentum conservation applied to a collision in two dimensions?
Also asked as: What is done with the momentum equation when the bodies do not move along a single straight line?

Momentum is a vector, so it is conserved separately along two perpendicular axes: Σpx(before) = Σpx(after) and Σpy(before) = Σpy(after). Each velocity is resolved into x and y components, and the two equations are solved for the unknowns. For an elastic collision the kinetic energy (a scalar, ½mv² with v² = vx² + vy²) is conserved too.

SLO PHY-11
Q211. A 1 kg body moving at 4 m s⁻¹ along +x collides with a 1 kg body moving at 3 m s⁻¹ along +y and they stick together. Find the speed and direction of the combined body.
  • x-direction: 1(4) + 1(0) = (1 + 1)vx, so vx = 2 m s⁻¹
  • y-direction: 1(0) + 1(3) = (1 + 1)vy, so vy = 1.5 m s⁻¹
  • Speed v = √(2² + 1.5²) = 2.5 m s⁻¹; direction θ = tan⁻¹(1.5/2) = 36.9° above the x-axis
  • KE before = ½(1)(16) + ½(1)(9) = 12.5 J; after = ½(2)(2.5)² = 6.25 J: half of the KE is lost, so the collision is inelastic.
SLO PHY-11
Q212. Justify why, though the momentum of a closed system is always conserved, some change in kinetic energy may take place. (Exercise Comprehensive Question 3.6)
Also asked as: How can momentum stay the same while kinetic energy changes?

Momentum is a vector and is conserved because the internal forces between the bodies are equal and opposite, so they cancel in the total. Kinetic energy is a scalar and the internal forces can do work that changes it: in an inelastic collision part of the KE is changed into heat, sound and deformation (KE decreases); in an explosion the chemical (stored) energy is changed into KE (KE increases); only in an elastic collision does the KE stay the same.

SLO PHY-14
Q213. In which three situations is kinetic energy gained, lost or unchanged while momentum is conserved?

Explosion: KE increases (chemical energy → KE; the initial KE is zero). Inelastic collision: KE decreases (→ heat, sound, deformation). Elastic collision: KE is unchanged.

SLO PHY-14
Q214. Why does the kinetic energy of the fragments of a bomb increase although the total momentum stays zero?

The total momentum is a vector sum, so momenta in opposite directions cancel and the total remains zero. Kinetic energy is a scalar, always positive, so the fragments' kinetic energies add up. The extra kinetic energy comes from the chemical energy stored in the bonds of the explosive.

Q215. Two equal-mass carts roll towards each other at 2 m s⁻¹ and 1 m s⁻¹ and couple together. Show that momentum is conserved but kinetic energy decreases.

Let each cart have mass m, and take the first cart's direction as positive. Before: p = m(2) + m(−1) = m; after: (2m)v = m gives v = 0.5 m s⁻¹, so momentum is conserved. KE before = ½m(2)² + ½m(1)² = 2.5m; KE after = ½(2m)(0.5)² = 0.25m. So 2.25m of KE (90 %) is converted into heat, sound and deformation.

SLO PHY-14
Q216. In a perfectly inelastic collision, why is the kinetic energy loss greatest?

After the collision the bodies move together with one common velocity; no relative motion is left, so the maximum possible kinetic energy relative to the centre of mass has been converted into other forms. Momentum is still conserved.

Q221. How would H, T and R of a projectile change on the Moon, where g is one-sixth of the Earth's value?

H = vi² sin²θ/2g, T = 2vi sinθ/g and R = vi² sin2θ/g are all inversely proportional to g. With g/6, each of H, T and R becomes 6 times larger for the same vi and θ (neglecting air).

SLO PHY-08
Q222. Is the momentum of a projectile conserved during its flight?
Also asked as: Which component of the momentum of a projectile is constant?

The momentum of the projectile alone is not conserved, because gravity is an external force acting on it: the vertical momentum changes. But there is no horizontal force, so the horizontal component of momentum (m vi cosθ) stays constant. This is the same fact as "the horizontal component of velocity is constant".

  • Range: X = vi t = 60 × 0.505 = 30.3 m

SLO PHY-11
Q224. Why do car makers use crumple zones, and how is this related to the karate chop?

Both use F = Δp/Δt. In a karate chop the momentum is stopped in a very short time, giving a large force on the tiles. In a crash the momentum of the passenger must be reduced to zero; a crumple zone increases the stopping time, so the same Δp produces a much smaller force on the passenger.

Q225. Why does the relative-speed result (Eq. 3.25) follow from both conservation laws together?

Eq. 3.23 comes from momentum conservation and Eq. 3.24 from kinetic energy conservation. Dividing one by the other eliminates the masses and leaves u₁ − u₂ = −(v₁ − v₂). So the relative-speed rule holds only if both laws hold, i.e. only for elastic collisions.

Q231. Short Question 3.3 — For a projectile motion, is the velocity ever zero? Is the acceleration ever zero?

Velocity: For a projectile launched at an angle (or horizontally) the velocity is never zero, because the horizontal component vi cosθ stays constant; at the top only its vertical component is zero. The velocity is zero (momentarily) only at the top of a vertical throw (θ = 90°). Acceleration: never zero; it is always g downward (ax = 0 but ay = ±g) while the projectile is in the air.

SLO PHY-05
Q232. Short Question 3.5 — An aeroplane while flying horizontally drops a bomb when it reaches exactly above the target, but misses it. Explain why.

The bomb has the same horizontal velocity as the plane at the instant of release. After release there is no horizontal force (air neglected), so it keeps moving forward with that horizontal speed while falling. In the time of fall t = √(2h/g) it travels X = v t forward and lands ahead of the target. To hit it, the bomb must be released earlier, before the plane is above the target.

Q233. Comprehensive Question 3.5 — Derive mathematical equations for (a) maximum height attained, (b) time of flight, (c) range of a projectile.

A projectile is launched with speed vi at angle θ: viy = vi sinθ, vix = vi cosθ, ay = −g.

  • (a) Maximum height: 2ayH = vfy² − viy², with vfy = 0 at the top: 2(−g)H = 0 − vi² sin²θ, so H = vi² sin²θ / 2g (3.15).
  • (b) Time of flight: y = viy t + ½ay t² with y = 0 at t = T: 0 = (vi sinθ)T − ½gT², so T = 2vi sinθ / g (3.17). (Time of summit T′ = T/2 = vi sinθ/g.)
  • (c) Range: R = vx T = vi cosθ × 2vi sinθ/g = vi² (2 sinθ cosθ)/g, so R = vi² sin2θ / g (3.19). Maximum range at θ = 45°: Rmax = vi²/g (3.20).
