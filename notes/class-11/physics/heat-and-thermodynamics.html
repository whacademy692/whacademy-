Unit Overview

What This Unit Is About

Thermodynamics is the branch of physics in which we study how heat is transformed into other forms of energy. This unit starts with temperature and thermal equilibrium, moves to internal energy, the ideal gas and kinetic theory, then builds the first law of thermodynamics with its four processes (isothermal, adiabatic, isochoric, isobaric). It ends with heat engines, the second law, Carnot's engine, the refrigerator and entropy.

The cover question of the unit: how does thermodynamics play its role in the human body, in terms of work and energy? When a boy rides a bicycle, heat (Q) is transferred from the body and work (W) is done, so the internal energy (U) falls; the food we eat supplies the energy to make up for it.

One idea ties the whole chapter together: energy is conserved (first law), but it cannot be converted from heat to work freely, and every natural process degrades energy and raises disorder (second law and entropy).

8.0 Introduction — Temperature And Heat

The idea of temperature starts from "hot" and "cold" as we feel them by touch, but physics needs measurable properties. Many measurable properties depend on temperature: the length of a metal rod, steam pressure in a boiler, the ability of a wire to conduct current, and the colour of a very hot glowing object.

Q1. Define temperature.
Also asked as: What does temperature describe? State its unit.

Temperature describes the degree of hotness and coldness of an object. It is the measure of the average kinetic energy of the molecules. Its unit is the kelvin (K).

SLO PHY-02
Q2. Define heat.
Also asked as: What kind of energy is heat? State its unit.

Heat is the form of energy associated with the kinetic energies of all the molecules in a substance. Its unit is the joule (J).

SLO PHY-02
Q3. Differentiate between heat and temperature.
Also asked as: Why can two bodies at the same temperature contain different amounts of heat energy?

Heat is the energy of all the molecules (unit joule); temperature measures only the average kinetic energy (unit kelvin). So a bigger body has more molecules and more total energy even at the same temperature.

SLO PHY-02
Q4. Name four measurable properties of matter that depend on temperature.
Also asked as: Why do physicists not depend only on the sense of touch to judge temperature?

(1) Length of a metal rod, (2) steam pressure in a boiler, (3) ability of a wire to conduct electric current, (4) colour of a very hot glowing object. Touch is only qualitative; measurable properties give a number.

SLO PHY-01
Q5. How is temperature related to the kinetic energy of the molecules of a material?
Also asked as: What does a higher temperature tell us about the molecules of a body?

Temperature is related to the kinetic energies of the molecules: a higher temperature means a higher average kinetic energy of the molecules.

8.1 Thermal Equilibrium

For Your Information (book box) — System and surroundings. System: the matter or space region being studied (for example a gas in a cylinder). Surroundings: everything outside the system. Boundary: the separation between system and surroundings; all energy changes caused by work or heat exchange take place through it.
Do You Know? (book box). Diathermic substances allow heat to pass through them, and the process is called a diathermic process.
Figure 8.1(a) in words: two thermometers show a hot region at 100 °C and a cold region at 0 °C with heat transferred from the hot region to the cold region; after some time both regions read 50 °C, reach thermal equilibrium and no more energy is transferred.
Q6. Define thermal equilibrium.
Also asked as: When are two systems said to be in thermal equilibrium with each other?
Also asked as: State the condition for thermal equilibrium.

Two systems are in thermal equilibrium with each other if they are at the same temperature.

SLO PHY-01
Q7. Which property decides whether two systems will be in thermal equilibrium or not?
Also asked as: What is the main property that determines thermal equilibrium?

Temperature.

SLO PHY-01
Q8. What happens when two bodies at different temperatures are connected by a diathermic substance?
Also asked as: Describe the direction of heat flow between a hot and a cold body in contact.

Heat flows from the higher-temperature body to the lower-temperature body until their temperatures become equal. After that, no net heat flow takes place between them.

SLO PHY-01
Q9. What is a diathermic substance? What is a diathermic process?
Also asked as: Define diathermic process.

A diathermic substance is one that allows heat to pass through it, and the process of heat passing through it is called a diathermic process.

SLO PHY-01
Q10. In Fig. 8.1(a), what temperatures are shown before and after the heat transfer?
Also asked as: Name the labelled parts of Fig. 8.1(a).

Before: 100 °C (hot region) and 0 °C (cold region), with an arrow showing thermal energy transferred from hot to cold. After some time: both regions are at 50 °C, so they are in thermal equilibrium and no more energy is transferred.

SLO PHY-01
Q11. When is a system in thermal equilibrium with its surroundings?
Also asked as: State the condition for equilibrium between a system and its surroundings.

A system is in thermal equilibrium with the surroundings if each part of the system and the surroundings are at the same temperature.

SLO PHY-01
Q12. Define a system in thermodynamics. Give an example.
Also asked as: What is meant by "system"?

The matter or space region that is being studied is called a system, for example a gas in a cylinder.

SLO PHY-01
Q13. What are the surroundings of a system?
Also asked as: Define the boundary of a system and state what takes place through it.

Surroundings: everything outside the system. Boundary: the surface separating the system from the surroundings; all energy changes caused by work done or heat exchange take place through this boundary.

SLO PHY-01
Q14. Define a closed system. Give an example.
Also asked as: In which kind of system can heat enter or leave but mass cannot?

A closed system is one in which heat energy can enter or leave but mass cannot. Example: a container sealed on all sides.

SLO PHY-01
Q15. Define an open system. Give an example.
Also asked as: In which kind of system can both heat and mass enter or leave?

An open system is one in which both heat energy and mass can enter or leave. Example: a glass beaker with an open top, where water can be added or removed and heat can be added or removed.

SLO PHY-01
Q16. Define an isolated system. Give an example.
Also asked as: Why is a thermos flask filled with hot water called an isolated system?

An isolated system has no transfer of mass or heat energy across its boundary. A thermos flask filled with hot water is an example, because neither heat nor mass crosses its boundary.

SLO PHY-01
Q17. Differentiate between closed, open and isolated systems.
Also asked as: Classify the system in each case: a sealed container, an open beaker of water, a thermos flask of hot water.

Open: mass and heat can cross (open beaker). Closed: heat can cross, mass cannot (sealed container). Isolated: neither mass nor heat can cross (thermos flask).

8.1.1 Internal Energy

Do You Know? (book box). When a substance melts or boils, energy is required to break the bonds holding the particles together, which increases the potential energy.
Figure 8.1(b) in words: a P–V graph shows a system going from state A (P₁, V₁, T₁) to state B (P₂, V₂, T₂) by two different paths, C₁ and C₂.
Q18. Define internal energy.
Also asked as: What is the sum of all forms of molecular energies of a substance called?

The sum of all the forms of molecular energies (kinetic and potential) of a substance is called its internal energy.

SLO PHY-02
Q19. Why is the internal energy of an ideal gas only the translational kinetic energy of its molecules?
Also asked as: What is an ideal gas in terms of molecular forces and collisions?

In an ideal gas all collisions between atoms or molecules are perfectly elastic and there are no intermolecular attractive forces, so there is no potential energy. The internal energy is therefore the translational K.E. of its molecules.

SLO PHY-02
Q20. Write the expression for the average kinetic energy of a gas molecule (Eq. 8.1).
Also asked as: According to kinetic theory, how is the average K.E. of gas molecules related to temperature?

⟨½mv²⟩ = (3/2)kT, where k is Boltzmann's constant and T is the absolute temperature. The average K.E. is directly proportional to T.

SLO PHY-02
Q21. How does the internal energy of an ideal gas change with temperature?
Also asked as: Relate a rise in temperature of a gas to its internal energy.

T ∝ ⟨K.E.⟩, so the internal energy of an ideal gas is directly proportional to its temperature. When the temperature rises, the average K.E. and so the internal energy increase.

SLO PHY-12
Q22. In what two ways can the internal energy of a system be increased?
Also asked as: Name the two ways of raising the internal energy of a substance.

By heating it and by doing mechanical work on it.

SLO PHY-02
Q23. Give examples showing that mechanical work can raise internal energy.
Also asked as: A piece of metal is hammered. Does its internal energy increase?

Rubbing two objects together raises their internal energy and temperature. A sliding object that comes to rest by friction has its mechanical work partly converted into internal energy. So a hammered piece of metal gains internal energy (its temperature rises).

SLO PHY-02
Q24. What is the internal energy of a real gas made of?
Also asked as: Why is the internal energy of a real gas a function of both temperature and volume?

In a real gas the molecules attract each other, so internal energy is partly kinetic energy and partly intermolecular potential energy. Hence it depends on temperature and volume.

SLO PHY-02
Q25. Why is internal energy called a function of state?
Also asked as: Does the change in internal energy depend on the path followed?

Internal energy is a function of state: it depends only on the initial and final states of the system, not on the path taken.

SLO PHY-02
Q26. In Fig. 8.1(b), compare the change in internal energy along paths C₁ and C₂ from A to B.
Also asked as: Name the labelled points and paths of Fig. 8.1(b).

Points: A (P₁, V₁, T₁) and B (P₂, V₂, T₂); paths: C₁ and C₂. The change in internal energy is the same along both paths because it depends only on the temperature difference between A and B.

SLO PHY-02
Q27. Why do we use the change in internal energy and not its absolute value?

Like gravitational potential energy, it is the change that is important, not the absolute value.

SLO PHY-02
Q28. State the sign convention for the change in internal energy ΔU.
Also asked as: What is the sign of ΔU when the temperature of a system rises, falls or stays constant?

ΔU = U₂ − U₁. Temperature rises: U₂ > U₁, ΔU positive. Temperature falls: U₂ < U₁, ΔU negative. Temperature constant: U₂ = U₁, ΔU = 0.

SLO PHY-12
Q29. When a substance melts or boils, what happens to the energy supplied to it?
Also asked as: Can the internal energy of a system rise while its temperature stays the same?

The energy supplied breaks the bonds holding the particles together and increases the potential energy. So internal energy rises while the temperature stays constant (for example, melting ice at 0 °C).

SLO PHY-02
Q30. Does the internal energy of an ideal gas depend on pressure?
Also asked as: Out of solid, liquid and gas of the same mass and temperature, which has the greatest and which the least internal energy?

For an ideal gas the internal energy depends only on temperature (U ∝ T), not on pressure. For the same mass and temperature the gas has the greatest and the solid the least, because melting and boiling need energy that adds potential energy.

8.1.2 Ideal Gas Equation And Its Applications

Do You Know? (book box) — ideal gas conditions. (1) The gas particles have negligible volume. (2) The particles are equally sized and have no attraction or repulsion with other particles. (3) The particles undergo perfectly elastic collisions with no energy loss.
Common Mistake: Putting the temperature in °C in PV = nRT. Always use kelvin: T(K) = t(°C) + 273.
Q31. State the ideal gas equation (equation of state) and name its symbols.
Also asked as: Write the relation between pressure, volume, moles and temperature of an ideal gas.

PV = nRT (Eq. 8.2). P = pressure, V = volume, n = number of moles, T = temperature in kelvin, R = universal gas constant = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

SLO PHY-03
Q32. What conditions must a gas satisfy to be called ideal?
Also asked as: List the three ideal-gas assumptions given in the book.

(1) Its particles have negligible volume. (2) The particles are equally sized and have no attraction or repulsion between them. (3) The particles undergo perfectly elastic collisions with no energy loss.

SLO PHY-03
Q33. Show that PV = NkT starting from PV = nRT.
Also asked as: Write the ideal gas equation in terms of the number of molecules N.

n = N/N_A (N = total molecules, N_A = Avogadro number). So PV = (N/N_A)RT = N(R/N_A)T. Putting R/N_A = k gives PV = NkT (Eq. 8.3).

SLO PHY-04
Q34. What is the Boltzmann constant? Write its relation with R and N_A.
Also asked as: State that the Boltzmann constant is given by k = R/N_A.

The Boltzmann constant is k = R/N_A (gas constant per molecule). Its value is k = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ ÷ 6.022140857 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 1.3806452 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ (taken as 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ in numericals).

SLO PHY-04
Q35. What are the dimensions and unit of the Boltzmann constant?
Also asked as: The unit of k is the same as the unit of which other quantity?

Dimensions: [ML²T⁻²K⁻¹]. The unit J K⁻¹ is the same as the unit of entropy.

SLO PHY-04
Q36. Calculate k from R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and N_A = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

Formula: k = R/N_A

  • k = 8.314 / (6.022 × 10²³)
  • Answer: k = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹

8.2 Kinetic Theory Of Gases

Figure 8.2 in words: a cube-shaped container with several gas molecules moving inside it; the labelled parts are "Gas molecule" and "Container".
Q41. Who developed the kinetic theory of gases and what does it explain?
Also asked as: What does the kinetic theory relate to what?

In the 19th century James Clerk Maxwell, Rudolph Clausius and others developed it. It explains how the motion of molecules influences macroscopic properties like temperature and pressure, relating T, P, V to microscopic properties such as K.E.

SLO PHY-05
Q42. Describe the basic assumptions of the kinetic theory of gases.
Also asked as: State the four assumptions of kinetic theory.

(1) A finite volume of gas consists of a very large number of molecules. (2) The size of the molecules is much smaller than the separation between them. (3) The molecules move randomly and change direction after every collision; collisions among molecules and with the walls are perfectly elastic. (4) Molecules exert no force on each other except during a collision.

SLO PHY-05
Q43. According to kinetic theory, what is a gas composed of?
Also asked as: Name the labelled parts of Fig. 8.2.

A gas is composed of a number of tiny, hard spheres (molecules) that collide with one another and with the container walls, moving according to Newton's laws. Fig. 8.2 labels the gas molecule and the container.

SLO PHY-05
Q44. Why are the collisions of gas molecules assumed to be perfectly elastic?

Perfectly elastic collisions mean no energy loss, so the molecules keep moving randomly with the same average kinetic energy.

SLO PHY-05
Q45. State the condition for a real gas to behave as an ideal gas.
Also asked as: At what pressure and temperature does a real gas behave ideally?

At low pressure and high temperature the density is low, the molecules are far apart and intermolecular forces are negligible, so the real gas behaves as an ideal gas.

SLO PHY-03
Q46. Show that the density of a gas is ρ = PM/RT.
Also asked as: Write the relation between density, pressure, molar mass and temperature of an ideal gas.

n = m/M, so PV = (m/M)RT, or PM = (m/V)RT. With m/V = ρ: PM = ρRT, hence ρ = PM/RT (Eq. 8.4). For a given mass, ρ ∝ P/T.

SLO PHY-03
Q47. Why can a real gas not behave as an ideal gas at high pressure and low temperature?

The density is high, the molecules are close together and the intermolecular forces are stronger.

SLO PHY-03
Q48. Find the density of oxygen (molar mass 32 g mol⁻¹) at 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa and 273 K. Take R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Given: P = 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa; M = 0.032 kg mol⁻¹; T = 273 K. Find: ρ

  • Formula: ρ = PM/RT
  • ρ = (1.013 × 10⁵ × 0.032) / (8.314 × 273)
  • Answer: ρ = 1.43 kg m⁻³

8.2.1 Work In Thermodynamics

Figure 8.3 in words: a cylinder with a movable frictionless piston of area A; the gas of volume V exerts force F = PA and the piston rises through distance d, so ΔV = Ad. Figure 8.4 in words: a P–V graph with a horizontal line at constant pressure from V₁ to V₂; the rectangle under the line (ΔV wide) is the work.
Do You Know? (book box). Work is a form of energy with unit joule (1 J = 1 kg m² s⁻²). Other units are sometimes used, such as atmospheres for pressure and litres for volume, giving the litre-atmosphere (l-atm) as a unit of work.
Q49. Derive the expression W = PΔV for the work done by a gas.
Also asked as: Show that the work done by a gas expanding at constant pressure is PΔV.

Pressure P = F/A, so F = PA. Piston moves a small distance Δy, so W = F Δy = PA Δy. Since AΔy = ΔV, W = PΔV (Eq. 8.5).

SLO PHY-06
Q50. Name the quantities in W = PΔV and write how ΔV is found.

W = work done by the gas, P = external pressure (not the gas pressure inside), ΔV = V_final − V_initial.

SLO PHY-06
Q51. Name the labelled parts of Fig. 8.3.
Also asked as: What do A, F, d and ΔV stand for in the piston–cylinder figure?

A: cross-sectional area of the piston; F = PA: force of the gas on the piston; P: pressure; V: volume of the gas; d (= Δy): distance moved by the piston; ΔV = Ad: change in volume.

SLO PHY-06
Q52. How can the work done by a gas be found from a P–V graph?
Also asked as: What does the area under the P–V graph represent?

The area under the P–V graph equals the work done. For constant pressure it is a rectangle: area = P ΔV (Fig. 8.4).

SLO PHY-06
Q53. What is the formula for work done by a constant force? How is it used for gases?

Work = force × displacement. For gases the force is F = PA, which gives W = PΔV when the gas expands or contracts.

SLO PHY-06
Q54. A gas expands at constant pressure 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa from 0.010 m³ to 0.025 m³. Find the work done by the gas.

Given: P = 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa; V₁ = 0.010 m³; V₂ = 0.025 m³. Find: W

  • Formula: W = PΔV = P(V₂ − V₁)
  • W = 2.0 × 10⁵ × (0.025 − 0.010)
  • Answer: W = 3.0 × 10³ J (positive: done by the gas)
SLO PHY-06
Q55. Example 8.2 — A 2 kg of argon gas is heated to 30 °C. If it is contained in a cylinder of radius 1 m and height 2 m, what is the pressure of the gas? (Molar mass of argon = 40 g mol⁻¹)

Given: m = 2 kg = 2000 g; T = 30 + 273 = 303 K; r = 1 m; h = 2 m. Find: P

  • Volume: V = πr²h = 3.142 × (1)² × 2 = 6.284 m³
  • Moles: n = 2000 g / 40 g mol⁻¹ = 50 mol
  • Formula: PV = nRT, so P = nRT/V
  • P = (50 × 8.314 × 303) / 6.284
  • Answer: P = 20044 Pa (about 2.0 × 10⁴ Pa)
SLO PHY-03
Q56. Assignment 8.2 — A sample of gas is compressed to one third of its initial volume at constant pressure 2.25 × 10⁵ N m⁻². During the compression 200 J of work is done on the gas. Determine the final volume of the gas.

Given: P = 2.25 × 10⁵ Pa; V_f = V_i/3; work done on gas = 200 J, so W (by gas) = −200 J. Find: V_f

  • V_i = 200 / (1.5 × 10⁵) = 1.33 × 10⁻³ m³
  • V_f = V_i / 3

8.2.2 Difference Between Work Done By The Gas And On The Gas

8.4 First Law Of Thermodynamics

Whenever heat is added to a system, the internal energy increases due to the rise in temperature. At the same time, if the system is allowed to expand, it may do work on its environment. The first law is based on the law of conservation of energy.

Do You Know? (book box). Thermodynamics also applies to living bodies (biological thermodynamics). When a boy rides a bicycle, heat (Q) is transferred from his body and work (W) is done by him, which removes internal energy (U). Humans and other living things get energy from the food they eat.
Q77. State the first law of thermodynamics.
Also asked as: Define the first law of thermodynamics.

When heat (Q) is added to a system, it increases the internal energy (ΔU) of the system and also does work (W) by the system on the environment.

SLO PHY-08
Q78. Write the mathematical form of the first law and name each symbol.

Q = ΔU + W (Eq. 8.7). Q = heat added to the system, ΔU = change in internal energy = U_f − U_i, W = work done by the system on the environment.

SLO PHY-08
Q79. Justify how the first law of thermodynamics expresses the conservation of energy.
Also asked as: Where does the heat supplied to a system go according to the first law?

The heat supplied is neither lost nor created: it goes partly into raising the internal energy (ΔU) and partly into work done (W). The total energy before and after is the same, which is the law of conservation of energy applied to heat and work.

SLO PHY-11
Q80. What happens to the internal energy when heat is removed from a system?
Also asked as: State the sign conventions for Q, W and ΔU in the first law.

Heat added: Q positive; heat removed: Q negative and the internal energy decreases. Work done by the system: W positive; work done on the system (compression): W negative.

SLO PHY-08
Q81. Why does the change in internal energy depend only on the initial and final states?

ΔU = U_f − U_i. Internal energy is a function of state, so ΔU is independent of the path followed.

SLO PHY-08
Q82. Give four everyday examples of the first law of thermodynamics.

(1) Melting of ice cubes. (2) Sweating in a crowded room: heat from the body goes to the sweat, which takes heat away but warms the room. (3) Filling air in a car tyre: the mechanical work of pumping raises the internal energy. (4) Human metabolism: doing work lowers internal energy, so food is needed.

SLO PHY-11

Adiabatic expansion: the gas does work, its internal energy and temperature decrease. Adiabatic compression: its internal energy and temperature increase.

The gas expands or compresses rapidly so that there is no time for heat to enter or leave (Q = 0).

Q111. Define a heat engine.
Also asked as: What does a heat engine do? Give two examples.

A heat engine is a device that converts heat energy into mechanical work. Examples: petrol engine and steam engine.

SLO PHY-13
Q112. What are the three essential parts of a heat engine?

(1) A hot reservoir (source), (2) a cold reservoir (sink), (3) a working substance.

SLO PHY-13
Q113. What is the role of the hot reservoir?
Also asked as: Name the symbols for temperature and heat of the hot reservoir.

It is the source of heat to be converted into useful work, and its temperature (T₁) must be kept at a higher level. It supplies a quantity of heat Q₁ flowing towards low temperature.

Q114. Why is a cold reservoir essential for a heat engine?

The temperature difference between the two reservoirs is responsible for the heat flow, since heat can flow only when there is a difference. The cold reservoir (sink) at T₂ absorbs the heat Q₂.

SLO PHY-13
Q115. What is the working substance of a heat engine?

The main part that practically converts heat into useful mechanical work; in most engines it is a gas. It performs work due to heat flowing from the source to the sink.

SLO PHY-13
Q116. State the working principle of a heat engine.
Also asked as: On which law is the working of a heat engine based?

The working principle is the second law of thermodynamics: useful work can be extracted during the heat transfer from a hotter body to a colder one.

SLO PHY-13
Q117. Write the relation between W, Q₁ and Q₂ for a heat engine.
Also asked as: Why is a heat engine made to work in cycles?

W = Q₁ − Q₂. For continuous operation the engine is made cyclic, so the working substance returns to its initial state each cycle.

SLO PHY-13

(1) Intake: piston moves down and the petrol–air mixture is drawn in through the inlet valve. (2) Compression: piston moves up, valves closed, the mixture is compressed adiabatically. (3) Power: a spark ignites it, temperature and pressure rise rapidly, the gas expands adiabatically and pushes the piston down (this stroke delivers the power). (4) Exhaust: piston moves up, the outlet valve opens and residual gases are expelled.

Q120. What is the approximate efficiency of a petrol engine?

About 25% to 30%; the rest of the energy is expelled to the atmosphere.

8.6 Reversible And Irreversible Processes

Figure 8.15 in words: a P–V graph with two paths between points 1 and 2: path A from 1 to 2 and path B from 2 back to 1, forming a closed loop (cyclic process).
Do You Know? (book box). A thermodynamic scale of temperature has two fixed points: absolute zero (0 K) and the triple point of water, where ice, liquid and vapour co-exist (273.16 K).
Q122. Define a reversible process. Give examples.
Also asked as: What is meant by a reversible process?

A process that can be retraced in exactly the reverse order without causing any change to its surroundings. Examples: the transformation between water and ice, slow liquefaction and evaporation, melting, freezing, boiling and condensing.

SLO PHY-14
Q123. Can any real process be exactly reversible?
Also asked as: If heat is absorbed in a direct process, what happens in the reverse process?

In reality no actual change is exactly reversible, but some changes done slowly can be taken as reversible. If heat is absorbed in the direct process it is evolved in the reverse; if work is done by the system in the direct process, it is done on the system in the reverse.

SLO PHY-14
Q124. Define an irreversible process. Give examples.
Also asked as: Name three irreversible processes.

A process that cannot be retraced in exactly the reverse order without causing a change to its surroundings. Examples: any process involving friction or dissipation of energy, decay of animals and plants, and convection, conduction and radiation.

SLO PHY-14
Q125. Differentiate between reversible and irreversible processes.

Reversible: can be retraced exactly without any change in the surroundings (slow melting, freezing, boiling). Irreversible: cannot be retraced without a change in the surroundings (friction, decay, heat flow by conduction).

SLO PHY-14
Q126. Define a cyclic process. Give an example.
Also asked as: What is a cyclic process in Fig. 8.15?

A succession of events that brings the system back to its initial condition. Example: event A takes the system from condition 1 to 2 and event B brings it back from 2 to 1.

SLO PHY-14
Q127. What is the net change in internal energy in a cyclic process?

Zero (ΔU = 0), because the working substance returns to its initial state.

SLO PHY-14
Q128. Why are heat engines normally operated in cycles, and what is a reversible cycle?

A cyclic operation lets the engine work continuously. In a reversible cycle all the changes are reversible; this is an idealization, but real processes can be taken close to it by ignoring minute changes.

8.7 Second Law Of Thermodynamics

For Your Information (book box). The second law addresses the direction of natural processes and the concept of entropy. Heat flows naturally from hot objects to cold ones, not the opposite. If a cup of hot tea is placed in a room with an air-conditioner running, the tea cools due to the low room temperature; when the A.C. is switched off, the room warms up but the tea does not warm back to its initial level.
Q129. Why is a second law of thermodynamics needed when we have the first law?

The first law is the law of conservation of energy and deals with conversion of heat into work and vice versa, but it does not address the conditions under which the conversion takes place. The second law deals with these circumstances and with the direction of heat flow.

SLO PHY-15
Q130. State Lord Kelvin's statement of the second law.
Also asked as: Can a heat engine convert all the heat taken from one reservoir into work?

It is impossible for a heat engine to convert heat taken from a single reservoir entirely into work without leaving any change in the working system.

SLO PHY-15
Q131. State Rudolf Clausius's statement of the second law.
Also asked as: Can heat flow from a cold body to a hot body by itself?

It is impossible to cause heat to flow from a cold body to a hot body without the expenditure of work.

SLO PHY-15
Q132. Why can the heat content of our atmosphere not be used to do work?

The atmosphere is a single heat source and there is no sink at a lower temperature relative to it. A single reservoir cannot be made to perform any work, no matter how much energy it contains.

SLO PHY-15
Q133. Why are both a hot and a cold reservoir essential for work?
Also asked as: Write the work done in terms of Q_h and Q_c.

A source Q₁ rejects heat towards a sink Q₂ at lower temperature while some heat is converted into mechanical work: W = Q_h − Q_c. In a cycle the substance returns to its initial state, so ΔU = 0.

SLO PHY-15
Q134. Explain with an example that heat naturally flows in only one direction.

A hot cup of tea in an air-conditioned room cools. If the A.C. is switched off the room warms, but the tea does not warm up to its earlier level. Heat naturally flows only from the hotter body to the colder one.

SLO PHY-15
Q135. State the second law of thermodynamics in terms of entropy.

If a system undergoes a natural process, it proceeds in the direction that causes the entropy of the system plus the environment to increase.

Q136. Compare Kelvin's and Clausius's statements.

8.8 Carnot's Engine

Q137. What is a Carnot engine?
Also asked as: Why is a Carnot engine called the most efficient engine?

A Carnot heat engine is a theoretical engine that operates on the Carnot cycle. A heat engine operating in an ideal reversible cycle between a heat source and a heat sink would be the most efficient engine.

SLO PHY-16
Q138. State the working principle of Carnot's engine.
Also asked as: Which processes make up the Carnot cycle?

It works on isothermal and adiabatic processes: isothermal expansion, adiabatic expansion, isothermal compression and adiabatic compression, which brings the gas back to its initial state while giving useful work.

SLO PHY-16
Q139. Describe the isothermal expansion step of the Carnot cycle.

The gas expands isothermally at the high temperature T₁, absorbing heat Q₁ from the hot reservoir. Pressure falls from P₁ to P₂ and volume rises from V₁ to V₂; the gas does work while its temperature stays constant (portion ab, Fig. 8.17 A).

SLO PHY-16
Q140. Describe the adiabatic expansion step of the Carnot cycle.

The cylinder is insulated, so the gas neither absorbs nor releases heat. It expands adiabatically and its temperature drops from T₁ to T₂; pressure falls to P₃ and volume rises to V₃ (portion bc, Fig. 8.17 B).

SLO PHY-16
Q141. Describe the isothermal compression step of the Carnot cycle.

The gas is kept at the low temperature T₂ and is compressed, rejecting heat Q₂. Pressure rises to P₄ and volume falls to V₄; work is done on the gas (portion cd, Fig. 8.17 C).

SLO PHY-16
Q142. Describe the adiabatic compression step of the Carnot cycle.

The gas is again placed in an insulated cylinder and compressed adiabatically, so its temperature rises from T₂ to T₁. Pressure rises to P₁ and volume falls to V₁ (portion da, Fig. 8.17 D).

SLO PHY-16
Q143. Name the four points and the four curves of the Carnot cycle in Fig. 8.16.

Points: a (P₁, V₁, T₁), b (P₂, V₂, T₁), c (P₃, V₃, T₂), d (P₄, V₄, T₂). Curves: ab isothermal expansion, bc adiabatic expansion, cd isothermal compression, da adiabatic compression.

SLO PHY-16
Q144. Why is ΔU zero for a complete Carnot cycle? How is the total work found?

The working substance returns to its initial state, so there is no change in internal energy. The total work done in one cycle is the area enclosed by the path abcda.

SLO PHY-16
Q145. Write the work done by a Carnot engine in terms of heat absorbed and rejected.

From the first law Q = ΔU + W with ΔU = 0, we get W = Q, i.e. W = Q₁ − Q₂ (Eq. 8.8), where Q₁ is absorbed in the isothermal expansion and Q₂ is rejected in the isothermal compression.

SLO PHY-23
Q146. Define the efficiency of a heat engine and derive η = 1 − Q₂/Q₁.

η = output (work) / input (heat) = (Q₁ − Q₂)/Q₁ = 1 − Q₂/Q₁.

SLO PHY-23
Q147. Write the efficiency of a Carnot engine in terms of temperatures.
Also asked as: State that the Carnot cycle sets a limit for the efficiency of a heat engine.

Heat Q is proportional to absolute temperature T, so η = 1 − T₂/T₁ (Eq. 8.9), where T₁ is the hot reservoir and T₂ the cold reservoir. In percentage: η = (1 − T₂/T₁) × 100%. No engine between these temperatures can exceed this.

SLO PHY-23
Q148. On what does the efficiency of a Carnot engine depend?
Also asked as: Why is it impossible for a real engine to have 100% efficiency?

It is independent of the nature of the working substance and depends only on the reservoir temperatures. For 100% efficiency T₂/T₁ would have to be zero, which needs T₂ = 0 or T₁ = ∞; there is no reservoir at absolute zero or at infinite temperature.

SLO PHY-23
Q149. State Carnot's theorem.
Also asked as: How can the efficiency of a heat engine be increased and why are real engines less efficient?

No heat engine can be more efficient than a Carnot engine operating between the same two temperatures. Usually the cold reservoir is the atmosphere, so efficiency is raised by increasing the temperature of the hot reservoir. All real engines are less efficient due to heat losses.

8.9 Refrigerator

Figure 8.18 in words: a heat pump: hot reservoir at T₁ receives heat Q₁, work W is done on the pump, and heat Q₂ is taken from the cold reservoir at T₂.
Q154. Define a refrigerator.
Also asked as: Why does heat not flow from a cold body to a hot body without work?

A refrigerator is a device designed to remove heat from a region that is at lower temperature than its surroundings. Heat flows naturally from hot to cold, so to move it from cold to hot the working of a heat engine has to be reversed and work done on the system.

SLO PHY-17
Q155. How is a refrigerator related to a heat engine?
Also asked as: Describe a refrigerator as a heat engine operating in reverse.

A refrigerator is a device in which the working substance performs a cycle in a direction opposite to that of a heat engine. It removes Q₂ from a cold reservoir at T₂, work W is done on it by the compressor, and Q₁ is rejected to a hot reservoir at T₁.

SLO PHY-17
Q156. What is a heat pump?

The same device used to heat a space that is at higher temperature than the surroundings is called a heat pump (Fig. 8.18).

SLO PHY-17
Q157. What is the working substance of a refrigerator? What does the compressor do?

The working substance is usually a gas called the refrigerant. The compressor performs work W on it.

SLO PHY-17
Q158. Write the energy balance of a refrigerator.

W = Q₁ − Q₂ or Q₁ = Q₂ + W: the heat rejected to the hot reservoir equals the heat removed from the cold reservoir plus the work done.

SLO PHY-17
Q159. Define the coefficient of performance (CP) of a refrigerator for cooling and derive its formula.

CP (cooling) is the ratio of the heat removed from the cold reservoir to the work required: CP = Q₂/W = Q₂/(Q₁ − Q₂) (Eq. 8.10). Since Q ∝ T, CP (cooling) = T₂/(T₁ − T₂).

SLO PHY-17
Q160. Write the coefficient of performance for heating (heat pump).

CP (heating) = Q₁/W = Q₁/(Q₁ − Q₂) (Eq. 8.11) = T₁/(T₁ − T₂).

SLO PHY-17

Q165. What is entropy?

Also asked as: Explain that entropy S is a quantity related to the disorder of the particles of a system.

Entropy is a thermodynamic quantity that represents the unavailability of a system's thermal energy for conversion into mechanical work. It is simply the degree of disorder or randomness in a system.

SLO PHY-22

Entropy is the measure of a system's thermal energy per unit temperature that is unavailable for doing useful work.

The change in entropy is positive if heat is added to the system and negative if heat is removed from the system.

Q186. Link temperature, kinetic energy and internal energy of an ideal gas.
Also asked as: Why does a rise in temperature of an ideal gas mean a rise in its internal energy?

T measures the average K.E.: ⟨½mv²⟩ = (3/2)kT. An ideal gas has no intermolecular potential energy, so its internal energy is just the total K.E. Hence T ↑ ⇒ ⟨K.E.⟩ ↑ ⇒ U ↑ and ΔU ∝ ΔT.

SLO PHY-02 · PHY-12 · PHY-03
Q187. How do Boyle's, Charles's and Gay-Lussac's laws follow from PV = nRT?

Keep n fixed. T constant: PV = nRT = constant (Boyle). P constant: V/T = nR/P = constant (Charles). V constant: P/T = nR/V = constant (Gay-Lussac). So one equation contains all three laws.

SLO PHY-03 · PHY-10
Q188. Connect PV = nRT, PV = NkT and k = R/N_A.

n = N/N_A. Substituting in PV = nRT gives PV = N(R/N_A)T. Writing k = R/N_A gives PV = NkT. So k is the gas constant per molecule, and PV = nRT (moles) and PV = NkT (molecules) are the same law.

SLO PHY-03 · PHY-04
Q189. How is the work done by a gas linked with the area under the P–V graph in the four processes?

In every process the work equals the area under its P–V curve. Isobaric: rectangle P ΔV. Isochoric: no width, so area and W are zero. Isothermal and adiabatic: area under a falling curve (adiabat is steeper). W = PΔV is the special case of constant pressure.

SLO PHY-06 · PHY-10
Q190. How does the first law Q = ΔU + W give the four processes?
Also asked as: Fill the first-law form for each process.

Isothermal (ΔU = 0): Q = W. Adiabatic (Q = 0): W = −ΔU. Isochoric (W = 0): Q = ΔU. Isobaric: Q = ΔU + PΔV. Each process is just the first law with one term removed or kept.

Q191. Which law and which first-law form describe a pressure cooker, and why?

The cooker is a closed container of fixed volume: isochoric. Heating raises temperature and pressure (Gay-Lussac: P ∝ T) and W = 0, so Q = ΔU.

SLO PHY-08 · PHY-10
Q192. Pumping air into a tyre warms the pump. Explain with the first law.

The mechanical work of pumping compresses the air quickly, so very little heat escapes (Q ≈ 0): W (by gas) is negative and ΔU = −W is positive. The internal energy and temperature rise.

SLO PHY-08 · PHY-12
Q193. Why is the Carnot cycle made of two isothermal and two adiabatic processes?

Heat is exchanged only during the isothermal steps (Q₁ absorbed at T₁, Q₂ rejected at T₂); the adiabatic steps move the gas between the two temperatures with no heat flow. This makes every step reversible and the cycle ideal.

Q195. How do Kelvin's and Clausius's statements connect to the heat engine and the refrigerator?

  • Formula: Q₂/Q₁ = T₂/T₁
  • Q₂ = Q × (T/3)/T
  • Answer: Q₂ = Q/3

Ex NP 8.9 — A gas expands from volume 1 m³ to 2 m³ at constant atmospheric pressure. (a) Calculate the work done by the gas. (b) Represent the work done in PV diagram. (Ans: 1.013 × 10⁵ J)

Given: P = 1 atm = 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa; V₁ = 1 m³; V₂ = 2 m³.

  • (a) Formula: W = PΔV = P(V₂ − V₁) = 1.013 × 10⁵ × (2 − 1)
  • Answer (a): W = 1.013 × 10⁵ J (positive: done by the gas)
  • (b) P–V diagram in words: volume on the horizontal axis (1 to 2 m³), pressure on the vertical axis; a horizontal line (isobar) at P = 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa from V = 1 m³ to V = 2 m³; the shaded rectangle under it (width 1 m³, height 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa) represents the work, W = 1.013 × 10⁵ J.
