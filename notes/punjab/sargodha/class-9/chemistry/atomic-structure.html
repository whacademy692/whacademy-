Part 1 CHEM-01

Why Elements Differ

Introduction — elements look and behave differently because their atoms differ.

Notes

Elements are very different from one another. A large number of elements exist as solids, very few are liquids, and the rest are gases. All of them are made up of atoms.

  • Sulphur looks very different from gold, which looks very different from bromine (Fig 2.1).
  • Iron is a heavy metal, while aluminium and zinc are light metals.
  • Metals are mostly lustrous; non-metals such as sulphur and carbon appear dull.
  • The difference in the properties of elements is due to the difference in the properties of their constituent atoms.

Fig 2.1 — "Different Elements look different": three photographs (sulphur, gold, bromine).

Questions and answers

1. Why do elements differ from one another?
• What is the reason for the difference in the properties of elements?

The difference in the properties of elements is due to the difference in the properties of their constituent atoms.

2. In which physical states do elements exist?

A large number of elements exist as solids, very few as liquids, and the rest as gases.

3. What are all elements made up of?

All elements are made up of atoms.

4. Give two examples to show that metals and non-metals differ in appearance.

Metals such as iron, aluminium and zinc are mostly lustrous, while non-metals such as sulphur and carbon appear dull.

Part 3 CHEM-01 CHEM-06 CHEM-07

Discovery of Electrons

Section 2.1 — cathode rays and J. J. Thomson's experiments.

Notes

Discharge tube: a hard glass tube provided with two metallic electrodes and a vacuum pump to evacuate the gas present in it (Fig 2.2).

  • When a very high voltage is applied to a gas at very low pressure, the glass surface behind the positive electrode starts to glow, due to rays emitted from the cathode. These rays were named cathode rays.
  • J. J. Thomson (1897, British physicist) studied cathode rays: 1. Passed them through oppositely charged electric plates → the rays bent towards the positive plate → they carry negative charge. 2. Installed two magnets on either side of the tube → the rays were also diverted by the magnetic field. 3. Used his findings to calculate the mass-to-charge ratio of cathode rays → proved they are negatively charged material particles.
  • These particles were later named electrons. Electrons are the subatomic particles of all elements.

Fig 2.2 — Discharge Tube Experiment (Cathode Rays): a glass tube with a cathode, an anode and a screen where the rays cause a glow.

Questions and answers

11. What is a discharge tube?
• Of what is a discharge tube made, and what is its use?

A discharge tube is a hard glass tube provided with two metallic electrodes and a vacuum pump to evacuate the gas present in it.

12. What is observed when a very high voltage is applied to a gas at very low pressure in a discharge tube?

The glass surface behind the positive electrode starts to glow, due to the rays emitted from the cathode.

13. What are cathode rays?
• Why were the rays in a discharge tube named cathode rays?

The rays emitted from the cathode (negative electrode) of a discharge tube, which make the glass behind the positive electrode glow, are called cathode rays.

14. Who studied the properties of cathode rays, and when?

The British physicist Joseph John (J. J.) Thomson studied the properties of cathode rays in 1897.

15. How did Thomson show that cathode rays carry a negative charge?

He passed them through oppositely charged electric plates; the rays bent towards the positively charged plate, showing that they carry a negative charge.

16. What did Thomson observe when he placed two magnets on either side of the discharge tube?

The cathode rays were also diverted by the magnetic field.

17. How did Thomson prove that cathode rays are material particles?

He used the findings of his experiments to calculate the mass-to-charge ratio of cathode rays, which proved that they are negatively charged material particles.

18. What name was given to the particles of cathode rays?
• Which subatomic particle is present in all elements and carries a negative charge?

They were named electrons. Electrons are the subatomic particles of all elements.

19. Name the labelled parts shown in Fig 2.2 (discharge tube).

Glass tube, cathode, anode (positive electrode) and the glowing screen; the tube is connected to a vacuum pump.

Part 4 CHEM-01 CHEM-06 CHEM-07 CHEM-08

Discovery of Proton and Neutron

Section 2.1 — Goldstein's canal rays, Rutherford's proton and the neutron.

Notes

Proton - First observed by E. Goldstein in 1886, based on the concept that atoms are electrically neutral, having the same number of positive and negative charges. - He used a gas-discharge tube with a perforated cathode. A new type of ray was produced at the anode which moved towards the cathode — he called them canal rays or anode rays (Fig 2.3). - The properties of these rays varied with the gas used in the tube. What he had discovered was gas ions, including hydrogen ions (H⁺). Goldstein did not know their significance at that time. - 1917 — Rutherford performed experiments which proved that the hydrogen nucleus is present in other nuclei. He thought a hydrogen nucleus (a proton) must be the fundamental building block of all nuclei, and possibly a new fundamental particle.

Neutron - Discovered in 1933; it carries no charge. Its mass is almost the same as that of a proton. - Electron, proton and neutron are called fundamental particles. They are present in all atoms (except ordinary hydrogen, which has no neutron), even though atoms behave very differently from one another. The number of these particles differs from atom to atom.

Fig 2.3 — Anode Rays: a discharge tube with a perforated cathode and an anode; rays move from the anode towards the cathode.

Questions and answers

20. Who discovered the proton, and in which year?

E. Goldstein first observed positively charged particles in 1886.

21. On what concept was Goldstein's search for positive particles based?

On the concept that atoms are electrically neutral, having the same number of positive and negative charges.

22. What are canal rays?
• What are anode rays?

Canal rays (anode rays) are a new type of ray produced at the anode of a gas-discharge tube having a perforated cathode; they move towards the cathode.

23. Why did the properties of canal rays vary?
• What did Goldstein actually discover?

The properties varied with the gas used in the discharge tube because he had discovered gas ions, including hydrogen ions (H⁺).

24. What did Rutherford prove in 1917?

Rutherford proved that the hydrogen nucleus is present in other nuclei. He concluded that a hydrogen nucleus, or proton, must be the fundamental building block of all nuclei.

25. Who discovered the neutron, and in which year?
• What are the charge and mass of a neutron?

The neutron was discovered in 1933. It carries no charge and its mass is almost the same as that of a proton.

26. What are fundamental particles?

Electron, proton and neutron are called fundamental particles; they are present in all atoms except ordinary hydrogen.

27. Which atom does not contain a neutron?

Ordinary hydrogen (¹₁H).

28. Do all atoms contain the same number of fundamental particles?

No. All atoms contain the same three kinds of particles, but different atoms contain different numbers of these particles.

29. Name the labelled parts shown in Fig 2.3 (anode rays).

Cathode (perforated), anode, high-voltage source, and the anode (canal) rays travelling from the anode towards the cathode.

The Nucleus and Fundamental Particles

Section 2.1 — Rutherford's gold foil experiment; charge and mass of the three particles.

Notes

Rutherford, 1911 — hit a stream of special particles at a very thin gold foil. He concluded that an atom has two portions: - a tiny central portion → the nucleus - a relatively large area surrounding it → the extranuclear portion

Almost all the mass of the atom is concentrated in the nucleus, because both heavy particles — protons and neutrons — are present there. In the nucleus these two particles are held together by a strong nuclear force.

Interesting Information: Although the nucleus is less than 1/100,000 of the size of the atom, it contains more than 99.9% of the mass of the atom.

Properties of the fundamental particles

Particle Charge Mass
Electron −1.6022 × 10⁻¹⁹ C 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg
Proton +1.6022 × 10⁻¹⁹ C 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg
Neutron 0.0 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg

Note (SLO "relative charge and mass"): the book prints the actual values above. Relative charge: electron −1, proton +1, neutron 0. Relative mass: proton ≈ neutron ≈ 1, electron ≈ 1/1836 of a proton (the book states the 1836 figure; the relative values are worked out from the table).

Note (SLO "path in a uniform electric field"): the book shows the direction of deflection only in words. A negative particle (electron) is deflected towards the positive plate; a positive particle (proton / H⁺ ion) towards the negative plate; a particle with no charge (neutron) is not deflected and moves straight.

Questions and answers

30. Who discovered the nucleus of the atom, and in which year?

Lord Rutherford discovered the nucleus in 1911.

31. Describe Rutherford's experiment briefly.

Rutherford hit a stream of special particles at a very thin gold foil. From this experiment he concluded that an atom has a tiny central nucleus and a relatively large extranuclear portion around it.

32. Into which two portions did Rutherford divide an atom?

Into a tiny central portion called the nucleus and a relatively large area surrounding it called the extranuclear portion.

33. Which particles are present in the nucleus?
• Which particles are present in the extranuclear portion?

Protons and neutrons are present in the nucleus; electrons revolve in the extranuclear portion (in shells around the nucleus).

34. Why is almost all the mass of an atom concentrated in the nucleus?

Because both the heavy particles, protons and neutrons, are present in the nucleus.

35. What holds protons and neutrons together in the nucleus?

The strong nuclear force.

36. What fraction of the atom's size is the nucleus? What fraction of the atom's mass does it contain?

The nucleus is less than 1/100,000 of the size of the atom, but it contains more than 99.9% of the mass of the atom.

37. Give the charge and mass of an electron.

Charge = −1.6022 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; mass = 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg.

38. Give the charge and mass of a proton.

Charge = +1.6022 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; mass = 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

39. Give the charge and mass of a neutron.

Charge = 0.0 (neutral); mass = 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

40. Which two subatomic particles have nearly the same mass?
• Which subatomic particle is the lightest?

Proton (1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg) and neutron (1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg) have nearly the same mass. The electron (9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg) is the lightest — a proton is 1836 times heavier than an electron.

41. What is the relative charge of an electron, a proton and a neutron?

Electron −1, proton +1, neutron 0.

42. Which particles have equal but opposite charges?

Electron (−1.6022 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) and proton (+1.6022 × 10⁻¹⁹ C).

43. Towards which plate does an electron move in a uniform electric field?
• Towards which plate does a proton move in a uniform electric field?

An electron (negative) moves towards the positive plate; a proton (positive) moves towards the negative plate; a neutron is not deflected.

Part 6 CHEM-02 CHEM-05

Bohr's Atomic Model

Section 2.1 — orbits, energy levels, ground state and sub-shells.

Notes

Niels Bohr, 1913 — proposed a model for the hydrogen atom, called Bohr's atomic model.

  • The electron revolves around the nucleus in specific paths called orbits or shells. In an orbit its energy is fixed.
  • The electron in the orbit closest to the nucleus has minimum energy → this is the ground state of the atom.
  • Orbits farther from the nucleus possess successively greater energy.
  • The electron is not allowed to occupy a space in between the orbits.
  • Since the electron in each shell has fixed energy, shells are also called energy levels. The shell nearest the nucleus is the first shell or K shell; the second shell is farther away and its electron has greater energy; electrons may also be present in the third and higher shells.

Sub-shells (orbitals) — each shell is divided into sub-shells; the number of sub-shells in a shell = n.

Shell n Sub-shells present
K (first) 1 s
L (second) 2 s, p
M (third) 3 s, p, d
N (fourth) 4 s, p, d, f

Interesting Information: The size of an atom is so small that it is not possible to see it with the naked eye. However, a "transition electron microscope" can be used to see atoms. (Printed this way in the book; the usual name is transmission electron microscope.)

Questions and answers

44. Who proposed the atomic model for the hydrogen atom, and when?

Niels Bohr proposed it in 1913; it is called Bohr's atomic model.

45. State Bohr's model of the atom.
• What does Bohr's atomic model say about the electron?

According to Bohr's model, the electron revolves around the nucleus in specific paths called orbits or shells. While in an orbit, the energy of the electron is fixed.

46. What is the ground state of an atom?

When the electron is in the orbit closest to the nucleus, its energy is minimum; this state of the atom is called the ground state.

47. Can an electron occupy a space between two orbits?

No. The electron is not allowed to occupy a space in between the orbits.

48. Why are shells also called energy levels?

Because the electron present in each shell has a fixed energy.

49. What is the K shell?

The shell nearest to the nucleus is called the first shell or K shell; an electron in it has a fixed, lowest value of energy.

50. How does the energy of an electron change as the distance of its shell from the nucleus increases?
• Arrange the energies of the first four shells in order.

The energy increases with distance from the nucleus: E₄ > E₃ > E₂ > E₁ (N > M > L > K).

51. What are sub-shells?
• How many sub-shells are present in a shell?

Each shell is further sub-divided into sub-shells (orbitals). The number of sub-shells in a shell is equal to the value of n of that shell.

52. Name the sub-shells present in the first, second, third and fourth shells.

First shell (n = 1): s. Second shell (n = 2): s, p. Third shell (n = 3): s, p, d. Fourth shell (n = 4): s, p, d, f.

53. Why is an atomic model needed?
• What is the use of an atomic model?

An atomic model is an aid (a simple picture) to understand the structure of an atom, which is too small to be seen with the naked eye.

54. Can atoms be seen with the naked eye? What can be used to see them?

No, the size of an atom is so small that it cannot be seen with the naked eye. A transition (transmission) electron microscope can be used to see atoms.

55. Name the labelled parts shown in the shell diagram (Fig. of Bohr's shells).

Nucleus at the centre; First shell (K, n = 1, E = E₁); Second shell (L, n = 2, E = E₂); Third shell (M, n = 3, E = E₃); Fourth shell (N, n = 4, E = E₄); with E₄ > E₃ > E₂ > E₁.

Part 7 CHEM-02 CHEM-03

Modern Approach and the 2n² Rule

Section 2.1 — electron cloud, probability, and the electron capacity of shells.

Notes

Modern approach: electrons are like charged clouds whose location around the nucleus cannot be predicted with 100% certainty. We can only say there is a certain probability of finding the electron at a certain probable distance from the nucleus.

Note (SLO "uncertainty principle"): the book does not name the uncertainty principle in its text. Standard statement: it is impossible to know both the exact position and the exact speed (momentum) of an electron at the same time, so only the probability of finding it can be given.

Interesting Information: The largest atom, cesium, is approximately nine times bigger than the smallest atom, helium.

Electron capacity of shells — formula 2n² (n = 1, 2, 3 … = shell number; shells are also called K, L, M, N …)

Shell n 2n² Maximum electrons
K 1 2 × 1² 2
L 2 2 × 2² 8
M 3 2 × 3² 18
N 4 2 × 4² 32
  • The K shell may have fewer than two electrons, or may not have any electron.
  • The process continues until the electrons present in the atom are finished.

Electron capacity of sub-shells

Sub-shell s p d f
Maximum electrons 2 6 10 14
  • First shell: 2 electrons → go to the s sub-shell.
  • Second shell: 8 electrons → s = 2 and p = 6.

Questions and answers

56. What is the modern view of the electron in an atom?
• How are electrons described according to the modern approach?

According to the modern approach, electrons are like charged clouds whose location around the nucleus cannot be predicted with one hundred percent certainty; we can only give a probability of finding the electron at a certain probable distance from the nucleus.

57. Can the exact path and location of an electron be mapped?

No. Only the probability of finding the electron at a certain distance from the nucleus can be stated, not its exact path or location.

58. State the uncertainty principle.

It is not possible to know the exact position and the exact speed of an electron at the same time; therefore the path of an electron can only be described by probability.

59. Which is the largest atom and which is the smallest? How much bigger is the largest?

The largest atom is cesium and the smallest is helium; cesium is approximately nine times bigger than helium.

60. State the formula for the maximum number of electrons in a shell.

Maximum number of electrons in a shell = 2n², where n is the number of the shell (1, 2, 3 …).

61. What is the maximum number of electrons that the K shell, the L shell and the M shell can hold?

K shell (n = 1): 2 × 1² = 2; L shell (n = 2): 2 × 2² = 8; M shell (n = 3): 2 × 3² = 18.

62. Can the K shell have fewer than two electrons?

Yes. The K shell may have fewer than two electrons, or it may not have any electron.

63. How many electrons can the s, p, d and f sub-shells hold at the most?

s = 2, p = 6, d = 10 and f = 14 electrons.

64. How are the 8 electrons of the second shell arranged in its sub-shells?

The s sub-shell holds 2 electrons and the p sub-shell holds 6 electrons.

65. To which sub-shell do the two electrons of the first shell go?

They go to the s sub-shell.

Common Mistake: Electrons do not move along fixed, visible circular tracks in the modern view — the shell diagram is only a model. Also, 2n² is the maximum a shell can hold, not the number it must hold.

Part 8 CHEM-09 CHEM-10

Atomic Number (Z)

Section 2.2 — the number of protons that identifies an element.

Notes
  • Electrons, protons and neutrons are the fundamental particles of all types of matter; the atoms of all elements contain the same three particles. An atom of one element differs from another because it contains a different number of them.
  • The number of protons present in the atoms of an element is always fixed → called the atomic number of that element, symbol Z.
  • An atom as a whole is electrically neutral, so the number of electrons = number of protons.
  • Atomic number is unique to each element; the element is identified by this number.
  • In the periodic table, elements are arranged in ascending order of their atomic numbers.

Questions and answers

66. Define atomic number.
• What is proton number?

The number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom of an element is called its atomic number (proton number), represented by Z.

67. Why is the number of electrons equal to the number of protons in an atom?

Because an atom, as a whole, is electrically neutral.

68. Why is atomic number called a unique number for an element?
• How is an element identified?

The number of protons is always fixed for an element and is different from that of every other element, so an element is identified by its atomic number.

69. How are elements arranged in the periodic table?

In ascending order of their atomic numbers.

70. How does an atom of one element differ from an atom of another element?

Each contains the same kinds of fundamental particles, but they contain different numbers of them (different atomic number).

Mass Number, Symbols and N = A − Z

Section 2.2 — mass number, writing symbols and finding neutrons.

Notes
  • The total number of protons and neutrons present in an atom almost accounts for the whole mass of that atom — hence it is called the nucleon number or mass number, symbol A.
  • The mass of an electron is very small, so it is not included in the mass number.
  • An atom may also be identified by its mass number. Example: oxygen has 8 protons (Z = 8) and 16 protons + neutrons (A = 16).
  • Symbol: atomic number is written as a left subscript and mass number as a left superscript:
  A
   X        e.g.   ¹⁶₈O   (oxygen: 8 protons, mass number 16)
  Z                ¹²₆C   (carbon: 6 protons, 6 neutrons)
Quantity Formula
Atomic number Z = number of protons = number of electrons (neutral atom)
Mass number A = protons + neutrons
Number of neutrons N = A − Z

Sample Example (book): Calculate the number of neutrons, protons and electrons in barium ¹³⁷₅₆Ba. - Protons = 56, electrons = 56; N = A − Z = 137 − 56 = 81.

Interesting Information: Changing the number of electrons of an element forms ions, while changing the number of neutrons of an element forms isotopes.

Questions and answers

71. Define mass number.
• What is nucleon number?

The sum of the number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom is called its nucleon number or mass number, represented by A.

72. Why is the mass of electrons not included in the mass number?

Because the mass of an electron is very small compared with protons and neutrons.

73. How are the atomic number and mass number written with the symbol of an element?

The atomic number is written as a left subscript and the mass number as a left superscript, e.g. ¹⁶₈O.

74. Give the atomic number and mass number of oxygen.

Atomic number = 8 (8 protons); mass number = 16 (protons + neutrons). Symbol: ¹⁶₈O.

75. How many protons and neutrons does ¹²₆C have?

6 protons and 6 neutrons.

76. Write the formula for calculating the number of neutrons.

N = A − Z (neutrons = mass number − atomic number).

77. How many neutrons are present in sulphur ³²₁₆S?

N = 32 − 16 = 16 neutrons.

78. Calculate the number of neutrons, protons and electrons in barium ¹³⁷₅₆Ba.

Protons = 56, electrons = 56, neutrons = 137 − 56 = 81.

79. What is formed when the number of electrons of an element is changed?
• What is formed when the number of neutrons of an element is changed?

Changing the number of electrons forms ions; changing the number of neutrons forms isotopes.

Common Mistake: Atomic number (Z) and mass number (A) are not the same. Z counts protons only; A counts protons plus neutrons. Neutrons = A − Z, never Z − A.

Part 10 CHEM-13 CHEM-14 CHEM-15

Isotopes and Their Masses

Section 2.3 — isotopes of hydrogen and carbon.

Notes
  • All atoms of an element have the same atomic number, but their mass number may vary depending on the number of neutrons in the nucleus.
  • Isotopes = atoms of the same element having a different number of neutrons in their nuclei (same number of protons, different number of neutrons).

Carbon — three isotopes (6, 7 and 8 neutrons):

Isotope Protons Neutrons
¹²₆C 6 6
¹³₆C 6 7
¹⁴₆C 6 8

Hydrogen — three isotopes:

Isotope Name Protons Neutrons
¹₁H Hydrogen 1 0
²₁H Deuterium 1 1
³₁H Tritium 1 2
  • Hydrogen (¹₁H) is the only atom that has no neutron.
  • ²₁H has twice the mass of ¹₁H, while ³₁H has three times the mass of ¹₁H. The masses of the three carbon isotopes are also different.
  • Chemical properties of elements are determined by the number of electrons, so all isotopes of an element show almost the same chemical behaviour, although their physical properties may be different.

Figures (book): "Isotopes of Hydrogen Atom" — nuclei labelled P = 1 / P = 1, N = 1 / P = 1, N = 2, each with one electron shell; "Isotopes of Carbon Atom" — nuclei labelled N = 6, P = 6 / N = 7, P = 6 / N = 8, P = 6, with electrons drawn in two shells.

Questions and answers

80. Define isotopes.
• What are isotopes?

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons (same atomic number) but different numbers of neutrons (different mass numbers).

81. Which particle is present in different numbers in isotopes of an element?

The neutron.

82. Name the isotopes of carbon and state the number of neutrons in each.

¹²₆C (6 neutrons), ¹³₆C (7 neutrons) and ¹⁴₆C (8 neutrons); each has 6 protons.

83. Name the three isotopes of hydrogen with their symbols.

Hydrogen ¹₁H, deuterium ²₁H and tritium ³₁H.

84. How many protons and neutrons are present in each isotope of hydrogen?

¹₁H: 1 proton, 0 neutrons; ²₁H: 1 proton, 1 neutron; ³₁H: 1 proton, 2 neutrons.

85. Which atom has no neutron in its nucleus?

Hydrogen (¹₁H).

86. Compare the masses of the three isotopes of hydrogen.

²₁H has twice the mass of ¹₁H, and ³₁H has three times the mass of ¹₁H.

87. Why do all isotopes of an element show almost the same chemical behaviour?
• On what do the chemical properties of elements depend?

Chemical properties of elements are determined by the number of electrons, and all isotopes of an element have the same number of electrons.

88. Which properties of isotopes may be different?

Their physical properties (and mass) may be different.

89. Why are isotopes not given separate places in the periodic table?

All isotopes of an element have the same atomic number, so they occupy the same place.

90. State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in ¹³₆C.

Protons = 6, electrons = 6, neutrons = 13 − 6 = 7.

91. Name the labelled parts shown in the figure of the isotopes of hydrogen.

Nucleus labelled with P (protons) and N (neutrons) — P = 1 in ¹₁H; P = 1, N = 1 in ²₁H; P = 1, N = 2 in ³₁H — surrounded by a shell containing the electron.

Common Mistake: Isotopes differ in the number of neutrons only. They do not differ in protons (that would be a different element) or in electrons (in a neutral atom).

Part 11 CHEM-11 CHEM-17

Radioactive Isotopes and Decay

Section 2.3 — radioactivity and radioactive decay.

Notes
  • The isotopes of the same element do not have the same physical properties. Several isotopes have unstable nuclei; they emit excess energy in the form of radiation.
  • Emission of radiation = radioactivity. An isotope that emits radiation = radioactive isotope.
  • Tritium (³₁H) is a radioactive isotope; the other two isotopes of hydrogen are stable and do not emit any radiation.
  • When a radioactive element emits radiation it is transformed into another chemical element → this is radioactive decay. The new element may be stable, or it may be radioactive and emit radiation too.

Decay examples given in the book

²³⁸₉₂U  ──►  ²³⁴₉₀Th  +  energy       (Uranium → Thorium)
²³⁴₉₀Th ──►  ²³¹₉₁Pa  +  energy       (Thorium → Protactinium)
²¹⁰₈₃Bi ──►  ²⁰⁶₈₁Tl  +  energy       (Bismuth → Thallium)

(Equations are reproduced exactly as printed in the book. In real nuclear physics ²³⁴Th decays to ²³⁴Pa and ²¹⁰Bi to ²⁰⁶Tl via α-decay — the book simplifies; keep the book's form for the exam.)

The proton number changes in each step (92 → 90 → 91; 83 → 81), so the identity of the atom changes.

Interesting Information: Every year, our body replaces about 98% of its atoms.

Questions and answers

92. What is radioactivity?

The emission of excess energy in the form of radiation by unstable nuclei is called radioactivity.

93. What is a radioactive isotope?

An isotope whose nucleus is unstable and which emits radiation is called a radioactive isotope.

94. Give an example of a radioactive isotope of hydrogen.
• Which isotopes of hydrogen are stable?

Tritium (³₁H) is the radioactive isotope of hydrogen; the other two isotopes (¹₁H and ²₁H) are stable and do not emit radiation.

95. What is radioactive decay?

The process in which a radioactive element emits radiation and is transformed into another chemical element is called radioactive decay.

96. Can the element formed by radioactive decay also be radioactive?

Yes. The new element may be stable, or it may be radioactive and emit radiation too.

97. Write the decay of uranium-238 as given in the book.

²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + energy.

98. What does thorium-234 give on further disintegration (as given in the book)?

²³⁴₉₀Th → ²³¹₉₁Pa (protactinium) + energy.

99. Write the decay of bismuth-210 as given in the book.

²¹⁰₈₃Bi → ²⁰⁶₈₁Tl (thallium) + energy.

100. How can radioactivity change the identity of an atom?

When a radioactive atom emits radiation its nucleus changes, so its proton number changes and it becomes an atom of another element (e.g. uranium, Z = 92, becomes thorium, Z = 90).

101. Why do radioactive isotopes emit radiation?

Their nuclei are unstable, so they emit excess energy in the form of radiation.

Part 12 CHEM-18

Applications of Radioactive Isotopes

Section 2.3 — medical imaging, radiocarbon dating, industry and energy.

Notes
  1. Medical imaging / diagnosis — doctors inject the patient with a small amount of radioactive fluid. Technetium-99m is used for diagnostic imaging of organs such as the brain and lungs. A special camera is used to watch how the radioactive fluid moves.
  2. Radiocarbon dating — a method of finding the age of a historical object containing organic material using the radioactive isotope ¹⁴₆C. The proportion of ¹⁴C in a sample from a dead plant or animal (a piece of wood or a bone) is measured to calculate when the animal or plant died. The older the sample, the less ¹⁴C is detected.
  3. Industry and energy — to test the strength of metals and concrete mixtures; to generate cheap nuclear power; to find oil fields.
  4. Medicine (treatment) — to diagnose and treat many medical conditions and diseases, including cancer and thyroid disorders.

Interesting Information: Gallium has many interesting properties. Its melting point is below body temperature, so it is liquid at room temperature. It does not evaporate.

Questions and answers

102. How are radioactive isotopes useful in medical imaging?
• How do doctors diagnose a disease using a radioactive isotope?

Doctors inject the patient with a small amount of radioactive fluid and use a special camera to watch how the fluid moves, which helps to diagnose the disease.

103. Name a radioactive isotope used in diagnostic imaging. Which organs are imaged?

Technetium-99m; it is used for diagnostic imaging across human organs such as the brain and lungs.

104. What is radiocarbon dating?

Radiocarbon dating is a method of finding the age of a historical object containing organic material with the help of the radioactive isotope of carbon, ¹⁴₆C.

105. How is radiocarbon dating carried out?

The proportion of ¹⁴C in a sample from a dead plant or animal (such as wood or bone) is measured; this information is used to calculate when the plant or animal died.

106. Why is less ¹⁴C detected in an older sample?
• How is radiocarbon dating useful for archaeologists?

The older the sample, the less ¹⁴C is left to be detected, so the amount of ¹⁴C tells the age of organic matter.

107. Give uses of radioactive isotopes in industry and energy.

They are used to test the strength of metals and concrete mixtures, to generate cheap nuclear power and to find oil fields.

108. Name two diseases that are diagnosed or treated using radioactive isotopes.

Cancer and thyroid disorders.

109. Why are isotopes important in our daily life?

Radioactive isotopes are used in medical imaging and treatment, radiocarbon dating, testing metals and concrete, nuclear power generation and oil-field exploration.

Part 13 CHEM-19 CHEM-20

Ionization by a Radioactive Source

Section 2.3 — how atoms become cations and anions.

Notes
  • Radiation emitted from a radioactive source causes atoms to ionize. For example, radiation from radium-226 can remove one or more electrons from an atom.
  • The ionizing radiation must have enough energy to remove the tightly bound electron from the orbit of the atom.
  • An electron is lost because the ionizing radiation collides with the atom and forces the electron away.
  • If a sodium atom is hit by ionizing radiation it may lose an electron → the atom becomes a positively charged ion (cation).
Na  ──(energy)──►  Na⁺  +  e⁻
                  (cation)

Note (SLO "anion" and "symbols for ions"): the book shows only the cation (Na⁺). Standard chemistry: an atom that gains electrons becomes a negatively charged ion (anion), e.g. Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻. The charge of an ion is written as a right superscript of the symbol (Na⁺, Cl⁻), while Z and A stay on the left (ᴬ_Z X).

Interesting Information: The same elements occur everywhere in the universe. Matter on Mars or in any other planet consists of the same elements found on Earth.

Questions and answers

110. How does a radioactive source ionize atoms?
• What is meant by ionization of atoms by radiation?

Radiation emitted from a radioactive source collides with an atom and forces an electron away from it; the atom then becomes an ion.

111. Give an example of a radioactive element whose radiation can remove electrons from atoms.

Radium-226.

112. What condition must the ionizing radiation satisfy to remove an electron?

It must have enough energy to remove the tightly bound electron from the orbit of the atom.

113. How does a sodium atom become a cation?
• Write the equation for the ionization of sodium.

When a sodium atom is hit by ionizing radiation it loses an electron and becomes a positively charged ion: Na → Na⁺ + e⁻.

114. What is a cation?

A positively charged ion formed when an atom loses one or more electrons (e.g. Na⁺).

115. What is an anion? How is it formed?

An anion is a negatively charged ion formed when an atom gains one or more electrons (e.g. Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻).

116. How is the charge of an ion written in a symbol?

As a right superscript of the symbol, e.g. Na⁺, Cl⁻; the atomic number and mass number are written on the left.

117. Why does an atom become positively charged when it loses an electron?

After losing an electron the atom has one proton more than the number of electrons, so the positive charge is greater than the negative charge.

Part 14 CHEM-16

Relative Atomic Mass

Section 2.4 — the carbon-12 scale and the atomic mass unit.

Notes
  • Chemists needed a way to compare the masses of different atoms. Without the relative masses of atoms, we would not know in which ratio of masses to mix reactants to carry out a chemical reaction.
  • In 1961 chemists adopted a new scale: the unit mass is 1/12th of the mass of the lighter isotope of carbon (carbon-12), taken as exactly 12. The mass of one carbon atom on this scale is exactly 12 and the masses of other atoms are measured relative to this unit.
  • Relative atomic mass of an element = the mass of an atom of that element relative to the mass of the light isotope of carbon taken as 12.
  • It is expressed in atomic mass unit (amu), defined as one-twelfth the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
1 amu = 1.67377 × 10⁻²⁷ kg
  • Examples: mass of one atom of hydrogen-1 = 1.007 amu; mass of one atom of sulphur-32 = 31.972 amu.

Questions and answers

118. Why is it necessary to know the relative masses of atoms?

Without knowing the relative masses of atoms we would not know in which ratio of masses to mix the reactants to carry out a chemical reaction.

119. Which scale was adopted in 1961 for measuring relative atomic masses?

A scale in which the unit mass is 1/12th of the mass of the lighter isotope of carbon (carbon-12), taken as 12.

120. Define relative atomic mass.

The relative atomic mass of an element is the mass of an atom of that element relative to the mass of the light isotope of carbon taken as 12.

121. Define atomic mass unit (amu).
• In which unit is relative atomic mass expressed?

One atomic mass unit (amu) is one-twelfth the mass of an atom of carbon-12; 1 amu = 1.67377 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.

122. What is the mass of one atom of carbon-12 on the atomic mass scale?

Exactly 12 amu.

123. Give the masses of hydrogen-1 and sulphur-32 on the atomic mass scale.

Hydrogen-1 = 1.007 amu; sulphur-32 = 31.972 amu.

Section 2.4 — the isotopic-abundance formula with worked examples.

Notes

  • Formula:
                      m₁p₁ + m₂p₂ + m₃p₃ + ...

                                 100

Sample Example 1 — Relative atomic mass of krypton

= (80 × 2.0 + 82 × 12.0 + 83 × 12.0 + 84 × 57.0 + 85 × 17.0) / 100

(Check: numerator = 160 + 984 + 996 + 4788 + 1445 = 8373 → 83.73, which the book gives as 83.7.)

Sample Example 2 — Relative atomic mass of the light isotope of chlorine Given: relative atomic mass of chlorine = 35.45; Cl-37 abundance = 24.23%; abundance of the light isotope = 75.77%.

35.45 = (Cl × 75.77 + 37 × 24.23) / 100
3545  = Cl × 75.77 + 37 × 24.23
3545 − 896.51 = Cl × 75.77
2648.49 / 75.77 = Cl
Cl = 34.95

Relative atomic mass of the light isotope of chlorine = 34.95.

Tip: Use the same formula both ways. To find an unknown isotope's mass, put the known relative atomic mass on the left, multiply both sides by 100, subtract the known isotopes' (m × p), then divide by the unknown isotope's abundance.

Common Mistake: Divide by 100 only when the abundances are written as percentages (adding up to 100). Do not forget to multiply each mass by its own abundance before adding.

Questions and answers

124. What are relative isotopic masses?
• What is isotopic abundance?

The mass numbers of the different isotopes of an element are called relative isotopic masses; the naturally occurring percentage of each isotope is its isotopic abundance.

125. State the formula for calculating the relative atomic mass of an element from its isotopes.

Relative atomic mass = (m₁p₁ + m₂p₂ + m₃p₃ + …) / 100, where m = relative isotopic mass and p = isotopic abundance (%).

126. How many isotopes does krypton have in Table 2.2? Name their relative isotopic masses.

Five isotopes, with relative isotopic masses 80, 82, 83, 84 and 85.

127. Calculate the relative atomic mass of krypton from Table 2.2.

(80 × 2.0 + 82 × 12.0 + 83 × 12.0 + 84 × 57.0 + 85 × 17.0) / 100 = 8373 / 100 = 83.7 (83.73 before rounding).

128. Which isotope of krypton is the most abundant?

The isotope with relative isotopic mass 84 (57.0%).

129. Calculate the relative atomic mass of the light isotope of chlorine, if the relative atomic mass of chlorine is 35.45 and Cl-37 has an abundance of 24.23%.

Abundance of the light isotope = 100 − 24.23 = 75.77%. 35.45 = (Cl × 75.77 + 37 × 24.23) / 100 → 3545 − 896.51 = Cl × 75.77 → Cl = 2648.49 / 75.77 = 34.95.

130. What is the abundance of the light isotope of chlorine?

75.77% (the heavier isotope, Cl-37, is 24.23%).

131. Why is the relative atomic mass of an element usually not a whole number?

Because it is the average of the relative masses of all its isotopes weighted by their abundances (e.g. chlorine = 35.45, krypton = 83.7).

Key Points

Chapter Key Points

From the book
  1. An English chemist, John Dalton, provided the evidence for the existence of atoms.
  2. Discharge tube experiments showed that atoms are no longer the smallest particles of matter; rather they are made up of still smaller particles called electron and proton. The neutron was discovered separately.
  3. Electrons, protons and neutrons are shown to be present in all the elements irrespective of the fact that the elements behave very differently. Different elements, however, contain different numbers of these particles.
  4. Lord Rutherford discovered that all atoms have a central part which he named the nucleus. The protons and neutrons are present in this nucleus while the electrons are revolving around the nucleus.
  5. An atom, being electrically neutral, contains the same number of electrons and protons.
  6. According to Bohr's atomic model, the electron revolves around the nucleus in definite circular paths called orbits or shells. As long as an electron revolves in an orbit its energy remains fixed. An electron will have more energy if it is located away from the nucleus.
  7. The number of protons present in the nucleus of an element is called the atomic number of that element.
  8. The total number of protons and neutrons present in the nucleus of an element is called its mass number.
  9. Isotopes are the atoms of the same element which have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
  10. Isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties but they differ in their physical properties.
  11. Isotopes of an element may be stable or radioactive. Radioactive isotopes have many useful applications in medicine.
  12. Radioactive isotopes have unstable nuclei and they throw out radiation.
  13. Relative atomic mass of an element can be calculated from the relative isotopic masses of this element and their isotopic abundances.

Ex 5 (ii) — A system just like our solar system exists in an atom. Comment on this statement.Easy
Question:

A system just like our solar system exists in an atom. Comment on this statement.

In Bohr's model the nucleus is at the centre, like the Sun, and electrons revolve around it in fixed orbits (shells), like planets — so the statement is partly true.
