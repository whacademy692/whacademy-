Part 1 CHEM-01

What is Chemistry?

Section 1.1 — the definition of chemistry, composition, structure and energy.

Notes

Chemistry is the branch of science which deals with the properties, composition and structure of substances. It also deals with the physical and chemical changes in matter and the laws or principles which govern these changes.

  • Composition = finding out the percentages of elements and compounds in a sample of matter.
  • Structure = the arrangement of atoms in matter.
  • Both physical and chemical changes may be brought about by absorption or evolution of energy.
  • Alternative SLO wording: the study of earth (solids), air (gases), sea (liquids) and sky (plasma) and their interaction with each other.

Questions and answers

1. Define chemistry.
• What is chemistry the study of?

Chemistry is the branch of science which deals with the properties, composition and structure of substances, the physical and chemical changes in matter, and the laws which govern these changes.

2. What is meant by the composition of matter?

Composition means finding out the percentages of elements and compounds in a sample of matter.

3. What is meant by the structure of matter?

Structure of matter means the arrangement of atoms in matter.

4. How can physical and chemical changes be brought about?

Both physical and chemical changes may be brought about by the absorption or evolution of energy.

5. Define chemistry in terms of earth, air, sea and sky.

Chemistry is the study of earth (solids), air (gases), sea (liquids) and sky (plasma) and their interaction with each other.

Part 2 CHEM-02

Branches of Chemistry

Section 1.1 — the 11 branches of chemistry and what each one studies.

Notes

Chemistry is a widely spread, complex subject, so it is divided into many distinct branches. Each branch has a distinct area of study on which scientists can focus and achieve breakthroughs and advancements.

# Branch What it studies Key points / uses
1 Physical Chemistry How substances behave at atomic and molecular levels; how physical laws make atoms and molecules show specific characteristics Predicts and changes the rates of reactions; optimises conditions for industrial-scale reactions
2 Inorganic Chemistry Synthesis, composition, properties and structure of elements and compounds containing little or no carbon Metals, non-metals, salts, acids, bases; used as fertilizers, medicines, catalysts, pigments, coatings
3 Organic Chemistry Carbon compounds (hydrocarbons and their derivatives) other than simple salts like carbonates, bicarbonates, oxides, carbides Structure, formation, properties, composition and reactions; found in and essential for all forms of life
4 Environmental Chemistry Chemical and biochemical phenomena of the planet: sources, reactions, effects and fates of chemical species in air, soil and water Understanding causes, effects and solutions of pollution; effects of humans' chemical release
5 Analytical Chemistry Analysis of substances Separation, identification and determination of concentration of components; uses modern sophisticated instruments
6 Biochemistry Life through chemical processes; chemical substances and vital processes in living organisms Structure and function of proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, nucleic acids
7 Nuclear Chemistry Reactions in the nucleus of an atom Radioactivity, nuclear processes, transformation of nuclei; applications in agriculture, medicine, industry, research
8 Polymer Chemistry Properties, structure and synthesis of polymers and macromolecules Polymers = large molecules made by linking building blocks; natural polymers: proteins, cellulose, nucleic acids
9 Geochemistry Chemical composition of Earth, its sources and minerals Minerals exploration; geochemical mapping → environmental monitoring, forestry, medical research
10 Medicinal Chemistry Design and synthesis of medicines/drugs beneficial for mankind Discovery, delivery, absorption and metabolism of drugs in the human body
11 Astrochemistry Molecules and ions occurring in space and interstellar space Abundance and reactions of atoms, molecules, ions in the universe; their interaction with radiation

Interesting Information (book): A geothermal heat pump uses a pump to transfer underground water into buildings during winter to heat them and in summer to cool them.

Questions and answers

6. Why is chemistry divided into many branches?
• Why is there a need to divide chemistry into branches?

Chemistry is a widely spread and complex subject. It is divided into branches to understand it, to concentrate on its specific aspects, and to give scientists distinct areas to focus on and achieve breakthroughs and advancements.

7. Name the branches of chemistry given in the chapter.

Physical, inorganic, organic, environmental, analytical, biochemistry, nuclear, polymer, geochemistry, medicinal chemistry and astrochemistry (11 branches).

8. What does physical chemistry deal with?
• Which branch investigates how substances behave at atomic and molecular levels?

Physical chemistry investigates how substances behave at atomic and molecular levels and how fundamental physical laws make atoms and molecules show specific characteristics.

9. How is physical chemistry useful in industry?

It is used to predict and change the rates of reactions and so to optimize the conditions for carrying out reactions on an industrial scale.

10. Define inorganic chemistry.

Inorganic chemistry is the study of the synthesis, composition, properties and structure of elements and compounds that contain little or no carbon.

11. Give the uses of inorganic compounds.

Inorganic compounds are used as fertilizers, medicines, catalysts, pigments, coatings and much more.

12. Define organic chemistry.
• Which branch deals with hydrocarbons and their derivatives?

Organic chemistry deals with carbon compounds (hydrocarbons and their derivatives) other than simple salts like carbonates, bicarbonates, oxides and carbides. It studies their structure, formation, properties, composition and reactions.

13. Which carbon compounds are not studied in organic chemistry?

Simple salts of carbon such as carbonates, bicarbonates, oxides and carbides.

14. Define environmental chemistry.

Environmental chemistry is the scientific study of the chemical and biochemical phenomena that occur on this planet — the sources, reactions, effects and fates of chemical species in the air, soil and water environments.

15. How does environmental chemistry help us?

It helps in understanding the causes, effects and solutions of different types of pollution and the effects humans have on the environment through the release of chemicals.

16. What is analytical chemistry?

Analytical chemistry deals with the analysis of different substances: separation, identification and determination of the concentration of the components present in material things.

17. How is analysis carried out in analytical chemistry nowadays?

By using modern and sophisticated instruments to analyze the matter.

18. Define biochemistry.

Biochemistry is the branch of chemistry in which we understand life through chemical processes; it is the study of chemical substances and vital processes occurring in living organisms.

19. Name the molecules whose structure and function are studied in biochemistry.

Proteins, carbohydrates, lipids and nucleic acids.

20. What is nuclear chemistry?

Nuclear chemistry deals with the reactions taking place in the nucleus of an atom — radioactivity, nuclear processes and transformation in the nuclei of atoms.

21. Give the fields in which nuclear chemistry is applied.

Agriculture, medicine, industry and research.

22. What are polymers?
• What does polymer chemistry study?

Polymers are large molecules made by linking together a series of building blocks. Polymer chemistry focuses on the properties, structure and synthesis of polymers and macromolecules.

23. Give examples of naturally occurring polymers.

Proteins, cellulose and nucleic acids present in living organisms.

24. Define geochemistry.

The study of the chemical composition of Earth and its sources and minerals is called geochemistry.

25. Give the applications of geochemistry.

Besides minerals exploration, geochemical mapping has applications in environmental monitoring, forestry and medical research.

26. For what is a geothermal heat pump used?

It transfers underground water into buildings to heat them in winter and cool them in summer.

27. What is medicinal chemistry?

In this branch the chemist tries to design and synthesize medicines or drugs beneficial for mankind, including discovery, delivery, absorption and metabolism of drugs in the human body.

28. Define astrochemistry.

Astrochemistry is the study of molecules and ions occurring in space and interstellar space — their abundance, their reactions and their interaction with radiation.

Part 3 CHEM-03 CHEM-04 CHEM-05

States of Matter

Section 1.2 — matter, solid, liquid and gas, plasma, intermediate and exotic states.

Notes

The world is made up of matter and energy. Energy is non-material in nature. Anything other than energy which carries weight (mass) and occupies volume (space) is called matter. A state of matter is one of the many distinct forms in which matter can exist. Four states are seen in everyday life: solid, liquid, gas and plasma. There are more states which are not seen in everyday life.

Three primary states — comparison (Fig 1.1)

The solid, liquid and gaseous states differ because of the strength of intermolecular forces, the arrangement of particles and the distance between the particles.

Property Gas Liquid Solid
Arrangement / distance Molecules very widely apart, no order whatsoever Molecules closely attached but moving randomly Particles closely packed; fixed at their positions
Intermolecular forces Very weak Significant Very strong (interatomic / intermolecular attractions)
Motion of particles Random Random Oscillate about their mean positions
Compressibility Easily compressible Not easily compressible Relatively incompressible (crystalline solids: almost incompressible), rigid
Density Very low Higher than gases Very high (crystalline solids are the most dense)
Shape and volume — — Definite shape, fixed volume
Container — — Only state that needs no container for storage

Note (SLO "fluidity"): the SLO asks for fluidity, but the book text does not discuss it. Standard chemistry: solids are rigid (do not flow); liquids and gases flow (fluids). Confirm with your teacher.

Plasma

  • Composed of particles with very high kinetic energy; not a commonly seen form of matter.
  • Exists in fluorescent tubes, lightning and welding arcs.
  • Can be considered a partially ionized gas containing electrons, ions, photons, etc.
  • Key Point 4: plasma is the fourth state; most of the matter in the rest of the universe exists in this state.

Intermediate states (between liquid–gas or liquid–solid)

State Description Use / example
Supercritical fluid Highly compressed state showing properties of both gases and liquids Reactions not possible in conventional solvents may be done in supercritical carbon dioxide
Liquid crystal Properties between those of conventional liquids and crystalline solids Display devices — computer monitors, clocks, watches, navigation systems
Graphene Two-dimensional crystal; single layer of carbon atoms in a hexagonal pattern Tough, flexible, light material with high resistance (see Fig 1.5)

Exotic states

States of matter that are not commonly encountered: dark matter, Bose–Einstein condensate (BEC), nuclear matter, quantum spin liquid and many others.

Questions and answers

29. Define matter.
• What is matter?

Matter is a substance that has mass (carries weight) and occupies space (volume). Anything other than energy that has weight and occupies volume is matter.

30. Is energy matter? What is the nature of energy?

No. Energy is non-material in nature.

31. What is meant by a state of matter?

A state of matter is one of the many distinct forms in which matter can exist.

32. Name the four states of matter observed in everyday life.

Solid, liquid, gas and plasma.

33. Name the three primary states of matter.

Solid, liquid and gas.

34. Why do solids, liquids and gases differ from each other?

They differ because of the different strength of intermolecular forces, the arrangement of particles and the distance between the particles.

35. Describe the arrangement of molecules in a gas.

Gas molecules are very widely apart with no order whatsoever and have very weak intermolecular forces.

36. Why are gases easily compressible?
• Why do gases have very low densities?

Gas molecules are very widely apart with very weak intermolecular forces, so gases are easily compressible and their densities are very low.

37. Describe the arrangement of molecules in a liquid.

Liquid molecules are closely attached but move randomly, and there are significant intermolecular forces between them.

38. Why are liquids not easily compressible?
• How does the density of liquids compare with that of gases?

Because of the significant intermolecular forces and closely attached molecules, liquids are not easily compressible. Their densities are higher than those of gases.

39. State the shape and volume of solids.

Solids have a definite shape and a fixed volume.

40. How do the particles of a solid move?

The particles remain fixed at their positions but can oscillate about their mean positions.

41. Why are solids relatively incompressible and rigid?

Their particles are closely packed with very strong interatomic or intermolecular attractions.

42. Which state of matter does not need a container for storage?

The solid state.

43. Which substances are the most dense? Why?

Crystalline solids. Their particles are perfectly arranged and strongly bonded, which makes them almost incompressible.

44. State the distinguishing macroscopic properties of solids, liquids and gases (density, compressibility).

Gases — very low density, easily compressible. Liquids — higher density than gases, not easily compressible. Solids — very high density, relatively incompressible and rigid. (See fluidity note above.)

45. What is plasma?
• Why is plasma called a partially ionized gas?

Plasma is a form of matter composed of particles with very high kinetic energy. It can be considered a partially ionized gas containing electrons, ions, photons, etc.

46. Where does plasma exist?
• In which state does most of the matter in the universe exist?

Plasma exists in fluorescent tubes, lightning and welding arcs. Most of the matter in the rest of the universe exists in the plasma state.

47. What are intermediate states of matter? Give examples.

They are states where liquid meets gas or liquid meets solid, e.g. supercritical fluids, liquid crystals and graphene.

48. What are supercritical fluids?

Supercritical fluids are highly compressed states of matter which show the properties of both gases and liquids.

49. What is the use of supercritical carbon dioxide?

Chemical reactions which cannot be carried out in conventional solvents may possibly be carried out in supercritical carbon dioxide.

50. What is a liquid crystal?
• Give the uses of liquid crystals.

A liquid crystal is a state of matter whose properties are between those of conventional liquids and crystalline solids. It is used in display devices such as computer monitors, clocks, watches and navigation systems.

51. What is graphene?

Graphene is a two-dimensional crystal — a single layer of carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal pattern.

52. State the properties of graphene.

Graphene is a tough, flexible and light material (the book adds "with a high resistance").

53. What are exotic states of matter? Give examples.

States of matter that are not commonly encountered are called exotic states, e.g. dark matter, Bose–Einstein condensate, nuclear matter and quantum spin liquid.

54. Name the three states of matter shown in Fig (1.1).

Solid, liquid and gas.

Part 4 CHEM-07

Element, Compound and Mixture

Section 1.3 — what elements, compounds and mixtures are.

Notes

Matter exists as elements, compounds and mixtures.

Element — the simplest form of matter; a pure substance containing the same kind of atoms. It cannot be broken down into simpler substances by ordinary chemical reactions. Elements exist in all three forms (solid, liquid, gas); most are solids; liquid and gaseous elements are very few (Fig 1.2: iron rods, copper wire, zinc screws and nuts). - Kinds: metal, non-metal, metalloid, noble gas. Forms: atoms, molecules, ions, isotopes. - Examples: sodium, potassium, magnesium, calcium, carbon, silicon, nitrogen, oxygen, chlorine, helium, copper, gold, zinc, silver, nickel, cobalt, mercury, bromine, iodine.

Compound — also a pure substance; made of two or more different elements combined in a fixed ratio. Elements react and form chemical bonds that are not easy to break (Fig 1.3: iron sulphide, copper chloride). - Kinds: molecular, ionic, intermetallic, coordination complexes; also inorganic or organic. - Examples: water, ammonia, methane, carbon dioxide, sodium carbonate, potassium chloride, starch, proteins, carbohydrates, mineral acids, organic acids. - Composition and properties of an element or compound are uniform throughout a sample and from one sample to another.

Mixture — formed when more than one type of element or compound is mixed together in any ratio. Examples: air, soil, milk, tap water. - Homogeneous — uniform concentration (salt solution in water). - Heterogeneous — concentration of constituents differs in different parts (rock, made of granite, mica, limestone; Fig 1.4 shows rock and chocolate).

Interesting Information (book): Many elements are found in nature but some are artificial. Technetium was the first element created by scientists in the laboratory.

Interesting Information (book, §1.7): Mixtures are closely related to our everyday lives. The air we breathe, the foods we consume, the fluids in our body and solids like steel are all homogeneous or heterogeneous mixtures.

Questions and answers

55. Define an element.
• Why is an element called the simplest form of matter?

An element is the simplest form of matter — a pure substance containing the same kind of atoms. It cannot be broken down into simpler substances by ordinary chemical reactions.

56. In which physical states do elements exist?

Elements exist in all three states — solid, liquid and gas. Most elements are solids; liquid and gaseous elements are very few.

57. Name the kinds of elements and the forms in which elements can exist.

Elements may be metals, non-metals, metalloids or noble gases, and can exist in the form of atoms, molecules, ions and isotopes.

58. Give five examples of elements.

Sodium, calcium, carbon, oxygen and copper (also potassium, magnesium, silicon, nitrogen, chlorine, helium, gold, zinc, silver, nickel, cobalt, mercury, bromine, iodine).

59. Which element was the first to be created artificially in a laboratory?

Technetium.

60. Define a compound.

A compound is a pure substance made up of two or more different elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio.

61. Name the types of compounds.

Compounds may be molecular, ionic, intermetallic and coordination complexes; they may also be inorganic or organic in nature.

62. Give five examples of compounds.

Water, ammonia, methane, carbon dioxide and sodium carbonate (also potassium chloride, starch, proteins, carbohydrates, mineral and organic acids).

63. Define a mixture.

A mixture is formed when more than one type of element or compound is mixed together in any ratio.

64. What are homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures? Give an example of each.

A homogeneous mixture has uniform concentration throughout (e.g. a solution of salt in water); a heterogeneous mixture has different concentrations of its constituents in different parts (e.g. a sample of rock).

65. Why is a rock a heterogeneous mixture?

Because the concentration of its constituents is different in different parts; rocks are made of different minerals such as granite, mica and limestone.

66. Give four everyday examples of mixtures.

Air, soil, milk and tap water (also foods, body fluids and steel).

67. Name the substances shown in Figs (1.2), (1.3) and (1.4) as elements, compounds and mixtures.

Elements — iron, copper, zinc; compounds — iron sulphide, copper chloride; mixtures — rock, chocolate.

Allotropic Forms of Substances

Section 1.4 — diamond, graphite, fullerene, sulphur and oxygen.

Notes

Elements may exist in more than one structural form with quite different physical and chemical properties. These forms are called allotropic forms and the phenomenon is called allotropy.

Element Allotropic forms Structure / properties (from the book)
Oxygen Oxygen (O₂) and ozone (O₃) Two allotropic forms
Carbon Diamond Giant macromolecular structure
Graphite Layered structure of hexagonal rings of carbon
Buckminster fullerene (C₆₀) Spheres of carbon atoms arranged in pentagons and hexagons; cage-like; molecule not charged, no boundaries, no unpaired electrons; covalent → soluble in organic solvents; stable at high temperature and pressure; low melting point, soft, does not conduct electricity
(Graphene — single hexagonal layer, shown in Fig 1.5) See §1.2
Sulphur Rhombic and monoclinic Two crystalline forms; rhombic is more stable than monoclinic

Note: Fig (1.5) shows graphene, diamond, Buckminsterfullerene, graphite, rhombic sulphur and monoclinic sulphur. (The book text spells it "monocline"; the figure label says "Monoclimic".)

Questions and answers

68. Define allotropy.
• What are allotropic forms?

The existence of an element in more than one structural form, with different physical and chemical properties, is called allotropy; these forms are called allotropic forms.

69. Name the allotropic forms of oxygen.

Oxygen (O₂) and ozone (O₃).

70. Name the three main allotropic forms of carbon.

Diamond, graphite and Buckminster fullerene (graphene is also shown in Fig 1.5).

71. What is the structure of diamond?

Diamond has a giant macromolecular structure.

72. What is the structure of graphite?

Graphite has a layered structure of hexagonal rings of carbon.

73. What is Buckminster fullerene? Write its formula.

Buckminster fullerene (C₆₀) consists of spheres made of carbon atoms arranged in pentagons and hexagons, giving a cage-like structure.

74. State the properties of fullerene C₆₀.

It is stable at high temperatures and pressures; molecule is not charged, has no boundaries and no unpaired electrons; it has a low melting point, is soft and cannot conduct electricity.

75. Why are fullerenes soluble in organic solvents?

Because fullerenes are covalent in nature.

76. Name the allotropic forms of sulphur. Which is more stable?

Rhombic and monoclinic sulphur; the rhombic form is more stable than the monoclinic.

77. Name the allotropes of carbon and sulphur shown in Fig (1.5).

Carbon — graphene, diamond, Buckminsterfullerene, graphite; sulphur — rhombic and monoclinic.

Part 6 CHEM-07

Elements vs Compounds vs Mixtures

Section 1.5 — how the three kinds of matter differ.

Notes
Elements Compounds Mixtures
Simplest form of matter; a pure substance containing the same kind of atoms A pure substance formed by the chemical combination of two or more atoms of different elements A sample of matter having more than one type of element or compound mixed together in any ratio (the book loosely calls it "an impure compound")
Cannot be broken down into simpler particles by ordinary chemical reactions Atoms combine in a fixed ratio by weight — water (H₂O): hydrogen and oxygen = 1 : 8 by weight Each component retains its identity and specific properties
As atoms, represented by a symbol: sodium Na, calcium Ca Can be broken into constituent elements by a chemical reaction — ammonia → nitrogen and hydrogen May be homogeneous or heterogeneous: salt solution = homogeneous; rock = heterogeneous
Gaseous elements exist as independent molecules: nitrogen (N₂), oxygen (O₂), chlorine (Cl₂); noble gases are mono-atomic: helium (He), argon (Ar) Properties are always different from the elements they are formed from — water differs from hydrogen and oxygen Components are not chemically bound and can be separated by physical methods
Exist as molecules (HCl, NH₃, H₂O) or network arrangements (ionic NaCl; covalent sand SiO₂) Properties = sum of the properties of its components

Common Mistake: Do not write that a mixture is "formed by chemical combination". Its components are not chemically bound and keep their own properties.

Questions and answers

78. Differentiate between an element and a compound.

An element is a pure substance of the same kind of atoms and cannot be broken into simpler particles by ordinary chemical reactions; a compound is a pure substance of different elements combined in a fixed ratio and can be broken into its elements by a chemical reaction.

79. Differentiate between a compound and a mixture.

In a compound the elements are chemically combined in a fixed ratio and its properties differ from those of its elements. A mixture's components are not chemically bound, can be in any ratio, keep their own properties and can be separated by physical methods.

80. Differentiate between an element and a mixture.

An element is a pure substance of one kind of atoms; a mixture contains more than one element or compound mixed in any ratio, and may be homogeneous or heterogeneous.

81. In what ratio by weight are hydrogen and oxygen present in water?

1 : 8 by weight.

82. How is an element represented when it exists in the form of atoms? Give examples.

By a symbol, e.g. sodium is Na and calcium is Ca.

83. In what form do gaseous elements and noble gases exist? Give examples.

Gaseous elements exist as independent molecules, e.g. nitrogen (N₂), oxygen (O₂), chlorine (Cl₂). Noble gases exist as mono-atomic molecules, e.g. helium (He) and argon (Ar).

84. Can a compound be broken into its elements? Give an example.

Yes, by a chemical reaction; e.g. ammonia can be converted back to nitrogen and hydrogen by a suitable chemical reaction.

85. How do the properties of a compound compare with those of its elements?

They are always different, e.g. the properties of water are different from those of hydrogen and oxygen.

86. In what forms do compounds exist? Give examples.

As molecules — hydrogen chloride (HCl), ammonia (NH₃), water (H₂O) — or as network arrangements of atoms — ionic NaCl and covalent sand (SiO₂).

87. How can the components of a mixture be separated?

They are not chemically bound together, so they can be separated by physical methods.

88. What are the properties of a mixture?

Each component keeps its identity and specific properties; the properties of a mixture are the sum of those of its components.

Questions and answers

89. Define a true solution.

A solution is a mixture in which solute particles are completely homogenized in the solvent, e.g. sodium chloride or copper sulphate dissolved in water. The particles cannot be seen and pass through filter paper leaving no residue.

90. What happens when a true solution is filtered?

The solute particles pass through the pores of the filter paper, leaving no residue.

91. What is a suspension? Give an example.

A suspension is a mixture in which solute particles do not dissolve in the solvent and can be seen; e.g. chalk in water.

92. How does a suspension behave on standing and on filtration?

On standing, its particles settle down. On filtration, they cannot pass through the pores of the filter paper and are collected as a residue.

93. What is a colloidal solution? Give two examples.

A colloidal solution is a solution whose solute particles do not homogenize with the solvent; they are a little bigger than particles of a true solution but too small to be seen with the naked eye. Examples: starch solution and white of an egg.

94. How does a colloidal solution behave on standing and on filtration?

Its particles do not settle down on standing and pass through the filter paper like those of a true solution.

95. Differentiate between a true solution, a colloidal solution and a suspension.

True solution — particles completely dissolved, invisible, pass through filter paper; colloid — particles slightly larger, invisible, do not settle, pass through filter paper; suspension — particles do not dissolve, visible, settle down, remain as residue on filter paper.

96. Which beakers in Fig (1.6) show a solution, colloidal solution and suspension?

From left to right: solution, colloidal solution, suspension.

Part 8 CHEM-09

Unsaturated and Saturated Solutions

Section 1.7 — how a solution becomes saturated; solubility.

Notes

Activity: take about 100 g of water in a beaker, add 5 g of table sugar and stir — it dissolves; add another 5 g — it also dissolves. This solution is unsaturated. Keep adding sugar: dissolving becomes more difficult until a stage comes when no more sugar dissolves at that temperature; extra sugar settles at the bottom. The solution is now saturated at that temperature.

  • Unsaturated solution: a solution which can dissolve more solute at a particular temperature.
  • Saturated solution: a solution in which the maximum amount of solute has been dissolved in a particular amount of solvent at a particular temperature.
  • Different solutes have different solubilities in a given solvent: at 20 °C, 36 g of NaCl dissolve in 100 g of water to give a saturated solution, whereas 203.9 g of table sugar dissolve in 100 g of water. The book explains: sugar molecules are larger than salt ions, so more water molecules can surround a single sugar molecule, causing it to dissolve in larger amounts.

Questions and answers

97. Define an unsaturated solution.

A solution which can dissolve more solute at a particular temperature is called an unsaturated solution.

98. Define a saturated solution.

A solution in which the maximum amount of solute has been dissolved in a particular amount of solvent at a particular temperature is called a saturated solution.

99. Describe how an unsaturated solution of sugar becomes saturated.

When more sugar is added to 100 g of water (5 g at a time), it keeps dissolving (unsaturated) until a stage comes when no more sugar dissolves at that temperature; this is the saturated solution.

100. What happens to extra sugar added to a saturated solution?

It does not dissolve and settles at the bottom of the beaker.

101. How much NaCl dissolves in 100 g of water at 20 °C to give a saturated solution?

36 g.

102. How much table sugar dissolves in 100 g of water at 20 °C to form a saturated solution?

203.9 g.

103. Why is the solubility of sugar in water far greater than that of sodium chloride at 20 °C?

Sugar molecules are larger than salt ions, so more water molecules can surround a single sugar molecule, causing it to dissolve in larger amounts.

Part 9 CHEM-09

Temperature and Solubility

Section 1.8 — how temperature changes the solubility of solids and gases.

Notes

Solubility of a solute = the amount of solute which can dissolve in 100 g of a solvent at a particular temperature. Usually solubility increases with temperature, but this is not a general rule.

Effect of temperature rise Examples
Solubility increases Potassium nitrate (KNO₃), silver nitrate (AgNO₃), potassium chloride (KCl), copper sulphate (CuSO₄), sodium nitrate (NaNO₃)
Solubility does not increase appreciably Sodium chloride (NaCl)
Solubility decreases Lithium carbonate (Li₂CO₃), calcium chromate (CaCrO₄), calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂); gases in water

Fig (1.6) Solubility curves (second figure): left graph plots grams of solute per 100 g H₂O against temperature (10–100 °C) for NaNO₃, KNO₃, NH₄Cl, NaCl, KClO₃, NH₃ and SO₂; right graph is the solubility curve of KNO₃ in water with regions labelled unsaturated, saturated (on the curve) and supersaturated. NaCl's line is almost flat; KNO₃'s curve rises steeply; NH₃ and SO₂ (gases) fall.

Activity: prepare a saturated sugar solution in 100 g of water at room temperature, heat it on a spirit lamp and add a little more sugar — it dissolves, showing that solubility of sugar increases on heating.

Interesting Information (book): 1. The increase in solubility of solids with temperature can be used to purify them; pure solids commonly appear as beautifully shaped crystals (e.g. crystals of potassium nitrate). 2. The solubility of gases generally decreases with temperature. CO₂ is more soluble in water at low temperature, so soda-water bottles are stored in the refrigerator to keep CO₂ dissolved longer.

Questions and answers

104. Define solubility.
• What is meant by the solubility of a solute?

The solubility of a solute is the amount of solute which can dissolve in 100 g of a solvent at a particular temperature.

105. How does temperature usually affect the solubility of solids in water? Is this a general rule?

Usually solubility increases with temperature, but this cannot be taken as a general rule.

106. Name compounds whose solubility in water increases with temperature.

Potassium nitrate (KNO₃), silver nitrate (AgNO₃), potassium chloride (KCl), copper sulphate and sodium nitrate.

107. How does temperature affect the solubility of sodium chloride in water?

Its solubility does not increase appreciably with the increase in temperature.

108. Name compounds whose solubility decreases with the increase in temperature.

Lithium carbonate (Li₂CO₃), calcium chromate (CaCrO₄) and calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂).

109. How does temperature affect the solubility of gases in water?

The solubility of gases in water decreases with the increase in temperature.

110. Why are soda-water bottles kept in a refrigerator?

Carbon dioxide is more soluble in water at low temperature, so cooling keeps the gas dissolved for a longer period of time.

111. How is the increase of solubility with temperature used to purify solids?

A solid dissolves more in hot water and separates out as pure, beautifully shaped crystals on cooling (e.g. potassium nitrate crystals).

112. What do you observe when a saturated sugar solution is heated and a little more sugar is added?

The extra sugar dissolves, showing that the solubility of sugar increases with temperature.

113. What do the regions of the KNO₃ solubility curve in Fig (1.6) represent?

Points on the curve represent saturated solutions; the region below the curve is unsaturated; the region above is labelled supersaturated.

114. Name the substances whose solubility curves are shown in Fig (1.6).

NaNO₃, KNO₃, NH₄Cl, NaCl, KClO₃, NH₃ and SO₂ (as labelled in the figure).

Key Points

Chapter Key Points

From the book
  1. Chemistry is that branch of science which deals with the composition of matter, changes in matter and the laws which govern these changes.
  2. To understand the vast and complex subject of chemistry, it is divided into many branches; physical, inorganic and organic chemistry are its main branches among many others.
  3. Matter exists mainly in three states — solid, liquid and gas — which differ because of the different distances between their particles.
  4. Plasma is regarded as the fourth state of matter, not normally observed in this world; most of the matter in the rest of the universe exists in this state.
  5. Matter also exists in intermediate states on the borderline of two principal states (liquid–gas, liquid–solid), e.g. supercritical fluids and liquid crystals.
  6. Matter exists in the form of distinct entities called elements, compounds and mixtures.
  7. Elements, compounds and mixtures have distinct properties individually and are very different from one another.
  8. Solutions, suspensions and colloidal solutions are different forms in which mixtures usually exist, each with its own characteristic properties.
  9. Different solutes have different solubilities in a particular solvent.
  10. The solubility of a solute is the amount of solute which can dissolve in 100 g of a solvent at a particular temperature.
  11. Change in temperature has different effects on the solubility of different compounds.
