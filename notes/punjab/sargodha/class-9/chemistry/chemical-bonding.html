Part 1 CHEM-01

Why Atoms Form Bonds

Section 3.1 — duplet and octet rule, noble gases and the stability of atoms.

Notes
  • Atoms have a tendency to decrease their energy. They do this by combining with other atoms; it is a natural phenomenon because it increases the stability of atoms.
  • Early chemists found the answer when the noble gases He, Ne, Ar, Kr and Xe were discovered. Helium has 2 electrons in its outermost shell, while all other noble gases have 8. Noble gases neither combine with themselves nor with other atoms — the probable reason for this lack of reactivity is their stability, due to 2 electrons (He) or 8 electrons (others) in the outermost shell.
  • This gave rise to the principle: 2 electrons (for H and He, which have only the first shell) or 8 electrons in the outermost shell means stability and hence unreactivity. It was named the Duplet or Octet Rule.
  • Atoms form bonds because they would like to lower their energy by completing their duplet or octet.
  • Sodium: it is easier to lose one electron and stabilise itself than to gain seven electrons. Sodium therefore adopts the energetically easier path and loses its electron to form a bond.
  • Hydrogen: it is favourable for H to lose one electron to become a proton (H⁺) or to gain one electron to become a hydride ion (H⁻). In the latter case it completes its duplet.
  • Alkali and alkaline earth metals are expected to be electropositive metals which form bonds with electronegative elements like oxygen and chlorine.
  • Although the octet rule played a significant role in understanding the nature of a chemical bond at the beginning, further investigations found it to be less important.
Rule Applies to Electrons in outermost shell Result
Duplet H, He (only the first shell) 2 Stable, unreactive
Octet All other noble gases (and atoms trying to become like them) 8 Stable, unreactive

Note (book box "Important Information!"): The arrangement of electrons around the nucleus of an atom in shells and sub-shells is called electronic configuration.

Questions and answers

1. Why do atoms combine with other atoms?
• Why do atoms form chemical bonds?

Atoms have a tendency to decrease their energy. Combining with other atoms lowers the energy and increases the stability of the atoms.

2. Name the noble gases whose discovery helped to explain the stability of atoms.

Helium (He), neon (Ne), argon (Ar), krypton (Kr) and xenon (Xe).

3. How many electrons are present in the outermost shell of helium and of the other noble gases?

Helium has two electrons in its outermost shell; all the other noble gases have eight electrons in their outermost shells.

4. Why are noble gases unreactive?
• Why do noble gas atoms not combine with themselves or with other atoms?

Noble gases are stable because of two electrons in the outermost shell of helium and eight electrons in the outermost shell of the rest. This stability makes them unreactive.

5. State the duplet rule.

For hydrogen and helium, which have only the first shell, having two electrons in the outermost shell means stability and hence unreactivity.

6. State the octet rule.

Having eight electrons in the outermost shell means stability and hence unreactivity. (The book names the principle the "Duplet or Octet Rule".)

7. Why does a sodium atom lose one electron instead of gaining seven electrons?

For sodium it is easier to lose one electron than to gain seven. Losing one electron is the energetically easier path to a stable octet, so sodium loses its electron to form a bond.

8. In what two ways can a hydrogen atom become stable?

It can lose one electron to become a proton (H⁺), or gain one electron to become a hydride ion (H⁻), which completes its duplet.

9. What is a hydride ion?

The hydride ion (H⁻) is formed when a hydrogen atom gains one electron; in this way it completes its duplet.

10. Which metals are expected to be electropositive, and with which elements do they form bonds?

Alkali and alkaline earth metals are expected to be electropositive; they form bonds with electronegative elements such as oxygen and chlorine.

11. What did later investigations show about the importance of the octet rule?

Although the octet rule played a significant role in the beginning in understanding the nature of a chemical bond, further investigations found it to be less important.

12. Define electronic configuration.

The arrangement of electrons around the nucleus of an atom in shells and sub-shells is called electronic configuration.

Part 2 CHEM-04

The Chemical Bond

Section 3.2 — what a chemical bond is and the three bond types of the chapter.

Notes
  • A chemical bond is a force of attraction between atoms which holds them together in the form of a molecule or a compound.
  • When atoms of different substances approach each other there are two possibilities: they may attract or repel. If the forces of attraction dominate the forces of repulsion, the energy of the system is lowered and the two atoms react to form a new molecule. Otherwise the atoms simply move away from each other.
  • Types of bonds considered here: (1) Ionic bond (2) Covalent bond (3) Coordinate covalent bond. The metallic bond is taken separately in section 3.3.
Bond One-line definition (SLO wording)
Ionic Strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions
Covalent Strong electrostatic attraction between shared electrons and two nuclei
Coordinate covalent A covalent bond whose shared pair is donated by one atom only
Metallic Strong electrostatic attraction between a cloud/sea of delocalised electrons and positively charged cations

Questions and answers

13. Define chemical bond.
• What is a chemical bond?

A chemical bond is a force of attraction between atoms which holds them together in the form of a molecule or a compound.

14. What happens when atoms of different substances approach each other?

They may attract or repel each other. If attraction dominates repulsion, the energy of the system is lowered and the atoms react to form a new molecule; otherwise they move away from each other.

15. Name the three types of bonds discussed in section 3.2.

(1) Ionic bond, (2) covalent bond and (3) coordinate covalent bond. (The metallic bond is discussed in section 3.3.)

16. Define ionic bond.

An ionic bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions; it is formed by the complete transference of electron(s) from one atom to another.

17. Define covalent bond.

A covalent bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between shared electrons and the two nuclei; it is formed by the mutual sharing of an electron pair provided by the bonded atoms.

18. Define coordinate covalent bond.

A coordinate covalent bond is a type of covalent bond in which the shared electron pair is donated by one atom only.

19. Define metallic bond.

A metallic bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between a cloud (sea) of delocalised electrons and positively charged cations; the positive ions are bound together by the mobile electrons.

The Ionic Bond

Section 3.2.1 — transfer of electrons, cations, anions and the crystal lattice.

Notes
  • An ionic bond forms from the tendency of atoms to lose or gain electron(s) to acquire the electronic configuration of the nearest noble gas, which is a more stable electronic structure. Example: sodium chloride.
  • Fig 3.1 — electronic configurations:
Atom 1st shell 2nd shell 3rd shell
₁₁Na 2 8 1
₁₇Cl 2 8 7
  • An electron from the outermost shell of sodium is transferred to the outermost shell of chlorine; both atoms now have the configuration of their nearest noble gas (Fig 3.2).
Na            →  Na⁺ + e⁻
e⁻ + Cl       →  Cl⁻
-----------------------------
Na + Cl       →  Na⁺Cl⁻

Fig 3.2 (prose): a sodium atom (Na) passes one electron to a chlorine atom (Cl); the result is a sodium ion (Na⁺) and a chloride ion (Cl⁻) with 8 electrons in its outer shell. In dot-and-cross form the Cl⁻ ion shows eight electrons around Cl (its own seven plus the one electron from Na, drawn as a cross), and Na⁺ shows none in its outer shell.

  • Sodium also reacts with fluorine and bromine to give sodium fluoride and sodium bromide.
  • Electrons that take part in a chemical reaction come only from the outermost shells of the atoms.
  • NaCl contains positively charged sodium ions (Na⁺) and negatively charged chloride ions (Cl⁻), held together by the electrostatic force of attraction. This bond is called an ionic or electrovalent bond; compounds with such a bond are ionic compounds.
  • Calcium (alkaline earth metal) loses two electrons to form calcium chloride (CaCl₂) — Fig 3.3:
Ca              →  Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻
2Cl + 2e⁻       →  2Cl⁻

Fig 3.3 (prose): one Ca atom gives one electron to each of two Cl atoms; the Ca²⁺ ion sits between two Cl⁻ ions, each Cl⁻ having a complete outer shell of eight electrons.

  • These ions surround each other three-dimensionally to form a crystal lattice (Fig 3.4 shows the crystal lattices of NaCl and CaCl₂).
  • Examples of ionic compounds: KCl, MgF₂, NaF, KBr, CaF₂.
  • Complete definition: an ionic bond is a bond formed by the complete transference of electron(s) from one atom to another.
Cation Anion
Formed by Loss of electron(s) Gain of electron(s)
Charge Positive Negative
Example Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ ; Ca → Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻ Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻
Usually formed by Metals (electropositive) Non-metals (electronegative)

⛔ Common Mistake: Writing that "electrons come from the whole atom". Only the outermost-shell electrons are transferred or shared in a chemical reaction.

Questions and answers

20. How is an ionic bond formed?
• Explain the formation of an ionic bond.

Atoms lose or gain electron(s) to acquire the electronic configuration of the nearest noble gas; the oppositely charged ions so formed are held together by electrostatic attraction, which is the ionic bond.

21. Write the electronic configurations of sodium and chlorine.

Sodium (₁₁Na): 2, 8, 1. Chlorine (₁₇Cl): 2, 8, 7 (1st, 2nd and 3rd shell respectively).

22. Explain the formation of sodium chloride.
• How do sodium and chlorine atoms form NaCl?

An electron from the outermost shell of sodium is transferred to the outermost shell of chlorine. Both atoms acquire the configuration of their nearest noble gas, forming Na⁺ and Cl⁻, which are held together by electrostatic attraction.

23. Write the equations for the formation of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ ; e⁻ + Cl → Cl⁻ ; overall Na + Cl → Na⁺Cl⁻.

24. From which shell do the electrons that take part in a chemical reaction come?

Only from the outermost shells of the atoms.

25. What holds the sodium ions and chloride ions together in sodium chloride?

The electrostatic force of attraction between the oppositely charged Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.

26. What is the other name of an ionic bond?
• What is an electrovalent bond?

An ionic bond is also called an electrovalent bond.

27. What are ionic compounds?

Compounds that contain an ionic (electrovalent) bond are called ionic compounds, e.g. NaCl.

28. Name the compounds formed when sodium reacts with fluorine and with bromine.

Sodium fluoride (NaF) and sodium bromide (NaBr).

29. Explain the formation of calcium chloride.

A calcium atom loses two electrons to form Ca²⁺; each of two chlorine atoms gains one electron to form 2Cl⁻. The Ca²⁺ and two Cl⁻ ions are held by electrostatic attraction as CaCl₂.

30. Write the equations for the formation of CaCl₂.

Ca → Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻ ; 2Cl + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻.

31. What is a crystal lattice?
• How do the ions of an ionic compound arrange themselves?

The oppositely charged ions surround each other three-dimensionally; this arrangement of ions is called a crystal lattice.

32. Give examples of ionic compounds.

KCl, MgF₂, NaF, KBr and CaF₂ (also NaCl and CaCl₂).

33. Give the complete definition of an ionic bond in terms of electron transfer.

An ionic bond is a bond formed by the complete transference of electron or electrons from one atom to another.

34. Compare the formation of a cation and an anion.

A cation is formed when an atom loses electron(s) and becomes positively charged (Na → Na⁺ + e⁻); an anion is formed when an atom gains electron(s) and becomes negatively charged (Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻).

Part 4 CHEM-04 CHEM-12

The Covalent Bond

Section 3.2.2 — sharing of electrons; single, double and triple bonds; polar and non-polar.

Notes
  • In an ionic bond atoms lower their energy by transferring electron(s) to reach the configuration of the nearest noble gas. That is not the only way: some atoms lower their energy by mutually sharing their electrons.
  • When two atoms approach to form a bond, important energy changes take place:
  • The electrons of one atom come under the attractive influence of the nucleus of the other atom — a new force of attraction that lowers the energy.
  • The electrons and nucleus of one atom also repel the electrons and nucleus of the other — a force of repulsion that increases the energy.
  • The atoms bring themselves to a distance at which attraction dominates repulsion. The total energy at this distance is minimum, so a stable molecule is formed.
  • Covalent bond (book's bold definition): a bond formed by the mutual sharing of an electron pair provided by the bonded atoms. This is a single covalent bond.
  • In some compounds the atoms share two electrons each → double covalent bond. Atoms can share three electrons each → triple covalent bond. Double and triple bonds have two and three electron pairs respectively, mutually shared between the two atoms.
Bond Electron pairs shared Drawn as
Single 1 one line ( — )
Double 2 two lines ( = )
Triple 3 three lines ( ≡ )
  • Mutually shared electrons may be shown by a dot (•) or a cross (×) — a dot for one atom's electron and a cross for the other atom's.

Fig 3.5 — formation of single, double and triple covalent bonds (prose + summary)

Molecule Bond between the atoms Structural formula Pairs shared
H₂ single H — H 1
Cl₂ single Cl — Cl 1
O₂ double O = O 2
N₂ triple N ≡ N 3

Fig 3.5 (prose): two H atoms overlap and share one electron pair (H •× H); two Cl atoms share one pair, each Cl keeping its other electrons as non-bonding pairs; two O atoms share two pairs; two N atoms share three pairs.

Formation of Covalent Compounds (Fig 3.6)

Compound How the atoms share electrons (book) Structural formula
Water H₂O Two H atoms share their electrons separately with electrons of one O atom H — O — H (O has two lone pairs)
Carbon dioxide CO₂ One C atom shares its four electrons with two O atoms; each O also shares two electrons O = C = O
Ammonia NH₃ N shares three electrons, one with each H H — N — H with the third H below (N has one lone pair)
Hydrogen cyanide HCN H shares with C; C shares three pairs with N H — C ≡ N
Methane CH₄ C shares its four electrons, one with each of four H atoms H—C(—H)(—H)—H (four C–H bonds)
Ethene C₂H₄ Each C shares two electrons with the other C and one with each of two H atoms H₂C = CH₂ (one C=C, four C–H)
Methanol CH₃OH C bonds with three H atoms and with O; O bonds with one H H₃C — O — H (O has two lone pairs)

(Lone-pair counts are read from the dot-and-cross figures of Fig 3.5–3.6.)

  • After mutually sharing their electrons, the bonded atoms acquire the electronic configuration of the nearest noble gas.

Polar and Non-polar Covalent Bonds

  • Covalent bond between two identical atoms → the shared pair lies in the middle of the two atoms → a pure covalent bond, essentially non-polar. Examples: H₂ and Cl₂.
  • Covalent bond between two non-identical atoms → the shared pair bends towards the atom with more electronegativity. A partial negative charge (δ⁻) forms on the more electronegative atom and an equal partial positive charge (δ⁺) on the less electronegative atom → a polar covalent bond.
  • HCl: the shared pair bends towards the more electronegative chlorine, giving it a partial negative charge → H^δ+ ×• Cl^δ−. The covalent bonds in water are also polar covalent (δ⁺ on each H, δ⁻ on O).
Non-polar covalent bond Polar covalent bond
Between Two identical atoms Two non-identical atoms
Shared pair In the middle Bent towards the more electronegative atom
Partial charges None δ⁻ on more electronegative atom, δ⁺ on the other
Examples H₂, Cl₂ HCl, H₂O

Questions and answers

35. Other than transfer of electrons, how can atoms lower their energy?

Some atoms lower their energy by mutually sharing their electrons.

36. What two forces act when two atoms approach each other to form a covalent bond?

A force of attraction (the electrons of one atom come under the influence of the other nucleus), which lowers the energy, and a force of repulsion (between the electrons and nuclei of the two atoms), which increases the energy.

37. At what distance is a stable molecule formed?

At the distance where the attractive forces dominate the repulsive forces; the total energy is then minimum and a stable molecule forms.

38. What is a single covalent bond?

A bond formed by the mutual sharing of one electron pair provided by the bonded atoms; it is represented by a single line (—).

39. What is a double covalent bond?

A bond in which two atoms share two electrons each, i.e. two electron pairs; it is represented by two lines (=).

40. What is a triple covalent bond?

A bond in which two atoms share three electrons each, i.e. three electron pairs; it is represented by three lines (≡).

41. How are single, double and triple covalent bonds represented?
• How are mutually shared electrons shown in a diagram?

Single bond by one line (—), double bond by two lines (=), triple bond by three lines (≡). The shared electrons are shown by a dot or a cross.

42. Explain the formation of the hydrogen molecule.

Two hydrogen atoms share one electron pair, forming a single covalent bond, H — H; each H gets two electrons (a duplet).

43. Explain the formation of the chlorine molecule.

Two chlorine atoms share one electron pair to form a single covalent bond, Cl — Cl, and each atom completes its octet.

44. Which of H₂, Cl₂, O₂ and N₂ contains a double bond and which a triple bond?

O₂ has a double bond (O = O); N₂ has a triple bond (N ≡ N). H₂ and Cl₂ have single bonds.

45. Explain the formation of the oxygen molecule.

Two oxygen atoms share two electrons each (two pairs) to form a double covalent bond, O = O.

46. Explain the formation of the nitrogen molecule.

Two nitrogen atoms share three electrons each (three pairs) to form a triple covalent bond, N ≡ N.

47. How is a water molecule formed?

Two hydrogen atoms share their electrons separately with the electrons of one oxygen atom, giving two O–H single covalent bonds (H — O — H).

48. How is a carbon dioxide molecule formed?

One carbon atom shares its four electrons with two oxygen atoms; each oxygen also shares two electrons, so there are two C = O double bonds (O = C = O).

49. Draw the structural formula of ammonia.
• How many covalent bonds does nitrogen form in NH₃?

Nitrogen forms three single covalent bonds with three hydrogen atoms, H — N — H (third H below N); nitrogen keeps one lone pair.

50. Draw the structural formula of hydrogen cyanide.

H — C ≡ N: a single bond between H and C and a triple bond between C and N.

51. Explain the formation of methane.

One carbon atom shares its four electrons with four hydrogen atoms (one with each) forming four C — H single covalent bonds in CH₄.

52. Which type of bond is present between the two carbon atoms of ethene (C₂H₄)?

A double covalent bond (C = C); each carbon is also singly bonded to two hydrogen atoms (H₂C = CH₂).

53. Draw the structural formula of methanol.

H₃C — O — H: carbon bonded to three hydrogens and to oxygen; oxygen bonded to one hydrogen.

54. What electronic configuration do atoms acquire after mutually sharing electrons?

The electronic configuration of the nearest noble gas.

55. What is a non-polar covalent bond? Give examples.

A covalent bond between two identical atoms, in which the shared pair lies in the middle of the two atoms; e.g. the bonds in hydrogen and chlorine molecules.

56. What is a polar covalent bond?

A covalent bond between two non-identical atoms in which the shared pair bends towards the more electronegative atom, creating a partial negative charge on it and an equal partial positive charge on the other atom.

57. Why is the bond in HCl polar?

Chlorine is more electronegative than hydrogen, so the shared pair bends towards chlorine, giving it a partial negative charge (H^δ+ — Cl^δ−).

58. Are the covalent bonds in water polar or non-polar?

They are polar covalent bonds, with partial positive charge on the hydrogen atoms and partial negative charge on oxygen.

59. Differentiate between polar and non-polar covalent bonds.

A non-polar bond is between identical atoms and the shared pair lies in the middle (H₂, Cl₂); a polar bond is between non-identical atoms and the pair bends towards the more electronegative atom, giving partial charges (HCl, H₂O).

Part 5 CHEM-04 CHEM-12

Coordinate Covalent Bond

Section 3.2.3 — donor, acceptor and the arrow notation.

Notes
  • A coordinate covalent bond is a type of covalent bond in which the shared electron pair is donated by one atom only. It forms when a species has an electron pair to donate to another species.
  • The species which donates the pair is the donor; the one which accepts it is the acceptor. An arrow head (→) pointing towards the acceptor represents this bond.

Hydroxonium ion (H₃O⁺) — Fig 3.7

  • Acids provide protons (H⁺) when dissolved in water. The proton has an empty outer shell and accepts one of the two pairs of electrons on the oxygen atom of a water molecule → hydroxonium ion (H₃O⁺).
H₂O :  + H⁺  →  [ H₂O → H ]⁺        (donor: O of H₂O, acceptor: H⁺)
  • The positive charge covers the whole ion. After formation there is no difference between a coordinate covalent bond and a covalent bond — all three bonds of oxygen behave exactly alike.

Reaction between ammonia and boron trifluoride — Fig 3.8

  • An electron pair from nitrogen of ammonia fills the partially empty outer shell of boron in BF₃.
H₃N:  +  BF₃   →   H₃N → BF₃        (donor: N of NH₃, acceptor: B of BF₃)

Other examples (Fig 3.8)

NH₃ + H–Cl   →   [ NH₄ ]⁺ Cl⁻          (ammonium chloride: N donates its pair to H⁺)
C₂H₅–O(H):  + H⁺   →   [ C₂H₅–O(H) → H ]⁺      (protonated ethyl alcohol)
  • In ammonium chloride the coordinate bond links the nitrogen of ammonia and the proton. The positive charge is spread all over the ammonium ion; all four N–H bonds behave exactly alike. This proves that the difference between a covalent bond and a coordinate covalent bond lies only in the way they are formed; once formed there is no difference.
  • When a coordinate covalent bond breaks, both the bonded electrons go to the donor species.
Covalent bond Coordinate covalent bond
Shared pair provided by Both atoms One atom only (donor)
Representation Line ( — ) Arrow ( → ) pointing to acceptor
After formation Same as coordinate bond Same as covalent bond

Questions and answers

60. What are a donor and an acceptor in a coordinate covalent bond?

The species that donates the electron pair is the donor; the species that accepts it is the acceptor.

61. How is a coordinate covalent bond represented?

By an arrow head (→) pointing towards the acceptor.

62. When is a coordinate covalent bond formed?

When one species has an electron pair to donate to another species that can accept it.

63. How is the hydroxonium ion (H₃O⁺) formed?

A proton (H⁺) from an acid, which has an empty outer shell, accepts one of the two electron pairs on the oxygen atom of a water molecule, forming a coordinate covalent bond.

64. Name the donor and the acceptor in the formation of H₃O⁺.

Donor: the oxygen of the water molecule (H₂O); acceptor: the proton (H⁺).

65. Is there any difference between a coordinate bond and a covalent bond after the hydroxonium ion is formed?

No. The positive charge covers the whole ion and all three bonds of oxygen behave exactly alike.

66. Explain the formation of a coordinate covalent bond between NH₃ and BF₃.

The electron pair on nitrogen of ammonia fills the partially empty outer shell of boron in boron trifluoride, forming H₃N → BF₃ (NH₃ is the donor, BF₃ the acceptor).

67. How is ammonium chloride formed from ammonia and hydrogen chloride?

Nitrogen of ammonia donates its lone pair to the hydrogen (proton) of HCl, forming the ammonium ion [NH₄]⁺ with a coordinate bond; with Cl⁻ it gives ammonium chloride.

68. How does ethyl alcohol form a coordinate bond with H⁺?

The oxygen atom of ethyl alcohol donates one of its lone pairs to H⁺, forming protonated ethyl alcohol with an O → H coordinate bond.

69. Do all four N–H bonds in the ammonium ion behave alike?

Yes. The positive charge is spread over the whole ammonium ion and all four N–H bonds behave exactly alike.

70. Where do the bonding electrons go when a coordinate covalent bond breaks?

Both the bonded electrons go to the donor species.

71. In what does the difference between a covalent bond and a coordinate covalent bond lie?

Only in the way they are formed; once formed, they are alike.

Part 6 CHEM-04 CHEM-07 CHEM-09 CHEM-11

The Metallic Bond

Section 3.3 — sea of electrons, strength of the bond and properties of metals.

Notes

Properties of metals (book list)

  1. Metals usually show metallic lustre.
  2. Metals usually have high melting and boiling points.
  3. Metals are good conductors of heat and electricity.
  4. Metals are usually hard and heavy.
  5. Metals can be made into different shapes by applying pressure.

These properties are different from those of ionic and covalent compounds, which suggests a different type of binding force between metallic atoms.

  • Metals usually have low ionisation energy values, so their atoms lose their outer electron(s) easily — the nuclei of metallic atoms cannot hold their outer electrons firmly.
  • In sodium metal each Na atom is surrounded by eight other Na atoms. The outer electrons move freely between the vacant spaces among the atoms because of their loose linkage with the nuclei. No electron stays attached to one particular nucleus; all electrons get attached to all nuclei at the same time. All atoms attract all electrons collectively, so they are bound together.
  • A metal therefore appears to have a sea of electrons in which all the nuclei (positive ions) are submerged.
  • Metallic bond: a type of chemical bond which has positively charged ions bound together by the mobile electrons (Fig 3.9).

Fig 3.9 (prose): rows of circles marked "+" (sodium ions) with small circles marked "−" (mobile electrons) moving between them.

Strength of the metallic bond depends on two factors:

  1. the number of positive charges on the positive ions, and
  2. the number of mobile electrons set free by each atom.
Metal Electrons set free per atom Ion charge Metallic bond Melting
Sodium (Fig 3.9) 1 + Not very strong Lower temperature
Magnesium (Fig 3.10) 2 2+ Stronger than in sodium Higher temperature than sodium

Fig 3.10 (prose): rows of circles marked "++" (Mg²⁺ ions) with twice as many mobile electrons between them as in Fig 3.9.

Explaining the properties

  • Conduction: the freely moving electrons make metals good conductors of heat and electricity.
  • Hard and heavy: atoms are strongly held and arranged in rows one above the other.
  • Shaping: when pressure is applied, the upper rows of atoms slip past the lower rows, so the shape changes; metals can therefore be drawn easily into wires and sheets (Fig 3.11).

Note (book box "Interesting Information!"): Metals are extensively used in machinery, automobiles, railways, aircraft, rockets, the construction industry, the electronics industry, jewellery, and electric wires, and in many other places.

Questions and answers

72. List the characteristic properties of metals.

Metals usually (1) show metallic lustre, (2) have high melting and boiling points, (3) are good conductors of heat and electricity, (4) are hard and heavy, and (5) can be made into different shapes by applying pressure.

73. Why do metal atoms lose their outer electrons easily?

Metals usually have low ionisation energy, so the nuclei of their atoms cannot hold the outer electrons firmly.

74. By how many atoms is each sodium atom surrounded in sodium metal?

By eight other sodium atoms.

75. What is meant by the "sea of electrons" in a metal?

The outer electrons of the metal atoms move freely between the atoms; none stays attached to a particular nucleus, so the metal appears to have a sea of electrons in which all the nuclei are submerged.

76. What holds the atoms of a metal together?

All the atoms attract all the mobile electrons collectively, so the positive ions are bound together by the mobile electrons — the metallic bond.

77. On what factors does the strength of a metallic bond depend?

On the number of positive charges on the positive ions and on the number of mobile electrons set free by each atom.

78. Why is the metallic bond in sodium weaker than in magnesium?

Each sodium atom sets free only one electron, whereas each magnesium atom releases two electrons and becomes 2+, so magnesium's metallic bond is stronger.

79. Why does magnesium melt at a higher temperature than sodium?

Because the metallic bond in magnesium (two mobile electrons per atom, 2+ ions) is stronger than in sodium (one electron per atom).

80. Why are metals good conductors of heat and electricity?

Because of the presence of freely moving electrons in metals.

81. Why are metals usually hard and heavy?

Their atoms are strongly held and arranged in rows one above the other.

82. Why can metals be drawn into wires and sheets?
• Which property of metals makes them malleable and ductile?

When pressure is applied, the upper rows of atoms slip past the lower rows, so the shape of the metal changes without breaking it; metals can thus be drawn into wires and sheets.

83. Name some industries in which metals are used.

Machinery, automobiles, railways, aircraft, rockets, construction, electronics, jewellery and electric wires.

84. In Fig 3.9, what do the circles marked "+" and "−" represent?

"+" circles are the positive metal ions (sodium ions); "−" circles are the mobile electrons moving between them.

Part 10 CHEM-08

Intermolecular Forces of Attraction

Section 3.7 — dipole–dipole forces and hydrogen bonding.

Notes
  • The attractive forces between the molecules of elements and compounds are intermolecular forces of attraction. They are generally very weak compared to the bonding forces between atoms. Among the three states of matter they are the weakest among gas molecules and strongest among solid molecules.
  • Some intermolecular forces are weak and others relatively strong; they affect the physical properties. Melting and boiling points depend on their strength: the stronger the forces among the molecules of a liquid, the higher its boiling point; the stronger the intermolecular forces, the higher the melting point of a solid.
  • Two types are explained: (1) dipole–dipole forces and (2) hydrogen bonding.

1. Dipole–Dipole Forces of Attraction

  • Present between molecules of a polar compound like HCl. Chlorine's electronegativity is greater than hydrogen's, so it attracts the shared pair with greater force → H^δ+ — Cl^δ−.
  • Because of these partial charges, HCl molecules attract each other; these are dipole–dipole forces (Fig 3.12: δ⁺ end of one molecule attracts δ⁻ end of the next).
  • Compounds with these forces show relatively higher melting and boiling points.

2. Hydrogen Bonding

  • A special case of dipole–dipole attractive forces. When hydrogen is covalently bonded to highly electronegative elements like F, O or N, the large electronegativity difference makes the bond highly polar, so strong dipole–dipole attractions exist among the molecules.
  • Example: in H₂O the O–H bonds are highly polar, so strong attractive forces develop between water molecules (Fig 3.13). This force between water molecules is hydrogen bonding.
  • The strength of hydrogen bonds gives water relatively higher melting and boiling points than compounds like H₂S and NH₃.

Occurs between Molecules of a polar compound (e.g. HCl) Molecules in which H is bonded to F, O or N (e.g. H₂O)
Cause Partial charges δ⁺ / δ⁻ Very large electronegativity difference → highly polar bond
Strength Relatively strong Strong (a special case of dipole–dipole)
Effect Relatively higher m.p. and b.p. Water has higher m.p. and b.p. than H₂S and NH₃

Nature of Bonding and Properties

Section 3.8 — how bonding decides properties, conduction and uses.

Notes

Notes — ionic compounds

  • Oppositely charged ions are held by strong electrostatic attraction in a crystal lattice. The ions are rigid, so ionic compounds are very stable solids with significantly high melting points.
  • Ions are spherical and oppositely charged and surround each other from all sides, so ionic bonds are non-directional. This arrangement is called the crystal lattice.
  • If an external force is applied to the lattice it breaks easily → ionic solids are highly brittle.
  • Solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity because the ions are tightly held and cannot move. In the molten state the ions become free and conduct.
  • Ionic solids are generally soluble in water. Water not only breaks the electrostatic attraction but also hydrates the free ions (Fig 3.14). Hydration = water molecules surround and interact with ions or molecules (the δ⁻ oxygen ends of water surround Na⁺; the δ⁺ hydrogen ends surround Cl⁻).
  • In aqueous solution ionic compounds also conduct because the free ions move towards their respective electrodes.
  • Ionic compounds generally react in aqueous solution: when two ionic solutions are mixed, the positive ions of one may react with the negative ions of the other to form a new compound.
NaCl(s) + AgNO₃(s)  --H₂O-->  Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) + Ag⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq)
                                          ↓
                         AgCl(s) + Na⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq)        (white precipitate of AgCl)

Note (book box "Interesting Information!"): Conduction of ionic compounds in the molten state and as aqueous solutions is used to prepare many important elements and compounds. Electrolysis of molten NaCl gives sodium metal and chlorine gas. Electrolysis of aqueous NaCl gives sodium hydroxide and chlorine gas. Diamonds, due to exceptional hardness, are highly valued in industry: diamond-tipped glass cutters make clean cuts in glass and diamond-tipped drill bits drill through hard rocks in mining. Graphite is used in pencils, polishes and crucibles; graphite electrodes are used in battery cells and in electric arc furnaces to produce steel.

Notes — covalent elements and compounds

  • Elements on the right side of the periodic table exist as covalently bonded diatomic molecules (except the inert gases): N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂. Because the forces between their molecules are very weak, their densities and boiling points are very low.
  • Bromine (Br₂) is a volatile fuming liquid; carbon, phosphorus and sulphur are covalent solids. These solid elements exist in both amorphous and crystalline forms.
  • Coal is the amorphous form of carbon; diamond and graphite are its crystalline forms. Coal is used as a fuel in electricity-generating plants.
  • Diamond: each carbon atom is surrounded by four other carbon atoms linked by strong covalent bonds. This rigid structure makes diamond the hardest thing on the planet; used as a cutting, polishing and drilling tool.
  • Graphite: a layered structure of hexagonal rings of carbon. The layers are not strongly bonded and slip past each other, so graphite is used as an industrial lubricant. The layers also have mobile electrons in between, so graphite is a good conductor of electricity and is used as an electrode.
  • Binary covalent compounds generally exist as low-boiling gases, except water: methane (CH₄), ammonia (NH₃), hydrogen sulphide (H₂S), hydrogen chloride (HCl), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), carbon dioxide (CO₂) and sulphur dioxide are gases at room temperature.
  • Water and hydrogen fluoride are liquids at room temperature. Liquid water has a high boiling point because of strong intermolecular forces between its molecules.
  • Covalent molecules like hydrogen chloride, sulphuric acid and nitric acid ionise completely in water, behaving as very strong acids:
HCl(g)   --H₂O-->  H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)
H₂SO₄(l) --H₂O-->  2H⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq)        (book prints ⇌ arrows)

Key Points

Chapter Key Points

From the book
  1. Atoms form bonds with other atoms to stabilise themselves by obeying the duplet and octet rules.
  2. The force of attraction which keeps the atoms together is called a chemical bond.
  3. The bond formed by the transference of one or more electrons is called an ionic bond.
  4. A covalent bond is formed by the mutual sharing of electrons between atoms; it may be single, double or triple.
  5. When a covalent bond forms between two identical atoms it is a non-polar covalent bond.
  6. When a covalent bond forms between two non-identical atoms with different electronegativity values, it is a polar covalent bond.
  7. Intermolecular forces are of two types: dipole–dipole attraction and hydrogen bonding.
  8. When an electron pair is provided by an atom or ion or molecule to form a bond, it is a coordinate covalent bond.
  9. Ionic solids are crystalline compounds with high melting and boiling points; they are generally soluble in aqueous solution.
  10. Lower molecular mass covalent compounds are gases or low-boiling liquids; higher molecular mass covalent compounds are solids. They are bad conductors of electricity and soluble in organic solvents.
  11. Properties of ionic and covalent compounds are adequately explained on the basis of the type of attractive forces present between them.

Exercise 4 — Descriptive questions free sample: 2 of 6 questions

Ex. 4 (i) — Explain the formation of an ionic bond and a covalent bond.Challenging
Question:

Explain the formation of an ionic bond and a covalent bond.

Ionic bond: atoms transfer electron(s) to reach the nearest noble-gas configuration. Na (2,8,1) loses its outer electron to form Na⁺; Cl (2,8,7) gains it to form Cl⁻; the ions are held by electrostatic attraction (NaCl). Similarly Ca → Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻ and 2Cl + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻ give CaCl₂. Covalent bond: atoms share electron pair(s). As two atoms approach, attraction between each nucleus and the other's electrons lowers the energy while repulsion raises it; at the distance of minimum energy a stable molecule forms. H₂ has one shared pair (H — H), O₂ two (O = O), N₂ three (N ≡ N); in water two H atoms share electrons separately with one O atom.

Ex. 4 (vi) — Why are metals usually hard and heavy?Easy
Question:

Why are metals usually hard and heavy?

In metals the atoms are strongly held by the metallic bond (positive ions attracted by the sea of electrons) and are arranged closely in rows one above the other.
