Part 1

History of Numbers

From the Sumerians to the Indo-Arabic system.

1. Who first studied numbers, and how did different civilizations count?
• How did Sumerians, Egyptians, Romans, Indians and Arabs write numbers?• Give a brief history of numbers.
Numbers were studied over thousands of years by many civilizations:

2. Which number system did the Sumerians use?
• What is the base of the Sumerian number system?
The Sumerians (1900 – 4500 BCE) used the sexagesimal (base 60) system for counting.
⛔ Common Mistake: The Sumerians used base 60. Both Egyptians and Indians used base 10.
3. What are Roman numerals? Write the value of each letter.
• Which seven letters are used in the Roman number system?
Roman numerals are a number system that was widely used throughout Europe as the standard writing system until the late Middle Ages. It uses 7 letters:
\(\text{I}=1,\ \text{V}=5,\ \text{X}=10,\ \text{L}=50,\ \text{C}=100,\ \text{D}=500,\ \text{M}=1000\)
4. What did the Indians contribute to numbers? Why are our numerals called Indo-Arabic?
• Who invented the concept of zero?• Why is the present number system called Indo-Arabic?
Indians developed the concept of zero (0) and made a significant contribution to the decimal (base 10) system. The number system used today was invented by Indians; it is called Indo-Arabic because Arab merchants took it to the Western world.
📝 Zero is the basis of the decimal number system, which is why its invention is considered the greatest Indian contribution.
5. Who was al-Khwārizmī, and what did the Arabs introduce to Europe?
• Who introduced algebra as a distinct field?• What did Al-Karaji do?
The Arabs (800 – 1500 CE) introduced the Arabic numerals 0 – 9 to Europe. Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwārizmī introduced algebra as a distinct field in the 9th century, and successors such as Al-Karaji expanded his work.
6. What is the counting set, and how did it grow into the real numbers?
• What are natural numbers?
In the modern era the set \(\{1, 2, 3, \dots\}\) was adopted as the counting set. This set of natural numbers was extended to the set of real numbers, which is used most frequently in everyday life.
Part 2

Rational and Irrational Numbers

Decimal forms and the set of real numbers.

7. What are rational numbers? Write the set in symbols.
• Define a rational number.• What is the set Q?
A rational number can be written in the form \(\dfrac{p}{q}\) where \(p, q\) are integers and \(q \neq 0\):
\(Q=\left\{\dfrac{p}{q}\;;\;p,q\in Z\ \wedge\ q\neq 0\right\}\)
8. What are irrational numbers?
• Define the set Q′.• Which numbers cannot be written as p/q?
The irrational numbers (\(Q^{\prime}\)) are those numbers that cannot be expressed as a quotient of integers. Examples: \(\pi,\ e,\ \sqrt{2}\).
9. How are the real numbers formed from rational and irrational numbers?
• What is R in terms of Q and Q′?
The set of real numbers is the union of the rational and irrational numbers:
\(R=Q\cup Q^{\prime}\)
Real numbersDecimal form
RationalTerminating or non-terminating and recurring
IrrationalNon-terminating and non-recurring
10. What is a terminating decimal? Give examples.
• Which rational numbers have a finite decimal form?
A terminating decimal has a finite number of digits after the decimal point. Such numbers are rational. For example:
\(\dfrac{1}{4}=0.25,\quad \dfrac{8}{25}=0.32,\quad \dfrac{3}{8}=0.375,\quad \dfrac{4}{5}=0.8\)
11. What is a non-terminating recurring decimal? Give examples.
• Is a recurring decimal a rational number?
A non-terminating and recurring decimal has an infinitely repeating pattern of digits after the decimal point. It is also a rational number.
\(\dfrac{1}{3}=0.\overline{3},\quad \dfrac{1}{6}=0.1\overline{6},\quad \dfrac{4}{9}=0.\overline{4},\quad \dfrac{22}{7}=3.\overline{142857}\)
12. What is the decimal form of an irrational number? Give examples.
• Name some irrational numbers.• What is Euler's number?
The decimal form of an irrational number is non-terminating and non-recurring — the digits never repeat a pattern.
\(\pi=3.1415926535\ldots,\quad e=2.71828182845\ldots,\quad \sqrt{2}=1.41421356237\ldots\)
📝 \(e = 2.7182\ldots\) is called Euler's Number.
13. Is 22/7 rational or irrational? How is it related to π?
• Is π equal to 22/7?
\(\dfrac{22}{7}=3.\overline{142857}\) is a rational number because its decimal repeats. \(\pi\) is irrational; \(\dfrac{22}{7}\) is only an approximation of \(\pi\).
14. Worked Example 1: Identify the following decimal numbers as rational or irrational: (i) 0.35 (ii) 0.444… (iii) \(3.\overline{5}\) (iv) 3.36788542… (v) 1.709975947…
• Basic level
Solution:
(i) 0.35 is a terminating decimal ⇒ rational.
(ii) 0.444… is non-terminating and recurring ⇒ rational.
(iii) \(3.\overline{5}=3.5555\ldots\) is non-terminating and recurring ⇒ rational.
(iv) 3.36788542… is non-terminating and non-recurring ⇒ irrational.
(v) 1.709975947… is non-terminating and non-recurring ⇒ irrational.
Part 3

The Number Line, Recurring Decimals and p/q

Representing numbers and converting recurring decimals.

15. Worked Example 2: How do we represent √5 on the number line?
• Intermediate level• Locate an irrational number on a number line.
Solution: \(\sqrt5\) is located by geometric construction. As \(\sqrt5=2.236\ldots\) is near 2, take \(OA=2\) units on the number line and draw \(AB=1\) unit perpendicular to it at \(A\), so that \(OAB\) is a right-angled triangle. By Pythagoras theorem:
\((OB)^2=(OA)^2+(AB)^2=(2)^2+(1)^2=4+1=5\ \Rightarrow\ OB=\sqrt5\)
With centre \(O\) and radius \(OB=\sqrt5\) draw an arc cutting the number line at \(P\). Then \(|OP|=\sqrt5\).
16. What is the sum or product of a rational and an irrational number?
• Rational + irrational = ?• Rational (≠ 0) × irrational = ?
(i) Rational number + Irrational number = Irrational number.
(ii) Rational number (≠ 0) × Irrational number = Irrational number.
17. What is the product of two irrational numbers?
• Is the product of two irrational numbers always irrational?
It can be rational or irrational. For example \(\sqrt2\times\sqrt2=2\) (rational), but \(\sqrt2\times\sqrt3=\sqrt6\) (irrational).
18. How do we change a recurring decimal into the form p/q?
• Convert a recurring decimal into a fraction.
Let \(x\) be the decimal. If one digit repeats, multiply by 10; if two digits repeat, multiply by 100. Subtract the original equation so the recurring part cancels, then solve for \(x\).
19. Worked Example 3: Express (i) \(0.\overline{5}\) and (ii) \(0.\overline{93}\) as \(\dfrac pq\).
• Intermediate level
Solution:
(i) Let \(x=0.\overline{5}=0.5555\ldots\) …(a)
Multiply by 10: \(10x=5.5555\ldots\) …(b)
Subtract (a) from (b): \(9x=5\)
\(x=\dfrac{5}{9}\)
(ii) Let \(x=0.\overline{93}=0.939393\ldots\) …(c)
Multiply by 100: \(100x=93.939393\ldots\) …(d)
Subtract (c) from (d): \(99x=93\)
\(x=\dfrac{93}{99}\ \left(=\dfrac{31}{33}\right)\)
20. How many rational numbers lie between two numbers? How do we find them?
• How do we find a rational number between a and b?
There are infinitely many rational numbers between any two numbers. To find one, take the average \(\dfrac{a+b}{2}\); to find another, take the average of the new number and one of the end numbers.
21. Worked Example 4: Find two rational numbers between 2 and 3.
• Basic level
Solution: The average of 2 and 3 is
\(\dfrac{2+3}{2}=\dfrac{5}{2}\)
The average of \(\dfrac52\) and 3 is
\(\dfrac{\frac52+3}{2}=\dfrac{\frac{11}{2}}{2}=\dfrac{11}{4}\)
Hence two rational numbers between 2 and 3 are \(\dfrac52\) and \(\dfrac{11}{4}\).

27. Worked Example 5: If \(a=\dfrac23,\ b=\dfrac32,\ c=\dfrac53\), verify the distributive properties over addition.
• Intermediate level
(i) Left: \(a(b+c)=ab+ac\)
LHS \(=\dfrac23\left(\dfrac32+\dfrac53\right)=\dfrac23\cdot\dfrac{19}{6}=\dfrac{19}{9}\)
RHS \(=\dfrac23\cdot\dfrac32+\dfrac23\cdot\dfrac53=1+\dfrac{10}{9}=\dfrac{19}{9}\)
LHS = RHS, verified.

(ii) Right: \((a+b)c=ac+bc\)
LHS \(=\left(\dfrac23+\dfrac32\right)\dfrac53=\dfrac{13}{6}\cdot\dfrac53=\dfrac{65}{18}\)
RHS \(=\dfrac23\cdot\dfrac53+\dfrac32\cdot\dfrac53=\dfrac{10}{9}+\dfrac{15}{6}=\dfrac{20+45}{18}=\dfrac{65}{18}\)
LHS = RHS, verified.
28. Worked Example 6: Which property justifies each statement? (i) \(a>13\Rightarrow a+2>15\) (ii) \(3<9\) and \(6<12\Rightarrow 9<21\) (iii) \(7>4\) and \(5>3\Rightarrow 35>12\) (iv) \(-5<-4\Rightarrow 20>16\)
• Basic level
Solution:
(i) Add 2 on both sides: \(a+2>13+2=15\) — order property w.r.t. addition.
(ii) \(3+6<9+12\Rightarrow 9<21\) — order property w.r.t. addition.
(iii) \(7\times5>4\times3\Rightarrow35>12\) — order property w.r.t. multiplication.
(iv) Multiply both sides by \(-4\): \((-5)(-4)>(-4)(-4)\Rightarrow20>16\) — order property w.r.t. multiplication; the sign reverses because the multiplier is negative.
Part 5

Radical Expressions

Laws of radicals and indices, surds and rationalization.

29. What is a radical expression? Name its parts.
• What are the index, radicand and radical sign?• Define the nth root of a.
If \(n>1\) is a positive integer and \(a\) is a real number, any real number \(x\) such that \(x=\sqrt[n]{a}\) is the nth root of a. Here \(\sqrt{\ }\) is the radical, \(n\) is the index and \(a\) is the radicand. The exponential form is:
\(x=\sqrt[n]{a}=a^{1/n}\)
30. State the laws of radicals.
• List the laws of radicals.
\(\sqrt[n]{ab}=\sqrt[n]{a}\cdot\sqrt[n]{b}\)
\(\sqrt[n]{\dfrac ab}=\dfrac{\sqrt[n]{a}}{\sqrt[n]{b}}\)
\(\sqrt[n]{a^m}=\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m\)
\(\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^n=a\)
31. State the laws of indices.
• List the laws of exponents.
\(a^m\cdot a^n=a^{m+n}\qquad (a^m)^n=a^{mn}\)
\((ab)^n=a^nb^n\qquad \left(\dfrac ab\right)^n=\dfrac{a^n}{b^n}\)
\(\dfrac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n}\qquad a^0=1\)
💡 Tip: A negative index means reciprocal: \(a^{-n}=\dfrac{1}{a^n}\).
32. Worked Example 7: Simplify (i) \(\sqrt[4]{16x^4y^8}\) (ii) \(\sqrt[3]{27x^6y^9z^3}\) (iii) \((64)^{-4/3}\)
• Intermediate level
Solution:
(i) \(\sqrt[4]{16x^4y^8}=(16)^{1/4}(x^4)^{1/4}(y^8)^{1/4}=2\cdot x\cdot y^2=2xy^2\)

(ii) \(\sqrt[3]{27x^6y^9z^3}=(3^3)^{1/3}(x^6)^{1/3}(y^9)^{1/3}(z^3)^{1/3}=3x^2y^3z\)

(iii) \((64)^{-4/3}=\dfrac{1}{(64)^{4/3}}=\dfrac{1}{(4^3)^{4/3}}=\dfrac{1}{4^4}=\dfrac{1}{256}\)
33. What is a surd? Give examples and non-examples.
• Define a surd.• Is √9 a surd?• Is every irrational number a surd?
An irrational radical with a rational radicand is called a surd.
• \(\sqrt5,\ \sqrt7,\ \sqrt2,\ \sqrt[3]{11}\) are surds.
• \(\sqrt9=3\) is not a surd (the result is rational).
• \(\sqrt\pi\) and \(\sqrt e\) are not surds (the radicand is irrational).
📝 Every surd is an irrational number, but not every irrational number is a surd — e.g. \(\sqrt\pi\).
34. What are the types of surds? What are conjugate surds?
• What is a monomial surd?• What is a binomial surd?
TypeMeaningExamples
Monomial surdA surd with a single term\(\sqrt5,\ \sqrt7\)
Binomial surdThe sum of two monomial surds\(\sqrt3+\sqrt5\)
Conjugate surds\(\sqrt a+\sqrt b\) and \(\sqrt a-\sqrt b\)\(\sqrt5+\sqrt2\) and \(\sqrt5-\sqrt2\)
The product of conjugate surds is rational:
\((\sqrt a+\sqrt b)(\sqrt a-\sqrt b)=a-b\)
35. What is rationalization of the denominator? How is it done?
• How do we remove a surd from the denominator?
To rationalize a denominator of the form \(a+b\sqrt x\) or \(a-b\sqrt x\), multiply the numerator and the denominator by its conjugate. The denominator then becomes a rational number.
36. Worked Example 8: Rationalize the denominator of (i) \(\dfrac{3}{\sqrt5+\sqrt2}\) (ii) \(\dfrac{3}{\sqrt5-\sqrt3}\)
• Intermediate level
Solution:
(i) \(\dfrac{3}{\sqrt5+\sqrt2}\times\dfrac{\sqrt5-\sqrt2}{\sqrt5-\sqrt2}=\dfrac{3(\sqrt5-\sqrt2)}{5-2}=\dfrac{3(\sqrt5-\sqrt2)}{3}=\sqrt5-\sqrt2\)

(ii) \(\dfrac{3}{\sqrt5-\sqrt3}\times\dfrac{\sqrt5+\sqrt3}{\sqrt5+\sqrt3}=\dfrac{3(\sqrt5+\sqrt3)}{5-3}=\dfrac{3(\sqrt5+\sqrt3)}{2}\)
Part 6

Real Numbers in Daily Life

Temperature conversions, profit and loss, profit sharing and simple profit.

37. Where are real numbers used in daily life?
• Give some fields in which real numbers are applied.
Real numbers are used in science and engineering, medicine and health, environmental science (climate modeling, pollution monitoring), computer science (algorithms, data compression, graphics), navigation and transportation (GPS, flight planning), surveying and architecture, and statistics.
38. Worked Example 9: The sum of two real numbers is 8 and their difference is 2. Find the numbers.
• Basic level
Solution: Let the numbers be \(a\) and \(b\).
\(a+b=8\) …(i) and \(a-b=2\) …(ii)
Adding: \(2a=10\Rightarrow a=5\). Put in (ii): \(5-b=2\Rightarrow b=3\).
The numbers are 5 and 3.
39. Name the three temperature scales and write the conversion formulae.
• How do we convert Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin?
The common scales are Celsius (°C), Fahrenheit (°F) and Kelvin (K):
\(K={}^{\circ}C+273\)
\({}^{\circ}C=\dfrac59\,({}^{\circ}F-32)\)
\({}^{\circ}F=\dfrac{9\,{}^{\circ}C}{5}+32\)
📝 Water freezes at 0 °C = 32 °F = 273.15 K and boils at 100 °C = 212 °F = 373.15 K. The book's examples use 273 for Celsius to Kelvin.
40. Worked Example 10: Normal human body temperature is 98.6 °F. Convert it into Celsius and Kelvin.
• Basic level
Solution:
\({}^{\circ}C=\dfrac59(98.6-32)=\dfrac59(66.6)=37^{\circ}\)
\(K=37+273=310\ \text{kelvin}\)
41. What are profit and loss? Write their formulae.
• How do we find profit percentage and loss percentage?
\(\text{Profit}=SP-CP,\qquad \text{Profit}\,\%=\dfrac{\text{profit}}{CP}\times100\)
\(\text{Loss}=CP-SP,\qquad \text{Loss}\,\%=\dfrac{\text{loss}}{CP}\times100\)
42. Worked Example 11: Jamil bought a bicycle for Rs. 6590 and sold it for Rs. 6850. Find the profit percentage.
• Basic level
Solution: \(CP=6590,\ SP=6850\).
Profit \(=6850-6590=\) Rs. 260.
\(\text{Profit}\,\%=\dfrac{260\times100}{6590}=3.94\,\%\approx4\,\%\)
43. Worked Example 12: Umair bought a book for Rs. 850 and sold it for Rs. 720. Find his loss percentage.
• Basic level
Solution: \(CP=850,\ SP=720\).
Loss \(=850-720=\) Rs. 130.
\(\text{Loss}\,\%=\dfrac{130}{850}\times100=15.29\,\%\)
44. Worked Example 13: Saleem, Nadeem and Tanveer earn a profit of Rs. 4,50,000. Their investments are in the ratio 4 : 7 : 14. Find each person's profit.
• Intermediate level• How is profit shared in a ratio?
Solution: Sum of ratios \(=4+7+14=25\).
Saleem \(=\dfrac{4}{25}\times4{,}50{,}000=\) Rs. 72,000
Nadeem \(=\dfrac{7}{25}\times4{,}50{,}000=\) Rs. 126,000
Tanveer \(=\dfrac{14}{25}\times4{,}50{,}000=\) Rs. 252,000
45. Worked Example 14: The simple profit on Rs. 6400 for 12 years is Rs. 3840. Find the rate of profit.
• Intermediate level
Solution: Principal = Rs. 6400, profit = Rs. 3840, time = 12 years.
\(\text{Rate}=\dfrac{\text{profit}\times100}{\text{time}\times\text{principal}}=\dfrac{3840\times100}{12\times6400}=5\,\%\)

Ex 1.1 · Q1 — Identify each of the following as a rational or irrational number: (i) 2.353535 (ii) 0.6̅ (iii)…Easy
Question:

Identify each of the following as a rational or irrational number:
(i) 2.353535 (ii) 0.6̅ (iii) 2.236067… (iv) √7 (v) e (vi) π (vii) 5 + √11 (viii) √3 + √13 (ix) 15/4 (x) (2 − √2)(2 + √2)

Solution:

(i) 2.353535 is a decimal that ends (terminating) ⇒ rational.

(ii) 0.6̅ = 0.666… is non-terminating and recurring ⇒ rational (0.6̅ = 2/3).

(iii) 2.236067… is non-terminating and non-recurring ⇒ irrational.

(iv) √7 = 2.6457… (7 is not a perfect square) ⇒ irrational.

(v) e = 2.71828… ⇒ irrational.

(vi) π = 3.14159… ⇒ irrational.

(vii) 5 + √11: rational + irrational ⇒ irrational.

(viii) √3 + √13: the sum of two non-similar surds ⇒ irrational.

(ix) 15/4 is a quotient of integers (= 3.75, terminating) ⇒ rational.

(x)

(2 − √2)(2 + √2) = (2)2 − (√2)2
                 = 4 − 2
                 = 2

2 = 2/1 ⇒ rational.

Ex 1.1 · Q6 — Find two rational numbers between: (i) 1/3 and 1/4 (ii) 3 and 4 (iii) 3/5 and 4/5Easy
Question:

Find two rational numbers between: (i) 1/3 and 1/4 (ii) 3 and 4 (iii) 3/5 and 4/5

Solution (by the average method):

(i)

(1/3 + 1/4)/2 = ((4 + 3)/12)/2 = 7/24
Between 7/24 and 1/3:
(7/24 + 8/24)/2 = (15/24)/2 = 15/48 = 5/16

Two rational numbers: 7/24 and 5/16.

(ii)

(3 + 4)/2 = 7/2
(7/2 + 4)/2 = (15/2)/2 = 15/4

Two rational numbers: 7/2 and 15/4.

(iii)

(3/5 + 4/5)/2 = (7/5)/2 = 7/10
(7/10 + 4/5)/2 = (7/10 + 8/10)/2 = (15/10)/2 = 3/4

Two rational numbers: 7/10 and 3/4.

(Any other rational numbers lying between the given numbers are also correct — there are infinitely many.)

Ex 1.3 · Q3 — A rectangle has sides of length 2 + √18 m and (5 − 4/√2) m. Express the area of the rectangle in the…Challenging
Question:

A rectangle has sides of length 2 + √18 m and (5 − 4/√2) m. Express the area of the rectangle in the form a + b√2, where a and b are integers.

Solution:

√18 = √(9 × 2) = 3√2
4/√2 = 4/√2 × √2/√2 = 4√2/2 = 2√2
Length = 2 + √18 = 2 + 3√2
Width  = 5 − 4/√2 = 5 − 2√2
Area = Length × Width
     = (2 + 3√2)(5 − 2√2)
     = 10 − 4√2 + 15√2 − 6(√2)2
     = 10 + 11√2 − 12
     = −2 + 11√2

Area = (−2 + 11√2) m², i.e. a = −2 and b = 11.

Ex 1.3 · Q7 — Mirha bought a toy for Rs. 1500 and sold for Rs. 1520. What was her profit percentage?Easy
Question:

Mirha bought a toy for Rs. 1500 and sold for Rs. 1520. What was her profit percentage?

Solution:

CP = Rs. 1500,  SP = Rs. 1520
Profit = SP − CP = 1520 − 1500 = Rs. 20
Profit % = (profit/CP × 100) %
         = (20/1500 × 100) %
         = 1.33 %

Her profit percentage is 1.33 % (= 4/3 %).

Review Ex 1 · Q10 — Salma invested Rs. 3,50,000 in a bank, which paid simple profit at the rate of 7 1/4 % per annum. After…Challenging
Question:

Salma invested Rs. 3,50,000 in a bank, which paid simple profit at the rate of 7 1/4 % per annum. After 2 years, the rate was increased to 8% per annum. Find the amount she had at the end of 7 years.

Solution:

Principal = Rs. 3,50,000
Rate for the first 2 years = 7 1/4 % = 29/4 % per annum
Profit for the first 2 years = (P × R × T)/100
                             = (3,50,000 × 29/4 × 2)/100
                             = Rs. 50,750

Rate for the remaining 7 − 2 = 5 years = 8 % per annum
Profit for the next 5 years = (3,50,000 × 8 × 5)/100
                            = Rs. 1,40,000

Total profit = 50,750 + 1,40,000 = Rs. 1,90,750
Amount at the end of 7 years = Principal + Total profit
                             = 3,50,000 + 1,90,750
                             = Rs. 5,40,750

Salma had Rs. 5,40,750 at the end of 7 years (simple profit, principal unchanged).
