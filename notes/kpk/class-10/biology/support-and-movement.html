Human Skeleton and its Role

Section 13.1 — what a skeleton is, endoskeleton and exoskeleton, locomotion and the functions of the skeletal system.

Quick Notes

Introduction: The earthworm, a very common invertebrate generally found in the soil, always crawls on the ground and moves slowly, and it cannot stand erect like human beings and other mammals. The reason is that it does not have any skeleton which can support it to stand erect and move faster. All animals which can move fast possess some type of skeleton. Free movement, also known as locomotion, is a life characteristic of all living animals; for all types of movement the skeleton plays a basic role.

What a skeleton is

A skeleton is the framework of hard structures that provide physical support and protection for the bodies of animals; it also helps in locomotion. Like other vertebrates, the human skeleton is an endoskeleton — it lies inside the body. It is made of living tissue: it contains living cells, nerves and blood vessels, and it can grow and repair itself.

For Your InformationThe skeletal system of some invertebrates (e.g. arthropods) is outside the body and is called an exoskeleton.

Role of the skeletal system

  • Forms the hard structure of the body and gives it a definite shape.
  • Skeletal muscles are attached to the bones; because muscles attach to bones, the bones can move and so we can move.
  • The skull protects the brain and the vertebral column protects the spinal cord; the lungs and heart are protected in the rib cage.
  • Bone marrow produces red blood cells, platelets and white blood cells.
  • Bones store mineral salts (calcium, potassium, phosphorus) — this is why bones remain hard and strong.
RememberThe human skeleton has two parts: the AXIAL skeleton (80 bones) and the APPENDICULAR skeleton (126 bones) — total 206 bones in an adult.
Science, Technology and SocietyThe skeleton performs various functions in the living body. These functions include shape, support, movement, protection and blood cells production. In different animals these functions are variously performed, and the shape of the skeleton is in accordance with the requirement of the particular animal’s type.

Axial Skeleton

Section 13.1.1 — skull, vertebral column, ribs, sternum and the other bones of the axis of the body.

Quick Notes

The term "axis" means the central point around which other structures are distributed. The axial skeleton lies in the centre of the body, around which the whole body is built. It includes the skull, the vertebral column, the ribs and the sternum (chest bone).

Skull

The skull is made up of the cranium and the facial bones. The cranium consists of 8 bones which protect the brain. There are 14 facial bones, which make up the skeletal part of the face (skull = 22 bones in total).

Vertebral column

The vertebral column (backbone) extends from the skull to the pelvis. It allows us to stand erect and also protects the spinal cord. It consists of 33 vertebrae, named according to their location: 7 cervical (neck), 12 thoracic (chest region), 5 lumbar (lower back), and 9 pelvic vertebrae. The pelvic vertebrae form two sets — the sacrum (fusion of the anterior five vertebrae) and the coccyx (fusion of four posterior vertebrae).

Chest bone and ribs

There are 12 pairs of ribs, attached at the dorsal (back) side with the vertebrae. Out of the 12, 10 pairs connect anteriorly with the chest bone (sternum), either directly or through an arch. The rib cage supports the thoracic cavity, in which vital organs like the heart and lungs lie. The lower 2 pairs are called floating ribs because they do not attach to the sternum.

Middle ear bones & neck bone

Each human ear has three small bones called ossicles — the malleus, the incus and the stapes. There is one bone in the neck called the hyoid bone.

RememberAt birth, infants have about 350 bones; as a person grows, many of these fuse and the number reduces to 206. The largest bone is the femur, and the smallest bone is found in the internal (middle) ear.

Composition of the Skeleton: Bones and Cartilage

Section 13.2 — bone, cartilage and the three types of cartilage.

Quick Notes

The human skeleton is made up of two types of connective tissue: bones and cartilage.

Bones

Bones are the hardest and most rigid form of connective tissue in the human body. Muscles pull our bones to enable movements; bones give shape and structure to the body and protect delicate internal organs. The internal portion of many bones produces red blood cells, platelets and white blood cells. The hard, white outer layer of a bone is called compact bone. Its matrix contains collagen fibres and is saturated with calcium phosphate, which gives strength to the bone. Bone cells called osteocytes lie in spaces called lacunae; the matrix contains nerve fibres and blood vessels that keep the osteocytes alive. Beneath the compact bone is the spongy bone, whose spaces contain bone marrow, where most blood cells are formed.

Cartilage

Cartilage is a specialised connective tissue which is firm and flexible and does not stretch. It has great tensile strength and is much softer than bone. In adults, cartilage covers the ends of bones at joints (like the knee joint); it also supports the flexible portion of the nose and the external ears (pinna). The ground substance of cartilage is an amorphous gel-like substance containing collagen fibres in parallel arrangements. The cells of cartilage are called chondrocytes, which lie in spaces called lacunae. The number of chondrocytes found in cartilage determines how flexible or elastic the cartilage is. There are no blood vessels in the ground substance of cartilage. One of its main functions is to keep bones from rubbing together, thus reducing friction.

For Your InformationIn common words, cartilage is referred to as "soft bone". In early embryonic life the whole skeleton is made of cartilage, which then gradually turns into bones.

Three types of cartilage

  • Elastic cartilage — cells are closer together with less ground substance; found in the external ear flaps and the epiglottis.
  • Hyaline cartilage — fewer cells and more ground substance; found at the ends of long bones, in the nose, ears, trachea, larynx and bronchi.
  • Fibrous cartilage — the least number of cells, so maximum ground substance; found in the inter-vertebral disc.

Types of Joints

Section 13.3 — immovable, slightly movable and freely movable joints; hinge and ball-and-socket joints.

Quick Notes

Joints are formed at the meeting point of two or more bones. They keep our bones together by holding them with each other, allow the bones to move to different extents, and give mobility to the body. On the basis of the extent of movement they allow, joints are classified into three categories:

a. Immovable joints

In immovable joints the bones are fused together and do not allow any movement. For example, the joints present among the bones of the skull are immovable joints.

b. Slightly movable joints

These joints allow little movement. For example, the joints of the vertebral column and the ribs.

c. Freely movable joints

These joints allow free movements. There are two major types: the hinge joint and the ball-and-socket joint.

  • Hinge joints allow movement only in two directions and are present at the elbow and the knee.
  • Ball-and-socket joints allow movement in all directions (up, down, forward and backward); the hip joint and the shoulder joint are examples of ball-and-socket joints.

Section 13.3.1 — roles of tendons and ligaments.

Quick Notes

Ligaments are tough bands of connective tissue which hold the bones at the joints in place. Tendons are tough connective tissues which attach muscles to bones. When a muscle contracts, the tendon exerts a pulling force on the attached bone, and as a result the bone is pulled towards the muscle.

Muscles and Antagonistic Movement

Section 13.6 — types of muscles, origin and insertion, antagonism, flexors and extensors.

Quick Notes

Locomotion and all other types of movement are due to the contraction of muscles. There are three types of muscles in the body: cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and skeletal muscle.

Three types of muscle

  • Cardiac muscle (heart muscle) is an involuntary muscle — we cannot control its function. Its contractions help the heart pump blood throughout the body, and it keeps on contracting throughout life.
  • Smooth muscles are found in the walls of organs (e.g. the digestive tract, air passageways, blood vessels and the urinary bladder); their contractions move materials within the organs.
  • Skeletal muscles are attached to the bones of the skeleton; their contractions help us in movement and locomotion.
Types of muscle
Type of muscleControlLocation / Function
CardiacInvoluntaryHeart — contracts throughout life to pump blood
SmoothInvoluntaryWalls of organs (gut, blood vessels, bladder) — moves materials
SkeletalVoluntaryAttached to bones — movement and locomotion

Antagonistic movement of skeletal muscle

Skeletal muscles are attached to bones by tough connective tissue called tendons and are under voluntary control. One end of a skeletal muscle is always attached to a fixed bone and the other to a moving bone. The point where the muscle attaches to the fixed bone is called the origin; the point where it attaches to the moving bone is called the insertion. Most skeletal muscles are arranged in pairs: one muscle moves a bone in one direction while the other moves it in the opposite direction. When one muscle contracts (pulls), the other relaxes or elongates. The phenomenon in which one muscle opposes the action of the other is called antagonism, and such muscles are called antagonistic muscles.

For Your InformationMuscles can only PULL bones — they cannot push them. The human body has over 500 muscles responsible for all types of movement.
For Your InformationIt should be remembered that the arm does not have only these two muscles. Many other muscles are also present, which work at the same time — e.g. muscles which work to give direction to these movements. Similarly, there are more complex movements of the fingers and wrists, which are studied in detail in higher classes.

Biceps and triceps — flexors and extensors

An example of antagonistic movement is the biceps and triceps of the upper arm. When the lower arm is bent it is in the flexed state; when it hangs down it is in the extended state. When the biceps contracts, its insertion pulls the radius bone upward (the triceps relaxes) and the lower arm moves up — this is flexion, so the biceps is the flexor. When the triceps contracts, its insertion pulls the ulna bone back (the biceps relaxes) and the lower arm straightens — this is extension, so the triceps is the extensor.

Disorders: Osteoporosis and Arthritis

Section 13.7 — calcium deficiency, osteoporosis, and the causes, symptoms and treatment of arthritis.

Quick Notes

From day-to-day experience we know of many problems associated with the skeletal system: sometimes, during a cricket match, the muscle of a fast bowler gets pulled, the joint of a wrestler may get dislocated, and the bones of a person may be fractured in an accident. Some of the disorders of the skeletal system are described here briefly.

Osteoporosis

The process of mineralisation forms bones: calcium phosphate is deposited in the collagen fibres to make the bones hard. This calcium is absorbed from the food we eat. When a person is not taking sufficient calcium in their daily diet, their bones start becoming weak. Demineralisation is the process in which calcium moves from the bones to the blood in order to maintain the calcium level of the blood. If this situation prolongs, the bones become soft and fragile — this disorder is known as osteoporosis. Such bones start bending and can be broken easily. Osteoporosis occurs especially in old people and in women.

RememberOsteoporosis = weak, soft, fragile bones caused by calcium deficiency and demineralisation. Remember: demineralisation = calcium moves FROM the bones TO the blood.

Arthritis

Arthritis is the disorder of the joints in which they get inflamed. It is a degenerative disease that damages the joints permanently, causing pain, stiffness and swelling. In this disorder the membranes lining the joints thicken and fluid production decreases, which increases friction and causes severe pain; in later stages this leads to complete immobility, and the joints become permanently swollen and de-shaped. There are three types of arthritis: osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis and gouty arthritis.

  • Osteoarthritis is the most common type. It affects the cartilage, which starts to wear away over time; in extreme cases the cartilage wears away completely, leaving bone-on-bone contact. It occurs most often in older people and usually affects the weight-bearing joints (the joints of the vertebrae, knee and hip). It causes joint pain and limits a person’s normal range of mobility.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune disease in which the body’s immune system attacks healthy joints, which become inflamed and swollen. It can destroy the cartilage and bone within the joint. It usually affects the joints in the fingers, wrists, knees and elbows, causing pain, stiffness, swelling and loss of function.
  • Gouty arthritis is caused by higher-than-normal levels of uric acid in the blood and the deposit of large amounts of uric acid in the lining of the joints in the form of crystals. It causes inflammation called gout or gouty arthritis. It usually affects the joint at the base of the big toe; other joints (knees, wrists and fingers) may also be affected.

Treatment of arthritis focuses on relieving symptoms and improving joint function. Analgesics (pain killers) and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are used to treat arthritis. Surgery can also be performed, in which the affected joint is replaced with an artificial joint.

For Your InformationArthroplasty is a surgery that replaces a damaged, worn-out or diseased joint with an artificial joint. Most knee replacements are carried out on people between the ages of 60 and 80, and the most common reason is osteoarthritis.

Key Points and Activities

The textbook summary of the unit and the practical outline.

Textbook Key Points
  • A skeleton consists of a hard living substance which provides a framework for maintaining the body shape or support.
  • The human skeleton can be divided into two types — the axial skeleton and the appendicular skeleton.
  • The axial skeleton includes the skull, the vertebral column, and the ribs with the sternum.
  • The appendicular skeleton consists of the pectoral girdle, the pelvic girdle and the limbs (appendages).
  • The human skeleton is made up of two types of materials, i.e. bones and cartilage.
  • Cartilage is much softer than bone; it is also a form of connective tissue.
  • Joints are formed at the meeting point of two or more bones; they keep our bones together by holding them with each other.
  • On the basis of the extent of movement allowed by the joints, they are classified into three categories: immovable, slightly movable and freely movable joints.
  • There are three types of muscles in our body: cardiac muscles (which make up our heart), smooth muscles (found in the digestive, respiratory, circulatory and urinary tracts etc.) and skeletal muscles (attached to the bones of the skeleton).
  • The muscles of our body work in pairs; when one muscle of the pair contracts (shortens) the other relaxes (elongates) to perform a coordinated movement known as the antagonistic movement of muscles.
  • Although bones are the hardest parts of our body, sometimes our skeletal system becomes weak and results in deformations.

B. Short and Detailed Answers

127. Why are bones considered as dynamic structures?

Because bones are living tissue — they contain living cells (osteocytes), nerves and blood vessels. They can grow, adapt to stress and repair themselves after injury, and they constantly exchange minerals such as calcium with the blood (mineralisation and demineralisation). So they are not fixed, dead structures but dynamic, changing ones.

128. What is the contribution of Vesalius in understanding the human skeleton?

Andreas Vesalius wrote the first major book on human anatomy, "de Humani Corpris Fabrica" (The Fabric of the Human Body), in 1543. In it he explained the different parts of the human skeleton, including the number of bones, and described the source, position and function of each muscle. The book contained 277 anatomical illustrations by the artist Titian.

129. Describe the function of three major types of joints and give an example of each.

(i) Immovable joints — bones are fused and allow no movement; example: the joints of the skull. (ii) Slightly movable joints — allow a little movement; example: the joints of the vertebral column and ribs. (iii) Freely movable joints — allow free movement; examples: the hinge joint (elbow, knee) and the ball-and-socket joint (hip, shoulder).

130. How are different types of arthritis caused?

Osteoarthritis is caused by the wearing away of cartilage over time, especially in weight-bearing joints (age-related). Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks healthy joints. Gouty arthritis is caused by high levels of uric acid in the blood, which deposit as crystals in the joints.

131. Differentiate between a skeletal muscle’s origin and insertion.

The origin is the point where the muscle attaches to the fixed (immovable) bone, while the insertion is the point where the muscle attaches to the moving bone. When the muscle contracts, the insertion (moving bone) is pulled towards the origin.

132. State the functions of flexors and extensors.

A flexor bends a limb at a joint by decreasing the angle between the bones — for example, the biceps flexes the lower arm. An extensor straightens the limb by increasing the angle — for example, the triceps extends the lower arm. Flexors and extensors work together as an antagonistic pair.
