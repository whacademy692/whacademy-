Coordination and Control

Nervous & chemical coordination, the brain and spinal cord, the eye and ear, the endocrine glands, reflexes, feedback and nervous disorders — in exam-ready Q&A.

Learning Objectives

Coordination: nervous vs chemical, the mechanism (stimulus → receptor → coordinator → effector).
Neuron & nerves: structure and the three functional types.
Brain & spinal cord: forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain; reflex arc.
Receptors: the eye (vision, disorders) and the ear (hearing, balance).
Endocrine glands: pituitary, thyroid, pancreas, adrenal, gonads & their hormones.
Feedback & disorders: negative feedback, epilepsy & paralysis.

Part 1 — Coordination & Its Mechanism

Coordination in organisms

Define coordination.
  • Define coordination.
  • What is meant by coordination?

Coordination means to integrate among different parts of the body and to respond to stimuli in order to keep harmony with the environment.

Name the two types of coordination.
  • Name the two types of coordination.

Nervous coordination (by the nervous system) and chemical coordination (by hormones).

Which organisms have only chemical coordination?
  • Which organisms have only chemical coordination?
  • Do plants have nervous coordination?

Plants and other organisms such as unicellular organisms and fungi have only chemical coordination; animals possess both types.

Which coordination is faster and why?
  • Which coordination is faster and why?

Nervous coordination is faster because it works at the speed of electricity, while chemical coordination (by hormones) is slower.

Mechanism — stimulus, receptor, coordinator, effector

Define stimulus.
  • Define stimulus.

A stimulus is any change in the internal or external environment that can initiate a response.

What are receptors?
  • What are receptors?

Receptors are the parts that receive stimuli; the main receptors are the sense organs — eye, ear, skin, tongue and nose.

What are effectors? Give examples.
  • What are effectors? Give examples.

Effectors carry out the response; in nervous coordination they are muscles and glands.

Name the three phases of coordination.
  • Name the three phases of coordination.

Phase I — receiving stimulus; Phase II — message to coordinator; Phase III — producing response.

Part 2 — Neuron, Brain, Spinal Cord & PNS

The neuron & nerves

Define a neuron.
  • Define a neuron.
  • What is a neuron?

A neuron is the basic unit of structure and function of the nervous system, specialised to conduct nerve impulses. Neurons are also called nerve cells.

What is the function of dendrites and the axon?
  • What is the function of dendrites and the axon?

Dendrites bring impulses towards the cell body; the axon takes impulses away from the cell body.

What is the myelin sheath?
  • What is the myelin sheath?

A fatty white covering around the axon of large neurons that insulates it; it is broken at intervals called nodes.

Name the three types of neuron.
  • Name the three types of neuron.

Sensory neurons (sense organs → CNS), motor neurons (CNS → effectors) and associative neurons (link the two, in the CNS).

Define a nerve and name its three types.
  • Define a nerve and name its three types.

A nerve is a collection of axons. Types: sensory, motor and mixed nerves.

The brain

What is the CNS made of and how is it protected?
  • What is the CNS made of and how is it protected?

The CNS is the brain and spinal cord, protected by the cranium, vertebral column, the meninges, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).

Which is the largest part of the brain and its functions?
  • Which is the largest part of the brain and its functions?

The cerebrum — it controls intelligence, memory, voluntary movement, reasoning and speech.

What is the function of the cerebellum?
  • What is the function of the cerebellum?

The cerebellum controls balance and coordinates voluntary movements.

What does the medulla oblongata control?
  • What does the medulla oblongata control?

It controls automatic functions — breathing, heart rate, circulation, blood pressure, swallowing and vomiting.

What is the function of the thalamus and limbic system?
  • What is the function of the thalamus and limbic system?

The thalamus relays sensory information to the cerebrum; the limbic system (with the hypothalamus) controls emotions and helps form memories.

Spinal cord & peripheral nervous system

State two functions of the spinal cord.
  • State two functions of the spinal cord.

It transmits impulses between the body and brain, and is the centre of most reflex actions.

How many pairs of cranial and spinal nerves are there?
  • How many pairs of cranial and spinal nerves are there?

12 pairs of cranial nerves (from the brain) and 31 pairs of spinal nerves (from the spinal cord); all spinal nerves are mixed.

Name the two parts of the autonomic nervous system.
  • Name the two parts of the autonomic nervous system.

The sympathetic (fight or flight) and the parasympathetic (rest and digest) systems.

Reflex action

Define reflex action and reflex arc.
  • Define reflex action and reflex arc.

A reflex action is an automatic, quick, involuntary response to a stimulus. The reflex arc is the short neuron pathway through which it happens.

Trace the path of an impulse in a reflex.
  • Trace the path of an impulse in a reflex.

Stimulus → receptor → sensory neuron → associative neuron (CNS) → motor neuron → effector.

Part 3 — Receptors: The Eye & The Ear

The eye

Name the three layers of the eyeball.
  • Name the three layers of the eyeball.

The sclera (outer), choroid (middle) and retina (inner).

What is the function of the iris, pupil and lens?
  • What is the function of the iris, pupil and lens?

The iris controls pupil size; the pupil controls the light entering; the lens focuses images onto the retina.

What is the function of rods and cones?
  • What is the function of rods and cones?

Rods perceive dim light (no colour); cones perceive colour. Rods contain rhodopsin (from vitamin A).

What is the blind spot and the fovea?
  • What is the blind spot and the fovea?

The blind spot is where the optic nerve leaves the eye (no receptors); the fovea has densely packed cones and the greatest acuity.

Explain the mechanism of vision.
  • Explain the mechanism of vision.

Light through the cornea and lens forms an image on the retina; rods and cones make impulses that the optic nerve carries to the cerebrum, which forms the image.

Eye disorders & Muslim scientists

Define myopia and hypermetropia and their cures.
  • Define myopia and hypermetropia and their cures.

Myopia (short sightedness): eyeball too long, light in front of retina — cured by a concave lens. Hypermetropia (long sightedness): eyeball too short, light behind retina — cured by a convex lens.

What is night blindness and its cause?
  • What is night blindness and its cause?

Poor vision in dim light caused by vitamin A deficiency (not enough rhodopsin).

State the contribution of Ibn al-Haitham.
  • State the contribution of Ibn al-Haitham.

Called the father of modern optics; wrote Kitabul Manazir, described the eye’s anatomy, rejected that rays leave the eye, and made the first pinhole camera.

What did Ali bin Isa contribute?
  • What did Ali bin Isa contribute?

He wrote Tashkirat ul Kahhalin (Notebook of the Oculist) on over 100 eye diseases.

The ear

Name the three parts of the ear.
  • Name the three parts of the ear.

External ear, middle ear and internal (inner) ear.

Name the ear ossicles and the Eustachian tube’s role.
  • Name the ear ossicles and the Eustachian tube’s role.

Malleus, incus and stapes (smallest bones). The Eustachian tube equalises pressure in the middle ear.

Explain the mechanism of hearing.
  • Explain the mechanism of hearing.

Sound vibrates the ear drum → ossicles → oval window → cochlea; pressure waves bend the hair cells of the organ of Corti, sending impulses via the auditory nerve to the cerebrum.

How does the ear maintain balance?
  • How does the ear maintain balance?

The three semi-circular canals sense head movement; impulses go to the cerebellum, which signals the muscles to keep balance.

Part 4 — Chemical Coordination, Feedback & Disorders

Hormones & endocrine glands

Define a hormone and the endocrine system.
  • Define a hormone and the endocrine system.

A hormone is an organic compound made by an endocrine (ductless) gland and released into the blood to regulate body processes. The endocrine system is the set of about 20 such glands.

Why is the pituitary called the master gland?
  • Why is the pituitary called the master gland?

Because it controls other endocrine glands (TSH → thyroid, ACTH → adrenal cortex, LH → gonads).

Name the hormones of the thyroid and their functions.
  • Name the hormones of the thyroid and their functions.

Thyroxine (raises metabolism, contains iodine) and calcitonin (lowers blood calcium).

Why is the pancreas both exocrine and endocrine?
  • Why is the pancreas both exocrine and endocrine?

Exocrine — makes pancreatic juice; endocrine — its islets of Langerhans secrete insulin (lowers glucose) and glucagon (raises glucose).

Name the hormones of the adrenal medulla and gonads.
  • Name the hormones of the adrenal medulla and gonads.

Adrenal medulla: adrenaline and noradrenaline. Gonads: testosterone (testes), oestrogen and progesterone (ovaries).

Negative feedback & disorders

Explain the negative feedback control of insulin.
  • Explain the negative feedback control of insulin.

After a meal glucose rises; islets of Langerhans secrete insulin, which lowers glucose; when glucose returns to normal, insulin secretion stops.

Define epilepsy and its treatment.
  • Define epilepsy and its treatment.

A functional disorder with sudden excessive nerve-impulse discharge causing seizures; anti-epileptic drugs (AEDs) prevent seizures but do not cure it.

Define paralysis and its treatment.
  • Define paralysis and its treatment.

A vascular brain disorder (blood clot or burst vessel) causing loss of movement; no cure — physiotherapy and mobility aids help the patient adapt.

Key Vocabulary

HormoneA chemical messenger made by an endocrine gland and carried by the blood.

HypermetropiaLong sightedness (eyeball too short), corrected by a convex lens.

ParalysisA vascular brain disorder causing loss of movement.
