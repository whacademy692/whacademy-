Part 1 BIO-01

Introduction to Genetics

Section 15.1 — inheritance, heredity, genetics and genes.

Key points
  • Inheritance (heredity): the process by which characters are transferred from parents to offspring.
  • Genetics: the branch of biology that deals with the study of inheritance, variations and the factors controlling them.
  • Every cell contains instructions for making characters; these instructions are present in DNA in the form of chemical codes called genes. Cells use these instructions to produce particular proteins, and these proteins make the cell’s characters.

Inheritance, Heredity and Genes

1. Define genetics.

The branch of biology that deals with the study of inheritance, variations and the factors controlling them is called genetics.

2. Define inheritance.
• What is heredity?

The process by which characters are transferred from parents to offspring is called inheritance or heredity.

3. What is the science that enables the study of the pattern of inheritance?

Genetics is the science that enables the study of the various phenomena involved in deciding the pattern of inheritance.

4. Where are the instructions for making characters present in a cell?

They are present in the DNA in the form of chemical codes called genes.

5. What are genes?

Genes are the chemical codes present in DNA that carry the instructions a cell uses to produce particular proteins.

6. How do genes produce the characters of a cell?

Cells use the instructions in their DNA (genes) to produce particular proteins, and these proteins make the cell’s characters.

7. Give examples of physical and mental characteristics passed from one generation to the next.

Examples include eye colour, complexion of the skin, height and hair colour.

8. Why are offspring not identical to their parents?

Because along with similarities, children inherit some unique characteristics (variations) that make them different from their parents and from one another.

Key points
  • Chromosomes: thread-like structures present in cells; discovered by Waldeyer in 1876.
  • In prokaryotic cells chromosomes are not enclosed in a nuclear membrane; in eukaryotic cells chromosomes are found within the nucleus.
  • When a cell is not dividing, its chromatin is in the form of fine threads scattered in the nucleus. During cell division the chromatin coils and forms compact chromosomes.
  • Each chromosome consists of two identical halves; each half is called a chromatid.
  • The two chromatids are attached at a point called the centromere, which holds them together until they separate during cell division.

Key points
  • Gene: the fundamental physical and functional unit of heredity. A gene is a segment of DNA, consisting of a specific sequence and number of nucleotides, that has a code for protein synthesis.
  • A gene determines a trait or character, such as eye colour, earlobe shape and hair texture.
  • Genes are located on chromosomes and, like chromosomes, they are also in pairs; before dividing a cell duplicates its chromosomes, so its genes are also copied.
  • Locus: the location of a gene on a chromosome.
  • Allele: an alternative (different) form of the same gene. For example, the gene controlling height in a pea plant exists in two forms — one allele results in tallness and the other causes dwarfness.
  • Genotype: the combination of the alleles of a gene pair.
  • Homozygous: a genotype in which the gene pair has the same alleles.
  • Heterozygous: a genotype in which the gene pair has different alleles.
  • Dominant allele: in a heterozygous genotype, the allele that masks the working of the other allele.
  • Recessive allele: the allele that is masked (not expressed).
  • Phenotype: the observable outcome of a genotype, in the form of a characteristic.
  • Letters are used to represent alleles: capital letters refer to dominant alleles and lowercase letters refer to recessive alleles.

Gene and Allele

24. Define gene.

A gene is the fundamental physical and functional unit of heredity; it is a segment of DNA with a specific sequence of nucleotides that codes for protein synthesis.

25. Define allele.
• What are the alternative forms of a gene called?

The alternative (different) forms of the same gene are called alleles.

26. State the difference between a gene and an allele.

A gene is a segment of DNA that codes for a trait, while an allele is one of the alternative forms of that same gene.

27. Define locus.
• What is the location of a gene on a chromosome called?

The location of a gene on a chromosome is called its locus.

28. Define genotype.

The combination of the alleles of a gene pair is called the genotype.

29. Define homozygous.

A genotype in which the gene pair has the same alleles is called homozygous.

30. Define heterozygous.

A genotype in which the gene pair has different alleles is called heterozygous.

31. Give an example of alleles of a gene.

The gene controlling height in a pea plant exists in two alleles — one for tallness and one for dwarfness.

32. Give examples of traits determined by genes.

Genes determine traits such as eye colour, earlobe shape and hair texture.

33. Why are genes said to be in pairs?

Because genes are located on chromosomes, and since chromosomes are in pairs, the genes on them are also in pairs.

34. How are genes copied before cell division?

Before dividing, a cell duplicates its chromosomes; as genes lie on chromosomes, each daughter cell gets an equal distribution of copied genes.

35. Why is a gene called a unit of inheritance?

Because a gene is a segment of DNA that can be copied and passed on from parents to the next generation, carrying the code for a character.

Dominant and Recessive Alleles

36. Define dominant allele.

In a heterozygous genotype, the allele that masks the working of the other allele is called the dominant allele.

37. Define recessive allele.

The allele which is masked or not expressed in a heterozygous genotype is called the recessive allele.

38. Define phenotype.

The observable outcome of a genotype, in the form of a characteristic, is called the phenotype.

39. How are dominant and recessive alleles represented in letters?

Capital letters are used for dominant alleles and lowercase letters for recessive alleles.

40. Differentiate between genotype and phenotype.

Genotype is the combination of alleles present in an organism, while phenotype is its observable characteristic (the expression of the genotype).

41. Which allele is expressed in a heterozygous condition — dominant or recessive?

In a heterozygous condition the dominant allele is expressed and the recessive allele is masked.

42. Phenotype is the expression of which makeup?

Phenotype is the expression of the genetic makeup (genotype) of an organism.

Protein Synthesis and the Central Dogma

Section 15.2.4 — transcription, translation and the flow DNA → RNA → Protein (Fig. 15.3).

Central dogmaDNA → RNA → Protein
Transcription vs translation
FeatureTranscriptionTranslation

Key points
  • Protein synthesis takes place in two major steps: transcription and translation.
  • Transcription: the two strands of DNA unzip and separate; one strand acts as a template for making a single strand of RNA called messenger RNA (mRNA). The instructions in the DNA strand are copied onto the mRNA.
  • Translation: the mRNA leaves the nucleus and travels to the ribosome. The ribosome attaches with mRNA, and transfer RNA (tRNA) carries specific amino acids to the ribosome. The amino acids are linked in a specific sequence, eventually forming a protein.
  • Central dogma: DNA → RNA → Protein.

Transcription, Translation and the Central Dogma

The central dogma states the flow of genetic information as DNA → RNA → Protein.

A protein is formed when amino acids are linked together in a specific sequence.

Key points
  • Gregor Mendel, an Austrian monk, discovered the fundamental mechanism of inheritance. In the years 1856–1864 he performed his famous experiments on the garden pea plant.
  • Mendel is generally regarded as the “Father of Genetics”.
  • He chose the garden pea because it is easy to grow, fairly resistant to pests, has a short generation time (seed to seed in about three months), and includes pure-breeding strains.
  • A pea plant has both male and female reproductive parts and normally reproduces by self-pollination; it also has easily distinguishable contrasting characters (e.g. round vs wrinkled seeds, purple vs white flowers).
  • Mendel studied seven contrasting characters of the garden pea (seed form, cotyledon colour, flower colour, pod form, pod colour, stem place and stem size).
  • P generation = parental generation; F1 = first filial generation (from Latin filius, meaning son); F2 = second filial generation. F1 offspring of two parents with different characters are called hybrids.
  • Dominant trait: the trait that dominates and appears in the F1 generation. Recessive trait: the trait that is masked by the dominant trait in F1.

Key points
  • Mendel crossed a true-breeding tall plant with a true-breeding short plant; all F1 offspring were tall (Tt). The allele for tallness (T) is dominant over the allele for shortness (t).
  • He let the F1 plants self-pollinate to produce the F2 generation. The recessive (short) form reappeared: about one-fourth of F2 plants were short and three-fourths were tall — a 3:1 ratio of dominant to recessive.
  • In the F2 generation: 25% received both shortness alleles (tt, short), 50% received Tt (tall), and 25% received both tallness alleles (TT, tall).
  • The 3:1 monohybrid F2 phenotypic ratio is evidence of the segregation of alleles.
  • In a dihybrid cross the inheritance of two characters is studied at a time.
  • Mendel crossed true-breeding round yellow seeds (RRYY) with wrinkled green seeds (rryy). All F1 plants had round yellow seeds (RrYy), showing only the dominant characters.
  • The F1 (RrYy) plants form four types of gametes: RY, Ry, rY and ry. On self-pollination there are 16 combinations of alleles in F2.
  • The F2 generation appeared in the ratio 9:3:3:1 — 9/16 round yellow, 3/16 round green, 3/16 wrinkled yellow, 1/16 wrinkled green.
  • This shows that alleles R and r segregated independently of alleles Y and y — the law of independent assortment.

Law of Segregation — Monohybrid Cross

63. State Mendel’s law of segregation.

The paired genes (alleles) are separated during gamete formation and each gamete receives one or the other allele, but not both.

64. What is a monohybrid cross?

A cross in which the inheritance of only one character (one pair of contrasting traits) is studied at a time is called a monohybrid cross.

65. What is the phenotypic ratio of a monohybrid cross in F2?

The phenotypic ratio of a monohybrid cross in the F2 generation is 3:1 (3 dominant : 1 recessive).

66. What is the genotypic ratio of a monohybrid cross in F2?

The genotypic ratio in F2 is 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt.

67. Of what is the 3:1 F2 ratio an evidence?

The 3:1 monohybrid F2 phenotypic ratio is an evidence of the segregation of alleles.

68. When a true-breeding tall pea is crossed with a true-breeding short pea, what is the F1 phenotype?

All the F1 offspring are tall (Tt), because the allele for tallness is dominant.

69. What percentage of F2 plants are short in a monohybrid cross?

About 25% (one-fourth) of the F2 plants are short (tt).

70. What percentage of F2 plants are tall in a monohybrid cross?

About 75% (three-fourths) of the F2 plants are tall — 25% TT and 50% Tt.

71. Which allele is dominant in the pea plant — tallness or shortness?

The allele for tallness (T) is dominant and the allele for shortness (t) is recessive.

72. Why did the short character reappear in the F2 generation?

Because the recessive (short) allele, which was masked in F1, segregated and some F2 plants received both recessive alleles (tt).

73. What are true-breeding (pure-breeding) plants?

True-breeding plants are genetically homozygous and produce offspring with the same character on self-pollination.

74. In a Tt F1 plant, what are the genotypes of the gametes formed?

A Tt plant forms two kinds of gametes — one carrying T and the other carrying t.

Law of Independent Assortment — Dihybrid Cross

75. State Mendel’s law of independent assortment.

Alleles separate independently of one another during the formation of gametes.

76. What is a dihybrid cross?

A cross in which the inheritance of two characters is studied at the same time is called a dihybrid cross.

77. What is the phenotypic ratio of a dihybrid cross in F2?

The phenotypic ratio of a dihybrid cross in the F2 generation is 9:3:3:1.

78. Of what is the 9:3:3:1 F2 ratio an evidence?

The 9:3:3:1 dihybrid F2 phenotypic ratio is an evidence of independent assortment of alleles.

79. How many types of gametes does an RrYy plant produce?

An RrYy plant produces four types of gametes: RY, Ry, rY and ry.

80. How many types of gametes will a plant having genotype AaBb produce?

A plant having the genotype AaBb will produce four types of gametes (AB, Ab, aB, ab).

81. How many combinations of alleles appear in the F2 of a dihybrid cross?

There are 16 combinations of alleles in the F2 generation of a dihybrid cross.

82. What were the parent genotypes in Mendel’s dihybrid cross?

He crossed round yellow seeds (RRYY) with wrinkled green seeds (rryy).

83. What was the phenotype of the F1 generation in the dihybrid cross?

All the F1 plants had round yellow seeds (RrYy) — the dominant characters.

84. Write the four phenotypic classes of the 9:3:3:1 F2 ratio.

9/16 round yellow, 3/16 round green, 3/16 wrinkled yellow and 1/16 wrinkled green.

For Your InformationScientific investigation is a pursuit to find the answer to a question using the scientific method. The scientific method is a systematic process that uses measurable observations to formulate, test or modify a hypothesis. In genetics the information is in the form of large mathematical data, so knowledge of mathematics is required for data analysis.
Key points
  • Dominance relations are of three types: complete dominance, incomplete dominance and co-dominance.
  • Complete dominance: one allele is completely dominant over the other, e.g. T (tallness) is completely dominant over t (dwarfness), so in Tt the plant appears tall.
  • Incomplete dominance: an allele does not completely mask the other; the heterozygous individual shows a blending/mixing of both phenotypes (intermediate).
  • Example of incomplete dominance — Japanese 4 O’Clock plant: red (R) and white (r). Red (RR) self-pollinates to red, white (rr) to white, but a red × white cross gives all pink (Rr) F1. When pink × pink (Rr × Rr) is crossed, the F2 genotypic ratio is 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr, giving 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white.
  • Co-dominance: different alleles of a gene are completely expressed together in the heterozygous condition (not intermediate).
  • Example of co-dominance — ABO blood group system: there are three alleles IA, IB and i. Allele IA makes antigen A, IB makes antigen B, and i makes no antigen. IA and IB are both dominant over i.
  • Blood groups: IAIA or IAi = A; IBIB or IBi = B; ii = O; IAIB = AB (co-dominance, both antigens A and B are produced).

Dominance Relations and Blood Groups

85. Name the three types of dominance relations.

The three types of dominance relations are complete dominance, incomplete dominance and co-dominance.

86. Define complete dominance.

Complete dominance is the condition in which one allele is completely dominant over the other and masks its effect, e.g. T over t.

87. Define incomplete dominance.

Incomplete dominance is the condition in which an allele does not completely mask the other, and the heterozygote shows a blending (intermediate) of both phenotypes.

88. Define co-dominance.

Co-dominance is the condition in which different alleles of a gene are completely expressed together in the heterozygous condition.

89. Give an example of incomplete dominance.

The inheritance of flower colour in the Japanese 4 O’Clock plant is an example of incomplete dominance.

90. What is the F1 phenotype when a red 4 O’Clock plant is crossed with a white one?

All the F1 offspring have pink flowers (Rr), showing incomplete dominance.

91. What is the F2 genotypic ratio when two pink 4 O’Clock plants are crossed?

The F2 genotypic ratio is 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr, giving 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white.

92. Give an example of co-dominance.

The ABO blood group system shows co-dominance between the IA and IB alleles.

93. How many alleles control the ABO blood group trait? Name them.

Three alleles control the ABO blood group trait: IA, IB and i.

94. Which antigens are made by the IA and IB alleles?

Allele IA makes antigen A on RBCs and allele IB makes antigen B on RBCs.

95. Does the i allele produce any antigen?

No, the i allele does not produce any antigen on RBCs.

96. Write the genotypes for blood group A.

Blood group A has the genotype IAIA or IAi.

97. Write the genotype for blood group O.

Blood group O has the genotype ii (homozygous recessive).

98. Write the genotype for blood group AB and name the dominance relation it shows.

Blood group AB has the genotype IAIB and shows co-dominance, as both antigens A and B are produced.

99. Differentiate between incomplete dominance and co-dominance.

In incomplete dominance the heterozygous offspring is intermediate between both parents, whereas in co-dominance the heterozygote shows the phenotypes of both parents together.

100. Which alleles of the ABO system are dominant over i?

Both the IA and IB alleles are dominant over the i allele.

Key points
  • Variation: differences in traits between individuals of the same species. Variations are necessary for natural selection.
  • a. Genetic recombination of chromosomes: during meiosis the homologous chromosomes separate and move independently to different nuclei, producing new combinations of chromosomes in gametes.
  • b. Crossing over: during prophase of meiosis the chromatids of homologous chromosomes exchange genes, producing chromosomes with different (recombinant) combinations of genes.
  • c. Random fertilization: each gamete has a unique genetic composition, so when gametes combine randomly, the organisms produced are different from all others of the species.
  • d. Mutation: any change in the nucleotide sequence of DNA. The original allele is the wild type and the new allele is the mutant allele. If there were no mutations, there would be no variation.
  • e. Environmental conditions: the environment has no role in the initial production of variations, but once produced, individuals with suitable variations survive and reproduce better.
  • Continuous variations: deal with a wide range of phenotype ranging from one extreme to the other, with many intermediate characters. They are controlled by many genes. Examples: skin colour, height, weight and IQ.
  • Discontinuous variations: show only a few clear-cut phenotypes with no intermediates, and are controlled by a few genes. Examples: human blood groups (A, B, AB, O) and ear lobes (free or attached).

Sources of Variation

101. Define variation.

Variation is the difference in traits between individuals of the same species.

102. Why are variations important?

Variations are necessary for natural selection and enable organisms to adapt and survive when the environment changes.

103. Name the sources of variation.

The sources of variation are genetic recombination of chromosomes, crossing over, random fertilization, mutation and environmental conditions.

104. How does genetic recombination of chromosomes produce variation?

During meiosis, homologous chromosomes separate and move independently to different gametes, producing new combinations of chromosomes.

105. Define crossing over.
• How does crossing over bring variations?

Crossing over is the exchange of genes between chromatids of homologous chromosomes during prophase of meiosis, which produces recombinant chromosomes with new gene combinations.

Because each gamete has a unique genetic composition, random combining of gametes during fertilization produces offspring different from all others.

A mutation is any change in the nucleotide sequence of DNA.

108. What are wild type and mutant alleles?

The original allele is called the wild type and the new allele produced by mutation is called the mutant allele.

If there were no mutations, there would be no variation.

The environment has no role in the initial production of variations, but individuals with suitable variations are best suited to survive and reproduce.

111. Relate meiosis with variation.

Meiosis produces variation through independent separation of homologous chromosomes and through crossing over, giving gametes with new combinations of genes.

112. During which phase of meiosis does crossing over occur?

Crossing over occurs during the prophase of meiosis.

Evolution, Natural & Artificial Selection

Sections 15.4.3 and 15.4.4 — organic evolution, Darwin’s natural selection and artificial selection (Figs 15.14–15.16).

Steps of natural selection

1. OverproductionOrganisms produce more offspring than the resources of the environment can support

3. ReproductionBetter-adapted individuals survive and pass on favourable variations
4. SpeciationOver many generations the population changes entirely and new species may evolve

For Your InformationDarwin’s theory suggests that unicellular organisms evolved into multicellular organisms. One of the recent theories of evolution, the theory of intelligent design, proposes that certain features of the universe and of living organisms are best explained by an intelligent cause and not by an undirected process such as natural selection. The textbook concludes that there is a Creator of the universe — Allah.
Key points
  • Evolution means gradual change over time. Organic evolution is defined as the development of advanced types of organisms from pre-existing simpler and imperfect forms by gradual modification over time.
  • Charles Darwin (1809–1882), an English naturalist, presented evidence that evolution occurs and developed the theory of natural selection to explain it. Natural selection is the mechanism of evolution.
  • Natural selection: the process through which individuals with better characters (variations) produce more surviving offspring than individuals lacking these variations.
  • Natural selection works in the following way: (1) Overproduction — organisms produce more offspring than can survive; (2) Competition — organisms compete for limited resources, and individuals with poor adaptability are excluded (this is natural selection); (3) Reproduction — better-adapted individuals survive and pass on favourable variations; (4) Speciation — over many generations the population changes entirely and new species may evolve.
  • Example of natural selection: evolution of pesticide resistance in insects. In the early 1950s a small concentration of pesticide controlled flour beetles, but survivors had variations for resistance, which they passed on; today flour beetles are not killed even by very high concentrations of the same pesticide.
  • Artificial selection: the process in which people (instead of nature) allow only the plants and animals with desirable characteristics to reproduce, causing the evolution of farm stock.
  • The varieties of animals which are artificially bred are called breeds; e.g. breeds of sheep, goat, cow and hen produced to increase meat, milk, eggs and wool.
  • The varieties of plants which are bred are called varieties or cultivars; e.g. improved varieties of wheat, rice, potato and apple.
  • Major cereals (rice, wheat, maize, sorghum and millet) were all developed from wild grasses by artificial selection between 7,000 and 12,000 years ago.

B. Short Answers

B1. If a man with type AB blood group marries a woman with type O blood group, what type of blood group do you expect in the offsprings?

Ans: Man = AB (IAIB), woman = O (ii). The gametes of the man are IA and IB; the gametes of the woman are i and i.

Possible offspring genotypes: IAi (blood group A) and IBi (blood group B).

So the offspring can have blood group A or blood group B (50% A and 50% B) — none will be AB or O.

B2. How can crossing (crossing over) bring variations?

Ans: During the prophase of meiosis, the chromatids of homologous chromosomes exchange genes (crossing over). This produces recombinant chromosomes with new combinations of genes, so the gametes — and the offspring formed from them — show variations.

B3. How are DNA and proteins packed to form a chromosome?

Ans: A long molecule of DNA is wrapped around bundles of histone proteins to form nucleosomes. These nucleosomes are arranged as “beads on a string”, and this string of beads coils further to form the compact structure of a chromosome.
