Part 1 BIO-01

Homeostasis, Osmoregulation and Thermoregulation

Introduction — what homeostasis is and the three regulations.

Osmoregulation vs excretion vs thermoregulation
TermMeaningWhat is regulated / removed
HomeostasisAbility to maintain the internal environment constantWater, solutes and temperature
OsmoregulationProcess by which the amount of water and solutes is maintained in the bodyWater-solute (water-salt) balance
ExcretionElimination of harmful solutes and extra water from the bodyNitrogenous wastes and extra water
ThermoregulationMaintenance of internal body temperature within a tolerable rangeBody temperature
For Your InformationThe terms internal environment and external environment were coined by Claude Bernard a century ago.
Common MistakeDo not mix up the three regulations. Osmoregulation controls the amount of water and solutes, excretion removes harmful wastes and extra water, and thermoregulation controls body temperature. All three are parts of homeostasis.

Homeostasis and the Internal Environment

1. Define homeostasis.
• What is homeostasis?

The ability to maintain the internal environment constant or nearly constant is called homeostasis.

2. Why do organisms need homeostasis?
• What is the importance of homeostasis?

The external environment keeps changing and affects the internal environment. By keeping the internal environment constant, homeostasis helps an organism to survive and utilise its environment in the best possible way.

3. Who coined the terms “internal environment” and “external environment”?

Claude Bernard coined these terms a century ago.

4. Name the most important components of the internal environment that are readily affected by the external environment.

Water, solutes and temperature.

5. Of what are body fluids made up?

Body fluids are made up of water and solutes.

Osmoregulation and Excretion

6. What is the water-solute balance commonly called?
• Why is the water-solute balance of body fluids important?

The water-solute balance is commonly called the water-salt balance. It is very important, and organisms maintain it during homeostasis.

7. Define osmoregulation.
• What is osmoregulation?

The process by which the amount of water and solutes are maintained in the body is called osmoregulation (regulation of the amount of water in body fluids).

8. Define excretion.
• What is meant by excretion?

Excretion is the elimination of harmful solutes (e.g. nitrogenous wastes) and extra water from the body.

Thermoregulation and Comparison

9. Define thermoregulation.
• What is thermoregulation?

The maintenance of the internal body temperature within a tolerable range is called thermoregulation.

10. Why is thermoregulation necessary for living organisms?

All metabolic reactions are catalysed by enzymes and each enzyme works best at a suitable body temperature. Since changes in external temperature affect the internal temperature, organisms keep it within a tolerable range.

11. Differentiate between osmoregulation and thermoregulation.

Osmoregulation: the process by which the amount of water and solutes is maintained in the body.

Thermoregulation: the process by which the internal body temperature is maintained within a tolerable range.

Part 2 BIO-02

Osmoregulation in Plants

Section 11.1.1 — mesophytes, hydrophytes, xerophytes and halophytes.

Four Groups of Plants

12. On what basis are plants divided into four groups for osmoregulation?

On the basis of the availability of water.

13. Name the four groups of plants on the basis of the availability of water.

Mesophytes, hydrophytes, xerophytes and halophytes.

Mesophytes

14. Define mesophytes.
• What are mesophytes? Where do they live?

Mesophytes are the terrestrial plants which live where a sufficient quantity of water is available.

15. What is the meaning of the word “mesophyte”?

Meso means medium and phyte means plant.

16. Give two examples of mesophytes.

Maize (corn), clover and rose are examples of mesophytes.

17. How do mesophytes absorb water?

Mesophytes absorb water through their roots.

18. How do mesophytes control the loss of water?
• How do mesophytes osmoregulate when water is plentiful and when it is scarce?

When water supply is sufficient, they keep their stomata open to promote the loss of excess water. When they face a shortage of water, they control extra transpiration by closing their stomata. Their waxy cuticle also prevents water loss.

Hydrophytes

19. Define hydrophytes.
• What are hydrophytes? Where do they live?

Hydrophytes are the plants which live in freshwater (ponds, rivers, streams, lakes etc.) or in wet soil.

20. What is the meaning of the word “hydrophyte”?

Hydro means water and phyte means plant.

21. Why do hydrophytes need to remove extra water from their bodies?

Water keeps on entering their bodies through their whole surface, so they must remove the extra water.

22. How do hydrophytes remove extra water?
• Describe the adaptation of hydrophytes for osmoregulation.

Many hydrophytes have broad leaves that float on the surface of the water. These leaves have a large number of stomata on their upper surface, through which water moves out of their bodies.

23. Name the most common example of hydrophytes.

The water lily.

Xerophytes

24. Define xerophytes.
• What are xerophytes? Where do they live?

Xerophytes are the plants which live in dry environments (e.g. deserts).

25. What is the meaning of the word “xerophyte”?

Xero means dry and phyte means plant.

26. Why do xerophytes have deep roots?

Deep roots help xerophytes to absorb water from almost dry soil.

27. Why do xerophytes have very few stomata and a thick waxy cuticle?

Both features reduce the loss of water from their bodies.

28. What are succulent organs?

The soft and juicy stems or roots in which some xerophytes (e.g. cacti) store water are called succulent organs.

29. Name an example of xerophytes. What is stored in their thick stems?

Cacti (singular: cactus). Their thick stems store water and also carry out photosynthesis.

Halophytes

30. Define halophytes.
• What are halophytes? Where do they live?

Halophytes are the plants which live in habitats with salty waters (e.g. sea or salty marshes).

31. What is the meaning of the word “halophyte”?

Halo means salt and phyte means plant.

32. Why does water tend to move out of the bodies of halophytes?

Their bodies have less salt concentration as compared to the external environment, so water tries to move out into the environment.

33. How do halophytes prevent water from moving out of their cells?

Halophytes absorb salts from the external water by active transport, so water does not move out of the cells.

34. How do halophytes get rid of excess salt?

The excess salt is either stored in cells or excreted out from salt glands on the leaves.

35. Give an example of halophytes.

Many sea grasses are halophytes.

Fig. 11.1: mesophytes (maize). Fig. 11.2: hydrophytes (water lilies). Fig. 11.3: xerophytes (thick stems that store water and carry out photosynthesis).

Common MistakeTranspiration and guttation are not the same. Transpiration is loss of water as vapours (mainly by day); guttation is loss of water as liquid drops from pores at the tips or edges of leaves (at night).

Excretion in Plants: Overview

37. Do plants have special organs for excretion?
• How do plants excrete wastes?

No, plants do not possess any special organs for excretion. They get rid of different wastes in different ways to maintain their internal environment.

38. Name the materials that plants need to excrete.

Carbon dioxide produced during respiration, oxygen and water released as by-products of photosynthesis, and some metabolic products like latex, resins and gums.

Carbon Dioxide and Oxygen

39. How is carbon dioxide excreted in plants by day and at night?

By day, CO₂ is retained in the leaves and used for photosynthesis. At night, when there is no photosynthesis, it is excreted through the open stomata of the leaves.

40. What happens to the oxygen produced in photosynthesis during the day?

It is utilised in cellular respiration. The extra oxygen is released out through the stomata.

Extra Water: Vacuoles and Transpiration

41. Where do plants store large amounts of water?

In the vacuoles of their cells.

42. What is the result of storing water in vacuoles?

It results in turgor, which provides support to the soft parts of the body.

43. In how many ways do plants remove extra water? Name them.

In two ways: transpiration and guttation.

44. Define transpiration.
• What is transpiration?

Transpiration is the loss of water from the plant surface in the form of vapours.

45. Name the three types of transpiration.

Stomatal transpiration, cuticular transpiration and lenticular transpiration.

46. Through which structures do stomatal, cuticular and lenticular transpiration occur?

Stomatal transpiration occurs through the stomata, cuticular transpiration through the cuticle, and lenticular transpiration through the lenticels.

47. When do plants remove extra water by transpiration?

During the day.

Guttation

48. Define guttation.
• What is guttation?

The removal of extra water in the form of little drops through small pores at the tips or edges of leaves, mostly at night, is called guttation.

49. Why do some plants use guttation?

Transpiration does not occur at night, so some plants such as grasses use guttation to remove extra water at night.

50. Name the plants that show guttation.

Some plants such as grasses.

51. Through which structures does water come out in guttation?

Through small pores at the tips or edges of the leaves.

52. Differentiate between transpiration and guttation.

Transpiration: loss of water as vapours, during the day, through stomata, cuticle and lenticels.

Guttation: loss of water as little drops, at night, through small pores at the tips or edges of leaves.

Latex, Resins and Gums

53. How do plants excrete other wastes?

Some store wastes in stems, leaves or roots as harmless crystals; some store wastes in leaves, which are lost when the leaves fall; and some excrete wastes through special pores by applying force.

54. How does a plant get rid of wastes stored in its leaves?

When the leaves fall, the plant body also gets rid of these wastes.

55. Name the substances excreted by the rubber plant, Acacia tree, coniferous trees and ladyfinger.

The rubber plant excretes latex, the Acacia tree gums, coniferous trees resins, and the ladyfinger mucilage.

56. Why are latex, gums and resins important?

They are very important plant products, economically very important, and are used in many industrial products.

Stomatal transpiration, cuticular transpiration and lenticular transpiration.

58. What does Fig. 11.6 show?

Lungs as Homeostatic Organs

59. Name the main organs involved in homeostasis in human beings.

The skin, lungs and kidneys.

60. What is produced in every cell along with energy during cellular respiration?

Carbon dioxide and water.

61. Why is carbon dioxide a harmful waste for the body?
• Why must the body remove carbon dioxide?

CO₂ is an acidic gas, so its accumulation can make the blood and other body tissues more acidic.

62. How do the lungs act as homeostatic organs?
• How do the lungs keep the carbon dioxide concentration down to a certain level?

Blood transports CO₂ from the cells to the lungs. The CO₂ diffuses from the blood into the air in the alveoli, and the air is then exhaled, so carbon dioxide is removed from the body.

63. By which process does carbon dioxide pass from the blood into the alveoli?

By diffusion.

Part 5 BIO-05

Skin and Thermoregulation

Section 11.2 (b) — the skin in cold and warm conditions, and fever.

How the skin regulates body temperature (Fig. 11.7)
ConditionWhat the skin doesResult
ColdMuscles at the base of hair contract; hairs stand up; goose bumps formHair and goose bumps make a blanket of warm air; body heat does not go out
WarmSweat glands produce sweatExcess body heat escapes by evaporation (cooling effect); some water, salts and nitrogenous wastes are removed
Do You Know?Dogs have fewer sweat glands in their skin. Hence they keep their tongue hanging outside their mouth to increase the evaporation of water, which in response produces a cooling effect.
For Your Information (Fever)In bacterial and viral infections, leukocytes increase in number. When leukocytes kill pathogens, special chemicals are produced. These chemicals displace the set point of the hypothalamus of the brain above the normal point of 37 °C. The brain sends messages to body parts to produce heat. It results in an increase in body temperature. This condition is called fever. Fever helps in stimulating the protective mechanisms against the pathogens.

Skin as the Organ of Thermoregulation

64. Why is the skin called an organ of thermoregulation?
• What is the role of the skin of mammals in homeostasis?

The skin of human beings and other mammals has been adapted as the organ for thermoregulation.

65. What is the function of the thin layer of fat cells beneath the epidermis?

It does not allow heat to enter or leave the body.

In Cold Conditions

66. How does the skin keep the body warm in cold conditions?

The muscles at the base of the hair contract, so the hairs stand up and goose bumps form. The hair and goose bumps make a blanket of warm air that does not allow the body’s heat to go out.

67. What are goose bumps?
• How are goose bumps formed?

Goose bumps are the bumps formed on the skin when the muscles attached at the base of the hair contract and the hairs stand up in cold conditions.

In Warm Conditions

68. How does the skin cool the body in warm conditions?

The sweat glands produce sweat. Excess body heat escapes through the evaporation of sweat, which gives a cooling effect.

69. Besides cooling, what else does sweating do?

Sweating also helps in the removal of some extra amounts of water, salts and nitrogenous wastes from the body.

70. Why do dogs keep their tongue hanging outside their mouth?

Dogs have fewer sweat glands in their skin. A hanging tongue increases the evaporation of water, which produces a cooling effect.

71. Name the parts of the human skin labelled in Fig. 11.7.

Epidermis, dermis, fatty tissue, blood vessels, follicle, oil gland, sweat gland and melanocytes.

Fever

72. What is fever?

Fever is an increase in body temperature that occurs when the set point of the hypothalamus is displaced above the normal point of 37 °C during infections.

73. What causes fever?

In bacterial and viral infections, leukocytes increase and kill pathogens, producing special chemicals. These chemicals displace the set point of the hypothalamus above 37 °C, and the brain sends messages to body parts to produce heat.

74. What is the normal set point of the hypothalamus?

37 °C. In fever, the set point of the hypothalamus of the brain is displaced above this normal point.

75. How is fever helpful for the body?

Fever helps in stimulating the protective mechanisms against the pathogens.

Part 6 BIO-06

Kidneys as Homeostatic Organs

Section 11.2 (c) — the kidneys control the composition of blood; the role of the liver.

How the kidneys control the composition of blood
Condition of bloodWhat the kidneys do
More solutes in the bloodExcrete the extra solutes and retain water in the body

For Your InformationThe liver also plays a role in homeostasis. It breaks the haemoglobin of dead red blood cells. The products of haemoglobin breakdown are sent to kidneys for removal. The liver changes ammonia (produced in the metabolism of proteins) to urea. Urea is then removed from the body by kidneys.

Kidneys and Osmoregulation

76. Which are the most important organs of homeostasis in humans?

The kidneys are the most important organs of homeostasis in human beings and other animals.

77. Of which system are the kidneys a part?

The kidneys are part of the urinary system.

78. What do the kidneys filter from the blood?

The kidneys filter the nitrogenous wastes from the blood and excrete them from the body.

79. What do kidneys do when there are more solutes in the blood?

They excrete the extra solutes and retain water in the body.

80. What do kidneys do when there is more water in the blood?

They produce more urine to remove the excess water from the body.

81. How do kidneys perform osmoregulation?

By controlling the water-solute balance of the body: excreting extra solutes when solutes are high, and producing more urine when water is in excess.

Role of the Liver

82. What is the role of the liver in homeostasis?

The liver breaks down the haemoglobin of dead red blood cells and sends the products to the kidneys for removal. It also changes ammonia into urea, which is removed from the body by the kidneys.

83. Name the nitrogenous waste that the liver forms from ammonia.

Urea. Ammonia is produced in the metabolism of proteins, and the liver changes it to urea.

Organs of the Urinary System

84. Name the organs of the human urinary system.

A pair of kidneys, a pair of ureters, a urinary bladder and a urethra.

85. What is another name for the urinary system?

The urinary system is also called the excretory system.

86. What is the function of the kidneys in the urinary system?

The kidneys remove extra water, salts and nitrogenous wastes from the blood and produce urine.

87. What is a ureter? What is its function?

A ureter is a tube, one from each kidney, which carries urine to the urinary bladder.

88. What is the function of the urinary bladder?

The urinary bladder temporarily stores urine.

89. What is the urethra?

The urethra is the tube that carries urine from the urinary bladder to the outside.

Ureter vs Urethra and the Path of Urine

90. Differentiate between ureter and urethra.

Ureter: carries urine from each kidney to the urinary bladder (one pair).

Urethra: carries urine from the urinary bladder to the outside of the body.

91. Trace the path of urine from the kidney to the outside of the body.

Kidney → ureter → urinary bladder (stored temporarily) → urethra → outside the body.

92. Name the parts labelled in Fig. 11.8.

Hilus, kidney, ureter, urinary bladder and urethra.

Position, Shape and Size

93. Where are the kidneys located in the human body?

The kidneys are present below the diaphragm in the abdominal cavity, on the sides of the vertebral column, attached to the dorsal body wall.

94. Which kidney is slightly lower and smaller?

The right kidney is slightly lower and smaller than the left kidney.

95. Describe the colour and shape of the kidney.

A kidney is a dark brown, bean-shaped organ with a concave and a convex side.

96. Which side of the kidney faces the vertebral column?

The concave side of each kidney is towards the vertebral column.

97. What is the size of a human kidney?

Each kidney is about 10 cm long, 5 cm wide and 4 cm thick.

98. What fraction of the body weight and of the blood supply do the kidneys have?

The weight of both kidneys is less than 1% of the total body weight, but they receive 20% of the blood supplied to the body with each heartbeat.

Coverings and Regions of the Kidney

99. What is the renal capsule?

The renal capsule is the tissue in which each kidney is enclosed.

100. What is the hilus?

The hilus is the depression on the concave side of the kidney. It is the point where the renal artery enters the kidney and the renal vein and ureter leave the kidney.

101. Name the two regions of the kidney seen in a longitudinal section.

The outer region is the renal cortex and the inner region is the renal medulla.

102. What are renal pyramids?

Renal pyramids are the cone-shaped areas in the renal medulla. The base of each faces the renal cortex and its tip lies in the renal pelvis.

103. What is the renal pelvis?

The renal pelvis is the cavity in which the tips of the renal pyramids lie. It extends to the outside of the kidney and forms the ureter.

104. Name the parts labelled in Fig. 11.9.

Renal artery, renal vein, renal pelvis, ureter, renal pyramid, renal cortex, renal capsule and renal medulla.

105. Differentiate between the renal cortex and the renal medulla.

Renal cortex: the outer region of the kidney, faced by the bases of the pyramids.

Renal medulla: the inner region of the kidney, containing the cone-shaped renal pyramids.

The Nephron and the Renal Corpuscle

106. What is the functional unit of the kidney?
• State that the nephron is the excretory unit of the kidney.

The functional unit of the kidney is the nephron.

107. How many nephrons are present in each kidney?

More than one million nephrons are present in each kidney.

108. Name the two parts of a nephron.

The renal corpuscle and the renal tubule.

109. What is the renal corpuscle?

The renal corpuscle is the first part of the nephron. It is composed of the glomerulus and the Bowman’s capsule.

110. What is the glomerulus?

The glomerulus is a network of capillaries.

111. What is the Bowman’s capsule?

The Bowman’s capsule is the cup-shaped structure around the glomerulus.

Renal Tubule and Collecting Duct

112. What is the renal tubule?

The renal tubule is a long tube attached with the Bowman’s capsule. It has three parts.

113. Name the three parts of the renal tubule.

The proximal convoluted tubule, the Loop of Henle and the distal convoluted tubule.

114. Describe the shape of the proximal convoluted tubule, Loop of Henle and distal convoluted tubule.

The first part (proximal convoluted tubule) is convoluted; the middle part (Loop of Henle) is U-shaped; and the last part (distal convoluted tubule) is again convoluted.

115. What is a collecting duct? Where does it open?

The distal convoluted tubules of many nephrons open into a single collecting duct. Many collecting ducts join together and open into the renal pelvis.

116. Name the parts labelled in Fig. 11.10.

Renal corpuscle (glomerulus and Bowman’s capsule), arteriole, venule, blood capillaries, proximal convoluted tubule, distal convoluted tubule, Loop of Henle, collecting duct, and the direction towards the ureter.

Blood Supply and Adaptations

117. Which blood vessel enters the kidney and which vessels leave it at the hilus?

The renal artery enters the kidney at the hilus; the renal vein and the ureter leave the kidney there.

118. How is the glomerulus suited for its function?

C. Detailed Answers

220. Describe the structure of kidneys in human beings.

Position and shape: human beings have a pair of dark brown, bean-shaped kidneys with a concave and a convex side. They lie below the diaphragm in the abdominal cavity, on the sides of the vertebral column, attached to the dorsal body wall; the right kidney is slightly lower and smaller than the left. Each is about 10 cm long, 5 cm wide and 4 cm thick.

Covering and hilus: each kidney is enclosed in a tissue called the renal capsule. On the concave side there is a depression called the hilus, where the renal artery enters and the renal vein and ureter leave the kidney.

Internal structure: a longitudinal section shows an outer renal cortex and an inner renal medulla. The medulla contains many cone-shaped renal pyramids whose bases face the cortex and whose tips lie in a cavity called the renal pelvis; the pelvis extends outside the kidney and forms the ureter.

Nephrons: the functional units of the kidney are the nephrons — more than one million in each kidney. Each nephron has a renal corpuscle (glomerulus and Bowman’s capsule) and a renal tubule (proximal convoluted tubule, Loop of Henle and distal convoluted tubule). The distal convoluted tubules of many nephrons open in a collecting duct, and many collecting ducts open into the renal pelvis.

221. Why do plants excrete? What are the different mechanisms through which plants excrete different substances?

Why: plants excrete to get rid of unwanted materials and to maintain their internal environment. They have no special excretory organs.

Carbon dioxide and oxygen: by day, CO₂ is retained in the leaves for photosynthesis and the oxygen is used in cellular respiration, the extra oxygen being released through the stomata; at night, CO₂ is excreted through the open stomata.

Extra water: it is stored in the vacuoles to give turgor; any extra is removed by transpiration (stomatal, cuticular and lenticular) during the day, and by guttation (little drops from pores at the tips or edges of leaves) at night, e.g. in grasses.

Other wastes: stored as harmless crystals in stems, leaves or roots; stored in leaves and lost when the leaves fall; or excreted through special pores by force — latex by the rubber plant, gums by the Acacia tree, resins by coniferous trees and mucilage by the ladyfinger.

222. What are kidney stones and how are they formed? Suggest ways in which these stones can be removed from the body.

Kidney stones: the filtered harmful substances (e.g. calcium oxalate, calcium and ammonium phosphate, uric acid) that gather in the kidneys and make larger objects which cannot pass in the urine are called kidney stones.

Formation: they form when a diet rich in calcium oxalate, calcium and ammonium phosphates (green vegetables, fats, dairy products) or extra vitamins C and D is taken, and also because of reduced water intake, excess uric acid in the blood, urinary tract infections and alcohol consumption.

Removal: (i) small stones pass out in the urine if the patient drinks plenty of water; (ii) large stones are removed by surgery, in which the kidney, ureter or urinary bladder is opened; (iii) lithotripsy, the non-surgical method, in which shock-waves break the stones into tiny pieces that pass out in the urine.

223. Define haemodialysis. How is it performed?

Definition: haemodialysis is a method of dialysis, i.e. cleaning the blood of a patient suffering from renal failure by an artificial method, in which the blood is passed through a machine called a dialyzer.

Procedure: the dialyzer contains tubes whose walls are made of semi-permeable membranes. The patient’s blood flows inside the tubes while the dialysis fluid flows outside. Extra water and wastes (e.g. urea) diffuse from the blood into the dialysis fluid. The purified blood is then returned back to the body.

224. How does a dialyzer work? Relate the function of a dialyzer with that of the kidney.

Working of the dialyzer: blood is pumped through tubes made of semi-permeable membrane while the dialysis fluid flows outside the tubes. Nitrogenous wastes, extra water and excess salts pass through the membrane into the dialysis fluid, and the cleaned blood is returned to the body.

Relation with the kidney: the kidney filters nitrogenous wastes from the blood and controls the water-solute balance, excreting them in urine. A dialyzer does the same job artificially for a patient whose nephrons have stopped working — the semi-permeable membrane acts like the filter, and the dialysis fluid receives the wastes just as urine does.

Activity 2Trace the movement of a molecule of urea from the blood to the urethra using a flow chart: Blood in the renal artery → arterioles → capillaries of the glomerulus → pressure filtration into the Bowman’s capsule (glomerular filtrate) → proximal convoluted tubule → Loop of Henle → distal convoluted tubule → collecting duct → renal pelvis → ureter → urinary bladder → urethra → outside the body. (Urea may also be added to the renal tubule from the blood capillaries by tubular secretion.)
Key Vocabulary

Unit 11 Vocabulary

Important terms you must know: learn these definitions for exam questions.

TermDefinition
HomeostasisThe ability to maintain the internal environment constant or nearly constant.
OsmoregulationThe process by which the amount of water and solutes is maintained in the body.
ExcretionThe elimination of harmful solutes (e.g. nitrogenous wastes) and extra water from the body.
ThermoregulationThe maintenance of the internal body temperature within a tolerable range.
MesophytesTerrestrial plants which live where a sufficient quantity of water is available, e.g. maize, clover and rose.
HydrophytesPlants which live in freshwater or in wet soil, e.g. the water lily.
XerophytesPlants which live in dry environments such as deserts, e.g. cacti.
HalophytesPlants which live in habitats with salty waters, e.g. sea grasses.
TranspirationThe loss of water from the plant surface in the form of vapours.
GuttationThe removal of extra water as little drops from small pores at the tips or edges of leaves, mostly at night.
Goose BumpsBumps on the skin formed when the muscles at the base of the hairs contract in cold conditions.
FeverAn increase in body temperature when the set point of the hypothalamus is displaced above the normal 37 °C during infection.
NephronThe functional unit of the kidney, made of a renal corpuscle and a renal tubule.
Renal CorpuscleThe first part of the nephron, composed of the glomerulus and the Bowman's capsule.
GlomerulusA network of capillaries inside the Bowman's capsule.
Bowman's CapsuleThe cup-shaped structure around the glomerulus.
Renal TubuleThe long tube attached to the Bowman's capsule: proximal convoluted tubule, Loop of Henle and distal convoluted tubule.
Glomerular FiltrateThe material filtered from the glomerulus and collected in the Bowman's capsule.
Pressure FiltrationFiltration of water, salts, sugars and nitrogenous wastes from the glomerulus into the Bowman's capsule due to high blood pressure.
ReabsorptionThe return of useful constituents of the filtrate from the renal tubule into the blood capillaries.
Tubular SecretionThe addition of waste materials such as salts, hydrogen ions and urea from the blood capillaries to the renal tubule.
UrineThe filtrate present in the renal tubule after pressure filtration, reabsorption and tubular secretion.
Kidney StonesLarger objects formed when harmful filtered substances gather in the kidneys; they cannot pass in the urine.
LithotripsyThe most recent, non-surgical removal of kidney stones by shock-waves that break them into tiny pieces.
Kidney (Renal) FailureThe condition in which the nephrons are progressively destroyed and stop functioning.
DialysisThe cleaning of the blood of a patient with renal failure by artificial methods.
HaemodialysisDialysis in which blood is passed through a dialyzer containing semi-permeable tubes.
Peritoneal DialysisDialysis in which dialysis fluid is filled in the peritoneal cavity through a catheter; it can be done at home.
Kidney TransplantationThe advanced treatment of kidney failure in which a healthy kidney from a donor is placed in the patient.
