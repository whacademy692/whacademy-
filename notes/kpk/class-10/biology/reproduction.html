Part 1 BIO-01

Types of Reproduction

Section 14.1 — what reproduction is and how asexual and sexual reproduction differ.

Common MistakeAn individual can live without reproduction, but a species cannot survive without it. Reproduction is essential for the continuity of the species, not for the survival of the individual.

What is Reproduction?

1. Define reproduction.
• What is reproduction?

Reproduction is the process by which living organisms produce new organisms of their own kind.

2. Why is reproduction important?
• State the importance of reproduction.

It ensures the continuity of a species by transmitting genetic material from one generation to the next.

3. Is reproduction essential for an individual to live?

No. An individual can live without reproduction, but a species cannot survive without it.

Asexual vs Sexual Reproduction

4. Name the two main types of reproduction.

Asexual reproduction and sexual reproduction.

5. Define asexual reproduction.
• What is asexual reproduction?

Asexual reproduction is the type of reproduction without the fusion of gametes, usually involving a single parent.

6. Define sexual reproduction.
• What is sexual reproduction?

Sexual reproduction is the type in which the male and female gametes of two parents fuse to produce offspring.

7. Differentiate between asexual and sexual reproduction.

Asexual reproduction involves no fusion of gametes, usually has a single parent and gives genetically identical offspring (clones); sexual reproduction involves the fusion of male and female gametes and gives offspring with a blend of characteristics of both parents.

8. What is a clone?
• Why are offspring of asexual reproduction called clones?

Offspring produced by asexual reproduction are similar to the single parent and show no genetic variation, so they are called clones.

9. Why do offspring of sexual reproduction differ from their parents?

Because they receive a blend of characteristics from both the male and female parents.

10. Which type of reproduction does not create genetic variation?

Asexual reproduction does not create genetic variation.

Binary and Multiple Fission

11. Define binary fission.
• What is binary fission?

Binary fission is a type of asexual reproduction in which the body of the parent organism divides into two daughter organisms.

12. Name some organisms that reproduce by binary fission.
• Give examples of binary fission.

Euglena, Amoeba, Chlamydomonas and bacteria.

13. Describe binary fission in bacteria.

The DNA molecule duplicates to form two DNA molecules which move to opposite sides; the cell wall and membrane constrict from both sides and meet in the centre, separating the cell into two daughter bacterial cells.

14. Describe binary fission in protists such as Amoeba.

The nucleus of the parent divides into two, followed by division of the cytoplasm, forming two daughter cells of almost equal size.

15. Define multiple fission.
• What is multiple fission?

Multiple fission is a type of asexual reproduction in which the nucleus divides repeatedly to produce many daughter cells from one parent cell.

16. Under what conditions does multiple fission occur?
• When does multiple fission take place?

It occurs during unfavourable conditions.

17. What is a cyst?

A cyst is a protective covering formed over the cell during multiple fission, inside which the nucleus divides to form many offspring.

18. Give examples of organisms that reproduce by multiple fission.

Amoeba and Plasmodium.

Budding and Spore Formation

19. Define budding.
• What is budding?

Budding is a type of asexual reproduction in which an outgrowth called a bud arises from the body of the parent and grows into an offspring.

20. Describe budding in yeast.

A bud grows on the side of the cell, the nucleus divides into two and one daughter nucleus enters the bud; the bud grows and may separate from or stay attached to the parent.

21. Define spore formation.
• What is spore formation?

Spore formation is asexual reproduction in which organisms such as fungi reproduce by making thick-walled spores.

22. Give an example of an organism that reproduces by spore formation.

Rhizopus (bread mould).

23. What is a sporangium?

A sporangium is a thick-walled sac in which spores are produced, located at the tips of erect branches of the mould.

24. Why are spores able to survive adverse conditions?

Because of their thick walls, which make them resistant to adverse environmental conditions.

25. What factors are needed for the germination of spores?

Optimum temperature, moisture, oxygen and light.

26. What are endospores?

Endospores are spores formed inside bacterial cells when bacteria face harsh conditions; they give rise to a new bacterium when favourable conditions return.

Natural Vegetative Propagation

27. Define vegetative propagation.
• What is vegetative reproduction?

Vegetative propagation is a type of asexual reproduction in which a plant produces a new generation using its vegetative parts — stems, leaves and roots.

28. Describe vegetative propagation through leaves.
• How does Bryophyllum reproduce vegetatively?

In Bryophyllum the leaves have buds at their margins; when a leaf falls, the buds form tiny shoots that break off and develop roots into new plants.

29. What is a stolon?
• Define a runner.

A stolon (runner) is a horizontal, above-ground stem that produces leaves and roots at its nodes, which develop into new plantlets, e.g. in strawberry.

30. What is a rhizome?
• Give examples of plants that reproduce by rhizomes.

A rhizome is a horizontal underground stem that produces the shoot and root systems of new plants, e.g. ferns and ginger.

31. What is a bulb?
• Give examples of plants that reproduce by bulbs.

A bulb is an underground bud with fleshy leaves that act as food storage units; new buds grow into new plants, e.g. tulips, onions and lilies.

32. What is a corm?
• How does a corm differ from a bulb?

A corm is an underground stem that resembles a bulb but has no fleshy leaves — only a few brown non-functional leaves on the outside — and it does not store significant amounts of starch, e.g. Colocasia and garlic.

33. What are cormels?

Cormels are new miniature corms produced by the branches of a corm stem, used to propagate the plant.

34. What is a tuber?
• How does a potato reproduce vegetatively?

A tuber is an underground, swollen, fleshy stem; the potato has axillary buds called "eyes" above each leaf that grow into new potato plants.

35. What is an "eye" of a potato?

An eye is a well-developed axillary bud found above each leaf on the tuber, which grows into a new potato plant.

36. Describe vegetative propagation through roots.
• What are suckers?

Some shrubs such as red raspberry give rise to new shoots (root sprouts or "suckers") from their roots, which can detach and grow into independent plants.

Artificial Propagation and Cloning

37. Define artificial vegetative propagation.
• What is artificial vegetative propagation?

It is the method developed by humans to produce more plants using their vegetative parts; the common methods are cutting and grafting.

38. Differentiate between natural and artificial vegetative propagation.

In natural vegetative propagation a plant produces new plants by itself from its stems, leaves or roots (e.g. stolon, bulb, tuber, Bryophyllum leaves); artificial vegetative propagation uses methods developed by humans, i.e. cutting and grafting.

39. What is cutting?
• Define cutting as a method of propagation.

Cutting is a method in which pieces of stem or root are cut from a plant and used to grow new plants.

40. Give examples of plants grown by stem cuttings and root cuttings.

Stem cuttings: grape vine, rose and chrysanthemum. Root cuttings: sweet potato and blackberries.

41. What is grafting?
• Define grafting.

Grafting is a method in which a branch of a desired variety of plant is attached (grafted) onto the stem of an ordinary plant to get fruits of better quality.

42. Why is grafting carried out?
• What is the advantage of grafting?

It is done to get better quality and quantity of fruits; it is used to propagate almost all fruit trees such as almond, plum and cherries.

43. Define cloning.
• What is cloning?

Cloning is a technique of asexual reproduction in which a population of genetically identical individuals is produced from a single parent using its tissues or cells.

44. State two uses of cloning.
• Why is cloning helpful in horticulture?

It makes identical copies of useful plants, preserves the quality of the yield and helps increase agricultural output.

Parts of a Flower

45. What is a receptacle?

The receptacle is the swollen tip of a flower on which the flower parts are present.

46. Name the four whorls of a flower.

Calyx (sepals), Corolla (petals), Androecium (stamens) and Gynoecium (carpels).

47. What are sepals?
• What is the function of sepals?

Sepals form the outermost whorl (calyx); they are usually green and protect the inner parts of the developing flower before it opens.

48. What are petals?
• What is the corolla?

Petals form the next whorl (corolla); they are usually coloured.

49. What is a stamen made of?
• Name the parts of a stamen.

Each stamen consists of an anther and a filament; the filament supports the anther.

50. What does the anther contain?

The anther contains pollen sacs (microsporangia) which produce microspores.

51. Name the parts of a carpel.

A carpel consists of the ovary (enlarged base), the style (stalk-like) and the stigma (tip of the style).

52. What do the ovules inside the ovary produce?

The ovules (megasporangia) produce megaspores.

53. Differentiate between bisexual and unisexual flowers.

In bisexual flowers both stamen and carpel are present in the same flower; in unisexual flowers the stamen and carpels are present in separate flowers.

Alternation of Generations and Gametophytes

54. What is alternation of generations?

It is a life cycle in which a diploid sporophyte generation alternates with a haploid gametophyte generation.

55. Which generation does the body of a flowering plant represent?

The complete body of a flowering plant represents the sporophyte (2n) generation.

56. What is the embryo sac?
• What is the female gametophyte?

The embryo sac is the female gametophyte that develops from the megaspore; it contains seven cells including the egg, five non-functional cells and the fusion nucleus.

57. How is the embryo sac formed from the megaspore mother cell?

The diploid megaspore mother cell undergoes meiosis to form four haploid megaspores; one survives and divides by mitosis into eight nuclei, which organise into the seven-celled embryo sac.

58. What is the pollen grain?
• What is the male gametophyte?

The pollen grain is the male gametophyte that develops from the microspore; it is a two-celled structure containing a tube cell and a generative cell.

59. What is the function of the tube cell and the generative cell?

The tube cell forms the pollen tube, and the generative cell forms two sperms.

Pollination and Fertilisation

60. Define pollination.
• What is pollination?

Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma of a flower.

61. Differentiate between self-pollination and cross-pollination.

Self-pollination involves one flower or flowers of the same plant, while cross-pollination involves two flowers of different plants of the same species.

62. Describe fertilisation in a flowering plant.

The tube cell forms a pollen tube that grows through the stigma and style, enters the ovule through the micropyle, and the generative cell forms two sperms that reach the embryo sac.

63. What is the micropyle?

The micropyle is a small opening in the ovule through which the pollen tube enters to reach the embryo sac.

64. Define double fertilisation.
• What is double fertilisation?

Double fertilisation is the fusion of two sperms — one with the egg to form the zygote and the other with the fusion nucleus to form the endosperm. It is unique to flowering plants.

65. What is the endosperm and what is its function?

The endosperm is a triploid (3n) tissue formed when the second sperm fuses with the fusion nucleus; it provides nourishment to the embryo.

66. What does the ovule and the ovary become after fertilisation?

The ovule matures into the seed and the ovary changes into the fruit.

67. What is parthenocarpy?

Parthenocarpy is the development of the ovary into a fruit without fertilisation of the egg, producing a seedless fruit, e.g. bananas and seedless grapes.

Insect- and Wind-Pollinated Flowers

68. List the characteristics of insect-pollinated flowers.

Brightly coloured large petals, fragrance, nectar, sticky pollen, and sticky or specially shaped stigmas to transfer pollen.

69. Give examples of insect-pollinated flowers.

Bougainvillea, Rose, Sunflower and Orchids.

70. List the characteristics of wind-pollinated flowers.

No scent or nectar; small, smooth, winged pollen; feathery stigmas; large anthers on long stalks; and pollen produced in large numbers.

71. Give examples of wind-pollinated plants.

Conifers, grasses, cereals, poplar, oak and walnut.

Structure of a Seed

72. What is a seed?
• Define a seed.

A seed is a mature ovule — a miniature plant with a protective cover in a suspended (inactive) state of development.

73. What is the seed coat (testa)?

The seed coat or testa is the black or brown outer covering of the seed that protects the embryo.

74. What is the hilum?

The hilum is a small scar on the seed coat that marks the place where the seed was attached in the fruit.

75. What is the function of the micropyle in a seed?

The seed absorbs water through the micropyle at the time of germination.

76. What are cotyledons?
• How do monocot and dicot cotyledons differ?

Cotyledons are the seed leaves of the embryo; a monocot seed has one cotyledon while a dicot seed has two large fleshy cotyledons.

77. What are the plumule and radicle?

The plumule is a minute bud that gives rise to the future stem, and the radicle gives rise to the future root.

78. Define epicotyl and hypocotyl.

The epicotyl is the portion between the plumule and the cotyledons, and the hypocotyl is the portion between the cotyledons and the radicle.

79. What is the endosperm of a seed?
• Which seeds have endosperm?

The endosperm is a nutrient-storing tissue formed from the endosperm nucleus. Monocot seeds have endosperm while dicot seeds have none; in monocots the cotyledon absorbs nutrients from the endosperm and transfers them to the embryo.

Seed Germination

80. Define seed germination.
• What is seed germination?

Seed germination is the development of the embryo of a seed into a seedling.

81. Which part emerges first during germination?

The radicle emerges first and forms the first root, after which the plumule grows to form the shoot.

82. Define epigeal germination.

In epigeal germination the hypocotyl elongates and forces the cotyledons above the ground, e.g. in melon, cucumber and bean.

83. Define hypogeal germination.

In hypogeal germination the epicotyl elongates and the cotyledons remain below the ground, e.g. in maize and pea.

84. Differentiate between epigeal and hypogeal germination.

In epigeal germination the hypocotyl elongates and brings the cotyledons above the ground (melon, cucumber, bean); in hypogeal germination the epicotyl elongates and the cotyledons remain below the ground (maize, pea).

85. State the conditions necessary for seed germination.

Water, oxygen and a suitable temperature (between 5°C and 30°C).

86. Why is water necessary for germination?

Water softens the seed coat, makes it burst, and activates enzymes that digest stored food and make it available to the embryo.

87. Why is oxygen necessary for germination?

The embryo cells use oxygen for cellular respiration to release energy from the stored food.

Theophrastus — Father of Botany

88. Who is known as the father of botany?
• State the contribution of Theophrastus.

Theophrastus is the father of botany; he identified over 300 plant species and his most remarkable contribution was the discovery of sex in plants.

89. What distinctions did Theophrastus make?

He distinguished between sexual and vegetative reproduction, dicot and monocot plants, and angiosperms and gymnosperms, and described grafting and cutting.

Fragmentation, Budding and Parthenogenesis

90. Name the asexual methods of reproduction in animals.

Fragmentation, budding and parthenogenesis.

91. Define fragmentation.
• What is fragmentation?

Fragmentation is a method in which the body of an organism breaks into many parts (fragments), each of which develops into a new organism.

92. Give an example of an animal that reproduces by fragmentation.

Planaria.

93. Describe budding in Hydra.

A small bud forms on the side of the body of Hydra; it enlarges and breaks away from the parent and develops into a new Hydra.

94. Define parthenogenesis.
• What is parthenogenesis?

Parthenogenesis is a special type of asexual reproduction in which an unfertilized egg cell develops into a new organism.

95. Why is parthenogenesis considered asexual reproduction?

Because the egg develops into a new animal without fertilisation (no fusion of gametes takes place).

96. Give an example of parthenogenesis.
• How is sex determined in honeybees?

In honeybees the fertilized eggs develop into females, while the unfertilized eggs develop into males.

Gonads and Gametogenesis

97. What are gonads?
• Define gonads.

Gonads are the special organs that produce gametes — the testes in males and the ovaries in females — and also secrete sex hormones.

98. Define gametogenesis.
• What is gametogenesis?

Gametogenesis is the formation of gametes in the gonads; it involves meiosis, which reduces the chromosome number from diploid (2n) to haploid (n).

99. Define spermatogenesis.
• What is spermatogenesis?

Spermatogenesis is the formation of male gametes (sperms).

100. Describe spermatogenesis.

In the testes, each diploid primary spermatocyte divides by meiosis into four haploid spermatids, and each spermatid develops into a motile sperm.

101. Define oogenesis.
• What is oogenesis?

Oogenesis is the formation of the female gamete (ovum).

102. Describe oogenesis.

In the ovary follicle, a diploid primary oocyte divides in meiosis I into a larger secondary oocyte and a first polar body; in meiosis II the secondary oocyte forms a second polar body and an egg.

Fertilisation

103. Define fertilisation.
• What is fertilisation?

Fertilisation is the process in which the male and female gametes fuse to form a zygote.

104. What is a zygote?
• What is the importance of fertilisation?

The zygote is the first cell of a new offspring; fertilisation restores the diploid number of chromosomes in the zygote.

105. Define external fertilisation.
• What is external fertilisation?

External fertilisation is the fusion of gametes outside the body of the female, usually in water, e.g. in fish and amphibians.

106. Why do animals with external fertilisation release many gametes?

Because fertilisation occurs in water where there are more chances of loss of gametes.

107. Define internal fertilisation.
• What is internal fertilisation?

Internal fertilisation is the fusion of gametes inside the body of the female; it occurs in many land animals.

108. Differentiate between external and internal fertilisation.

In external fertilisation the gametes fuse outside the female’s body, usually in water (fish, amphibians), and many gametes are released; in internal fertilisation the egg stays in the female’s reproductive duct and is fertilised there by the sperm (reptiles, birds, mammals).

109. Name the two ways the fertilized egg develops after internal fertilisation.

In some animals (reptiles and birds) eggs with hard shells are laid, while in most mammals the embryo develops inside the reproductive duct of the female.

Reproductive System of a Rabbit

110. To which group do rabbits belong?

Rabbits belong to the class Mammalia.

111. Where are the testes of a rabbit located?
• What is the scrotum?

The testes lie in a sac-like structure called the scrotum, which is outside the body.

112. Where does sperm formation take place in a male rabbit?

Sperm formation takes place in the coiled seminiferous tubules of the testes.

113. Name the associated ducts of the male reproductive system of a rabbit.

Two epididymis and two sperm ducts (vas deferens), which join the urethra that transports sperms out.

114. State the functions of the secretions of the seminal vesicles, prostate gland and Cowper’s gland.

Seminal vesicle secretion provides nutrients for the sperms, the prostate gland secretion neutralises acidity, and the Cowper’s gland secretion lubricates the ducts.

115. Where are the ovaries of a female rabbit located?

In the abdominal cavity, just ventral to the kidneys.

116. Where is the egg formed in a female rabbit?
• What is a follicle?

The egg is formed inside a special structure called the follicle in the ovary.

117. Name the associated ducts of the female reproductive system of a rabbit.

Two oviducts (fallopian tubes) and a uterus; each horn of the uterus opens into a cervix, and both cervices open into the birth canal (vagina).

B. Short Questions

140. How can vegetative methods of reproduction be used to achieve better yield?

Vegetative methods such as cutting, grafting and cloning produce plants that are genetically identical to a chosen high-quality parent. By using these methods, growers can quickly multiply plants with the best fruit quality and desirable features, thereby achieving a better and more uniform yield.

141. Differentiate between internal and external fertilization. Which type of fertilization will ensure better chances of fusion of gametes?

In external fertilisation the gametes fuse outside the female’s body (usually in water), e.g. in fish and amphibians; in internal fertilisation the gametes fuse inside the body of the female, e.g. in mammals. Internal fertilisation ensures better chances of fusion of gametes because the gametes are protected and there is less loss.

142. What is population planning? Why is it important?

Population planning is the effort to control the rate of population growth through family planning, changing social attitudes and improving the status of women. It is important because rapid population growth puts pressure on agricultural land and resources, hinders economic and social development, and lowers living standards.

143. How does self-pollination bring more variations in plants?

Self-pollination, which involves one flower or the same plant, actually produces little variation because the gametes come from a single parent. It is cross-pollination — the transfer of pollen between two different plants of the same species — that brings more variation, by combining the genes of two parents to give offspring with new blends of characteristics.

144. How can the spread of HIV be controlled? What is the role of the community and NGOs in the control of AIDS?

The spread of HIV can be controlled by avoiding unsafe sexual activity, not sharing needles, screening blood before transfusion, and following Islamic teachings. The community, the National AIDS Control Programme and NGOs help by educating people, raising awareness through events like World AIDS Day, and supporting HIV/AIDS patients.

145. Name the four whorls present in a flower and also describe the components of each whorl.

Calyx — made of sepals (usually green, protect the bud); Corolla — made of petals (usually coloured); Androecium — made of stamens, each with an anther and filament (male part); Gynoecium — made of carpels, each with an ovary, style and stigma (female part).

146. How are wind-pollinated flowers different from insect-pollinated flowers?

Wind-pollinated flowers have no scent or nectar, small smooth winged pollen, feathery stigmas and large anthers on long stalks, and produce pollen in huge numbers. Insect-pollinated flowers have brightly coloured large petals, fragrance, nectar and sticky pollen and stigmas to attract insects and stick pollen to them.

147. Why do seeds need water and oxygen for germination?

Water softens and bursts the seed coat and activates the enzymes that digest stored food for the embryo. Oxygen is needed by the embryo cells for cellular respiration to release energy from the stored food for growth.

C. Detailed Questions

150. Describe the alternation of generation in a flowering plant.

A flowering plant shows a life cycle in which a diploid sporophyte generation alternates with a haploid gametophyte generation. The plant body is the sporophyte (2n); it produces haploid spores by meiosis. Megaspores develop into the female gametophyte (embryo sac) and microspores into the male gametophyte (pollen grain). These gametophytes produce gametes by mitosis, which fuse at fertilisation to form a diploid zygote that grows into the next sporophyte — thus the two generations alternate.

151. Explain the different plant structures modified for vegetative propagation.

Stems are modified as the stolon/runner (strawberry), rhizome (ferns, ginger), bulb (onion, tulip, lily), corm (garlic, Colocasia) and tuber (potato). Leaves bear marginal buds in Bryophyllum, and roots give rise to suckers in red raspberry. Each of these vegetative structures can grow into a new, independent plant.

152. Explain gametogenesis.

Gametogenesis is the formation of gametes in the gonads by meiosis. In spermatogenesis, each diploid primary spermatocyte in the testes divides by meiosis into four haploid spermatids, which develop into motile sperms. In oogenesis, a diploid primary oocyte in the ovary divides in meiosis I into a secondary oocyte and a first polar body; in meiosis II the secondary oocyte forms a second polar body and one egg.

153. How are seeds produced? What is the structure of a seed?

After double fertilisation, the zygote develops into the embryo and the fusion nucleus into the endosperm; the ovule then matures into a seed. A seed consists of a protective seed coat (testa), a hilum (scar of attachment), a micropyle (for water absorption), and the embryo, which has cotyledons, a plumule (future shoot) and a radicle (future root); monocot seeds also contain endosperm.

154. Write a comprehensive note on seed germination and its types.

Seed germination is the development of the embryo of a seed into a seedling. It begins with the absorption of water through the micropyle, followed by the emergence of the radicle (first root) and then the plumule (shoot). It needs water, oxygen and a suitable temperature (5–30°C). There are two types: in epigeal germination the hypocotyl elongates and brings the cotyledons above the ground (melon, cucumber, bean); in hypogeal germination the epicotyl elongates and the cotyledons stay below the ground (maize, pea).
