Part 1 BIO-01

Respiration, Gaseous Exchange and Breathing

Common MistakeRespiration and breathing are not the same thing. Respiration is a chemical, energy-releasing process inside cells; breathing is only the physical movement of air into and out of the lungs.

Respiration, Energy and Sunlight

1. Define respiration.
• What is respiration?

Respiration is an energy-releasing process at the cellular level in which food molecules (e.g. glucose) are broken down into simpler compounds like carbon dioxide and water with the release of energy.

2. Why is oxygen required for aerobic respiration?

Oxygen is required for the complete oxidation of food molecules in aerobic respiration; carbon dioxide is released as a by-product.

3. What is the ultimate source of energy for life?

Sunlight is the ultimate source of energy for life. Photosynthetic organisms capture it to make energy-rich food.

4. Which gas is released as a by-product of photosynthesis?

Oxygen is released as a by-product of photosynthesis.

5. What do all organisms take from and release into their environment for respiration?
• From where do organisms obtain the oxygen needed for respiration?

All organisms take in oxygen from the environment (air or water) and release carbon dioxide into the environment.

Gaseous Exchange and Diffusion

6. Define gaseous exchange.
• What is meant by gas exchange?

The exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide between an organism and its environment is called gaseous exchange.

7. By which process does gaseous exchange occur?

Gaseous exchange occurs by diffusion.

8. Define diffusion.
• What is diffusion?

Diffusion is a process in which molecules move from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration.

Breathing

9. Define breathing.
• What is breathing?

Breathing is the physical process in which animals move air into and out of their body, to get oxygen from air and to release carbon dioxide in it.

10. Name the organs of breathing in higher animals.

Lungs are the organs of breathing in higher animals.

11. Why does oxygen diffuse from the air into the blood during breathing?

Fresh air at the respiratory surface of the lung has a higher concentration of oxygen than the blood, so oxygen diffuses into the blood.

12. Why does carbon dioxide diffuse from the blood into the lungs?

The concentration of carbon dioxide is higher in the blood than in the lungs, so it diffuses into the lungs and is expelled out by breathing.

Respiration vs Gaseous Exchange vs Breathing

13. Differentiate among respiration, gaseous exchange and breathing.
• How are respiration, gas exchange and breathing different from each other?

Respiration: energy-releasing oxidation of food (e.g. glucose) in the cells.

Gaseous exchange: exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide between an organism and its environment, by diffusion.

Breathing: physical process of moving air into and out of the body (lungs).

14. How are breathing, gaseous exchange and cellular respiration related to each other?

Breathing moves air to and from the lungs. In gaseous exchange, oxygen is taken from the air and carbon dioxide is released into it; oxygen is transported to the cells and carbon dioxide to the lungs. In cellular respiration, the oxygen is used to oxidize food and carbon dioxide is produced.

Routes of Gaseous Exchange in Plants

15. What is the epidermis?

The outer surface of the root, stem and leaves is called the epidermis. It allows the exchange of gases between the inner cells and the environment.

16. What are stomata?
• Define stomata. Where are they present?

Stomata are small pores in the epidermis of leaves and young stems through which air moves in and out.

17. What are lenticels?
• Define lenticels. Where are they present?

Lenticels are special pores in the bark of woody stems that allow gaseous exchange with the environment.

18. Why are lenticels needed in woody stems?

The entire surface of a woody stem is covered by bark, and gaseous exchange cannot occur through bark. The lenticels present in the bark allow gases to be exchanged with the environment.

19. Through which structures does gaseous exchange occur in plants?
• Name the routes of gaseous exchange in plants.

Gaseous exchange in plants occurs through the general surface (epidermis and cuticle), the stomata and the lenticels.

20. How does gaseous exchange occur in leaves and young stems?

Through the stomata. Air moves in and out through these small pores of the epidermis, and gases are exchanged between the inner cells and the air.

21. What is the role of the cuticle in gaseous exchange?

At some parts a thick cuticle is present over the epidermis; it also allows the exchange of gases.

Day and Night Gas Exchange

22. Which gas do leaves take in and which do they release during the daytime?

During the daytime, leaves take in carbon dioxide from the environment and release oxygen into it.

23. Which gas do plant cells take in and which do they release at night?

At night, the cells get oxygen from the environment and release carbon dioxide.

24. Why do plant cells take oxygen from the environment at night?

At night there is no photosynthesis, so no oxygen is produced, while all cells keep carrying out respiration. Therefore the cells get oxygen from the environment and release carbon dioxide.

25. Do plant cells respire during the daytime?

Yes. During the daytime all plant cells carry out respiration, while the green parts (leaves) also carry out photosynthesis.

26. How are photosynthesis and respiration linked in a leaf during the day?

The carbon dioxide produced in respiration is used in photosynthesis (along with carbon dioxide taken from the environment), and the oxygen produced in photosynthesis is used in respiration.

27. Compare the gases used and released in photosynthesis and respiration.
• How are photosynthesis and respiration related in terms of gases?

Photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide and water and releases oxygen as a by-product.

Respiration uses oxygen to break down organic molecules and releases carbon dioxide and water with energy.

The carbon dioxide of respiration is used in photosynthesis, and the oxygen of photosynthesis is used in respiration.

Tilling and the Leaf Diagram

28. What is the effect of tilling on soil?
• How does tilling help gaseous exchange between roots and air?

Tilling helps soil drainage and aeration. It creates air spaces between soil particles, which allow better exchange of gases between the roots and the air.

29. Name the parts labelled in the diagram of gaseous exchange in a leaf (Fig. 10.2 b).

Cuticle, mesophyll and stoma (air enters and air leaves through the stoma).

Air Passageway: Overview

30. Into which two main components can the human respiratory system be divided?

The human respiratory system can be divided into the air passageway and the lungs.

31. What is the air passageway?
• Define air passageway.

The connected tubes through which air reaches the lungs, and through which it moves out again after gaseous exchange, are collectively called the air passageway.

32. Name the parts of the air passageway.
• Write the correct sequence of the structures through which air flows in the human body.

Nostrils and nasal cavities → pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles (which end in the alveoli).

33. For which mode of life is the human respiratory system well adapted?

It is well adapted for the terrestrial (land) mode of life and ensures a smooth exchange of gases between the environment and the body.

Nostrils and Nasal Cavities

34. What are external nostrils?
• Where are the external nostrils located?

The external nostrils are two openings on the ventral side of the head. They allow air from the outside into the two nasal cavities.

35. What happens to the air in the nasal cavities?

The air is warmed, moistened and freed from dust by the hairs and the mucous membrane of the nasal cavity.

36. Why is it advised to breathe through the nose and not through the mouth?
• Why should we breathe through the nose?

In the nasal cavity the air is warmed, moistened and freed from dust by the hairs and the mucous membrane, which keeps us healthy.

Pharynx, Larynx and Epiglottis

37. What is the pharynx?
• Describe the pharynx.

The pharynx is an about 4.5 inches long muscular passage into which the nasal cavities lead. It is lined by a mucous membrane.

38. Name the organs of lymphatic tissue present in the pharynx. What do they do?

The pharynx contains tonsils and adenoids; they trap and filter microorganisms.

39. What is the larynx?
• What is the voice box?

The larynx or voice box is the part into which air moves from the pharynx. It surrounds the upper part of the trachea, and its cavity is lined by a mucous membrane.

40. What two passages open at the back of the pharynx?

One passage opens into the esophagus on the dorsal side; the other opens through the glottis into the trachea on the ventral side.

41. What is the glottis?

The glottis is the opening through which the passage from the back of the pharynx leads into the trachea (ventral side). It is guarded by a lid-like structure called the epiglottis.

42. What is the epiglottis? State its function.
• What prevents food from entering the larynx?

The epiglottis is a lid-like structure that guards the glottis. It prevents food from entering the larynx.

Trachea and Bronchi

43. What is the trachea? Give its other name.
• What is the windpipe?

The trachea or wind pipe is a tubular structure that lies ventral to the esophagus and extends to the chest cavity (thorax).

44. How does the inner surface of the trachea help in cleaning the air?

The inner surface of the trachea is ciliated and secretes mucus, which helps to filter, moisten and warm the incoming air.

45. How many cartilaginous rings does the trachea have? What is their shape?

The trachea has 16 to 20 C-shaped cartilaginous rings.

46. What are bronchi?

Inside the thorax, the trachea divides into two branches called bronchi. Each bronchus enters the lung on its own side.

47. Do the bronchi also have cartilaginous rings?

Yes, the bronchi also possess cartilaginous rings, but they are smaller than those of the trachea.

48. What happens to each bronchus after it enters the lung?

On entering the lung, each bronchus divides and sub-divides progressively into smaller bronchi.

Bronchioles and Alveoli

49. What are bronchioles?
• When are the smaller bronchi called bronchioles?

When the smaller bronchi attain a diameter of one millimetre or less, they are called bronchioles. Bronchioles have no cartilage.

50. How does each bronchiole end?

Each bronchiole ends in a duct. The duct opens in a cluster of pouches (alveoli) that resemble bunches of grapes.

51. What is an alveolus?
• Define alveoli.

An alveolus (plural: alveoli) is a microscopic, thin-walled pouch at the end of a bronchiole. The alveoli provide the surface for gaseous exchange.

52. Why are alveoli an excellent site for gaseous exchange?
• How are alveoli adapted for gaseous exchange?

The alveoli are very thin-walled, and their walls are covered with a network of blood capillaries where gaseous exchange occurs.

Diagrams of the Respiratory System

Nasal cavity, nostril, epiglottis, larynx, pharynx, trachea, bronchus, pleural cavity, right lung, left lung and diaphragm.

54. Name the parts labelled in the general outline of the respiratory system (Fig. 10.5).

Ethmoid sinuses, nose, pharynx, tongue, lung, trachea, bronchus, bronchioles and alveoli (shown magnified).

The Lungs and the Pleura

Section 10.3 (b) — position, pleura and lung size.

TidbitThe size of the lungs of any organism is related to the activities and body form of that organism. Cheetah and elephant have large lungs.

Lungs, Pleura and Pleural Fluid

55. Where are the lungs located?
• What structures enclose the lungs?

The lungs are placed in the chest cavity. They are enclosed from above and the sides by the chest wall, and the diaphragm lies below them.

56. What is the pleura?
• What encloses each lung?

Each lung is enclosed in a double membrane called the pleura.

57. What is the function of the pleural fluid?

The pleural fluid, present in the pleura, makes the movements of the lungs (expansion and contraction) easy.

58. Why do the cheetah and the elephant have large lungs?

The size of the lungs of an organism is related to its activities and body form. The cheetah is a very active animal and the elephant has a very large body, so both need more oxygen and have large lungs.

The diaphragm is a dome-shaped sheet of skeletal muscles. The muscles in between the ribs are called intercostal muscles.

Phases of Breathing and the Chest Cavity

59. What are the two phases of breathing?

Breathing has two phases: inspiration (taking in of air) and expiration (letting out of air). They take place continuously one after the other.

60. Define inspiration.
• What is inspiration?

Inspiration is the taking in of air into the lungs.

61. Define expiration.
• What is expiration?

Expiration is the letting out of air from the lungs.

62. When does air move into the lungs and when does it move out?

Air moves in when the air pressure in the lungs is lower than the pressure outside the body, and it moves out when the pressure in the lungs is greater than the atmospheric pressure.

63. By which structures is the thorax (chest cavity) bound?

The thorax is bound by the ribs and muscles at the sides, the sternum (chest bone) on the ventral side, the backbone on the dorsal side and the diaphragm at the posterior end.

64. What are intercostal muscles?

The muscles present in between the ribs are called intercostal muscles.

65. What is the sternum?

The sternum is the chest bone; it bounds the thorax on the ventral side.

Inspiration

66. What happens to the volume of the chest cavity and the pressure in the lungs during inspiration?

The volume of the chest cavity increases and the pressure in the lungs decreases.

67. What are the two reasons for the increase in the volume of the chest cavity during inspiration?

(1) The muscles of the diaphragm contract, so it becomes flat instead of dome-shaped. (2) The intercostal muscles between the ribs contract, so the ribcage expands.

68. Why does atmospheric air rush into the lungs during inspiration?

Because the volume of the chest cavity increases, the pressure on the lungs decreases (becomes lower than the atmospheric pressure), so the atmospheric air rushes into the lungs through the air passageway.

Expiration

69. What happens to the ribs and the diaphragm during expiration?

The muscles of the ribs and the diaphragm relax. The ribcage goes down to its original position and the diaphragm becomes dome-shaped.

70. Why is air expelled from the lungs during expiration?

The volume of the chest cavity decreases, which increases the pressure on the lungs and expels the air out.

Comparison and Diagram

71. Differentiate between inspiration and expiration.

Inspiration: diaphragm and intercostal muscles contract; diaphragm becomes flat and ribcage expands; chest volume increases, pressure decreases; air rushes in.

Expiration: diaphragm and rib muscles relax; diaphragm becomes dome-shaped and ribcage goes down; chest volume decreases, pressure increases; air is expelled out.

72. What happens to the diaphragm and ribcage during inhalation and exhalation (Fig. 10.6)?

In inhalation the diaphragm contracts (moves down) and the ribcage expands as the rib muscles contract. In exhalation the diaphragm relaxes (moves up) and the ribcage gets smaller as the rib muscles relax.

Exchange at the Alveoli

73. Explain gaseous exchange in the lungs.
• How does gaseous exchange take place in the alveoli?

Oxygen diffuses from the inhaled air in the alveoli (higher concentration) into the blood of the capillaries (lower concentration), and carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood of the capillaries (higher concentration) into the alveoli (lower concentration).

74. Why does oxygen diffuse from the alveoli into the capillaries?

The blood entering the capillaries has a low oxygen concentration, whereas the inhaled air in the alveoli has a higher oxygen concentration, so oxygen diffuses into the capillaries.

75. Why does carbon dioxide diffuse from the capillaries into the alveoli?

The concentration of carbon dioxide in the blood of the capillaries is more than that of the alveoli, so carbon dioxide diffuses out of the blood and into the alveoli.

76. Describe the flow of blood around an alveolus (Fig. 10.7).

Deoxygenated blood comes through an arteriole to the network of capillaries around the alveolus. After gaseous exchange, oxygenated blood returns through a venule.

Smoking and the Risk of Cancer

77. What is the most crucial risk of smoking?

Smokers have a much higher risk of developing life-threatening diseases. The most crucial risk is cancer in the lungs, kidneys, oral cavity, larynx, breast, bladder, oesophagus, pancreas and stomach.

78. Name any four organs in which smoking can cause cancer.

Lungs, kidneys, oral cavity, larynx, breast, bladder, oesophagus, pancreas and stomach (any four).

79. How many chemicals does tobacco smoke contain? How many of them are known carcinogens?

Tobacco smoke contains over 4,000 different chemicals, of which more than 69 are known carcinogens.

80. What are carcinogens?

Carcinogens are cancer-causing chemicals.

Harmful Substances of Tobacco Smoke

81. What is tar?

Tar makes up the majority of the particulate matter inhaled by a smoker. It contains many carcinogens.

82. What are the harmful effects of tar?

Tar contains many carcinogens; it stains the teeth and fingers of smokers, stains and damages the lung tissue, and increases the production of mucus and phlegm.

83. What are the harmful effects of nicotine?

Nicotine increases heart rate and blood pressure, causes constriction in the blood vessels, damages the muscles, and disturbs the hormonal systems and metabolism.

84. How can nicotine cause coronary diseases?

Nicotine increases the platelet stickiness in the blood, which can cause blockage and cause coronary diseases.

85. How is carbon monoxide formed in cigarette smoke?

Carbon monoxide is formed when a cigarette is lit.

86. How does carbon monoxide affect the blood?

Carbon monoxide combines with red blood cells and prevents oxygen transportation.

87. How does carbon monoxide affect the lungs?

When carbon monoxide enters the air ways, it stays along with other toxic chemicals of cigarette smoke. These toxins cause inflammation, impair lung functions and increase the risk of lung diseases.

88. Why is benzene harmful?

Benzene, a major component of cigarettes, is a carcinogen and causes blood cancer (leukaemia).

89. What are the effects of formaldehyde present in cigarettes?

Formaldehyde causes cancers and respiratory, skin and gastrointestinal problems.

90. Name any four metals found in cigarette smoke.

Arsenic, nickel, cadmium, chromium and lead. Thirty different metals are found in cigarette smoke, many of which have been linked to cancer.

91. Why are the radioactive compounds in cigarettes dangerous?

All the radioactive compounds found in cigarettes are carcinogens.

92. Name some chemicals of cigarette smoke that are also used in everyday products (Fig. 10.8).

Cadmium (batteries), arsenic (poison), acetic acid (vinegar), nicotine (insecticide), methane (sewer gas), ammonia (toilet cleaner), toluene (industrial solvent), stearic acid (candle wax), methanol (rocket fuel), carbon monoxide (pollution), hexamine (barbeque lighter) and butane (lighter fluid).

Passive Smoking and Social Effects

93. What is passive smoking?

Breathing of smoke-laden air by non-smokers is known as passive smoking.

94. What are the harmful effects of passive smoking?

The majority of non-smokers are allergic to cigarette smoke, which irritates their eyes and throats. They can also develop cancer by continuously living in an environment polluted with cigarette smoke.

95. What steps have been taken against smoking in public places?

Separate zones for smokers are made in public places such as offices, hotels and restaurants, and the Government has banned smoking at public places and on public transport.

96. Why do people not like the company of cigarette smokers?

Because of the bad smell that comes from their mouth.

97. What has the World Health Organization (WHO) asked governments to do to stop young people from starting smoking?

The WHO has called on governments to stop tobacco advertisements.

Lungs and Circulatory System — Summary

98. Describe the biological consequences of smoking in relation to the lungs and the circulatory system.

Lungs: tar stains and damages lung tissue and increases mucus and phlegm; carbon monoxide and other toxins cause inflammation, impair lung functions and increase the risk of lung diseases; carcinogens (tar, benzene, formaldehyde, radioactive compounds, metals) cause cancer, most crucially lung cancer.

Circulatory system: nicotine increases heart rate and blood pressure, constricts blood vessels and increases platelet stickiness, which can cause blockage and coronary diseases; carbon monoxide combines with red blood cells and prevents oxygen transportation; benzene causes blood cancer (leukaemia).

Section 10.4.1 — breathing rate and the breathing centre.

ConditionRate of breathing

Breathing Rate and Exercise

99. State the rate of breathing at rest.
• What is the rate of breathing under resting condition?

Under resting condition the rate of breathing is 15–20 times per minute.

100. State the rate of breathing during exercise.
• What is the breathing rate after exercise?

During exercise the breathing rate increases up to 30–40 times per minute.

101. Why does the rate of breathing increase during exercise?

More oxygen is required for the oxidation of glucose molecules to get more energy. As a result, more carbon dioxide is produced, which accumulates in the blood.

102. What is the breathing centre?
• Which part of the brain controls the rate of breathing?

The medulla of the brain is the breathing centre.

103. How does the breathing centre increase the rate of breathing during exercise?

The medulla detects more carbon dioxide in the blood and sends nerve impulses to the diaphragm and rib muscles at greater speed. The speed of contraction and relaxation of these muscles increases, so the rate and depth of breathing increase.

104. Morning walk is traditionally considered beneficial for health. Analyse this sentence in the context of better gaseous exchange.

Walking is exercise, which increases the rate and depth of breathing, so more oxygen is taken into the blood and more carbon dioxide is removed. Breathing fresh air, which has a higher concentration of oxygen than the blood, also makes the gaseous exchange in the lungs better.

Inspired Air vs Expired Air

105. What is the percentage of oxygen in inspired air and in expired air?

Inspired air contains 21% oxygen and expired air contains 16% oxygen.

106. What is the percentage of carbon dioxide in inspired air and in expired air?

Inspired air contains 0.04% carbon dioxide and expired air contains 4% carbon dioxide.

107. Compare the water vapours in inspired and expired air.

The water vapour in inspired air is variable, whereas expired air is saturated with water vapour.

108. What is the temperature of expired air?

The temperature of expired air is 38 °C (the temperature of inspired air is variable).

109. Why does expired air contain less oxygen and more carbon dioxide than inspired air?

In the alveoli, oxygen diffuses from the inhaled air into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveoli, so the air breathed out has less oxygen and more carbon dioxide.

110. Differentiate between the composition of inspired and expired air.
• Compare the composition of inhaled and exhaled air.

Oxygen: 21% in inspired air, 16% in expired air. Carbon dioxide: 0.04% in inspired air, 4% in expired air. Water vapours: variable in inspired air, saturated in expired air. Temperature: variable in inspired air, 38 °C in expired air.

Bronchitis

111. What is bronchitis?
• Define bronchitis.

When the lining of the bronchi or bronchioles becomes inflamed or infected, the condition is called bronchitis.

112. What are the effects of bronchitis?

Bronchitis reduces the amount of air that can flow and causes the formation of mucus in the airways.

113. What are the causes of bronchitis?

Bronchitis is caused by viruses, bacteria and their particles that irritate the bronchial tubes.

114. How is bronchitis treated?

Short-term bronchitis resolves without treatment in two weeks. If conditions do not improve, medicines are given — antibiotics when it is due to bacterial infection and anti-viral medicines when it is due to viral infection. Coughing helps to remove irritants from the bronchi.

Emphysema

115. What is emphysema?
• Define emphysema.

Emphysema is a severe type of lung disease in which the walls of the alveoli are damaged. The patient feels difficulty in breathing (shortness of breath).

116. What are the causes of emphysema?

Environmental pollution can cause emphysema, and cigarette smoke is the most common cause.

117. Are the effects of emphysema reversible?

No. The effects of emphysema are permanent and irreversible; however, if smoking is stopped, further damage might be reduced.

Pneumonia

118. What is pneumonia?
• Define pneumonia.

Pneumonia is an inflammation in the lungs due to infection in the alveoli. The alveoli are filled with pus.

119. What are the causes of pneumonia?

Pneumonia is caused by bacteria, viruses or fungi.

120. What are the general symptoms of pneumonia?

The general symptoms start as a cold (an upper respiratory infection, for example sneezing, sore throat and cough). These are then followed by high fever (sometimes as high as 104 °F), shaking chills and a cough with sputum production.

121. How high can the fever rise in pneumonia?

In pneumonia the high fever is sometimes as high as 104 °F, and it is accompanied by shaking chills and a cough with sputum production.

122. What do the air sacs look like in pneumonia (Fig. 10.11)?

In pneumonia the air sacs (alveoli) are inflamed and filled with fluid (pus), unlike the normal air sacs, which are filled with air.

123. How is pneumonia transmitted?

Pneumonia can be transmitted by breathing in air with small droplets. These droplets get into the air when an infected person (having these germs) coughs or sneezes.

Asthma

124. What is asthma?
• Define asthma.

Asthma is the inflammation (swelling) in the walls of the bronchi and bronchioles.

125. What happens during an attack of asthma?

During an attack of asthma, the walls of the bronchi and bronchioles swell and their inner passage is reduced. It results in shortness of breath.

126. What are the symptoms of asthma?

The patient experiences recurring attacks of shortness of breath, wheezing (whistling sound when breathing out), coughing and chest tightness.

127. What is wheezing?

Wheezing is the whistling sound produced when an asthma patient breathes out.

128. What is the most common reason for asthma?

The most common reason for asthma is the sensitivity to allergens.

129. What are allergens? Give examples.

Allergens are allergy-causing factors. Examples are pollens, cold, smoke or other chemicals in the air.

130. How do allergens cause an attack of asthma?

When asthma patients inhale allergens such as pollens, cold, smoke or other chemicals in the air, they may suffer an attack of the symptoms of asthma, i.e. the walls of the bronchi and bronchioles swell and the inner passage becomes narrow (constricted).

131. How is asthma treated?

Asthma patients are given short-term treatments. They use medicines which contain chemicals to dilate the bronchioles.

132. What are bronchodilators?

Bronchodilators are medicines that contain chemicals to dilate the bronchioles. They are given to asthma patients in the form of inhalers.

133. Differentiate between asthma and bronchitis.

Asthma: the walls of the bronchi and bronchioles swell (inflammation) and their inner passage is reduced; the most common reason is sensitivity to allergens; treated with bronchodilators given as inhalers.

Bronchitis: the lining of the bronchi or bronchioles becomes inflamed or infected; caused by viruses, bacteria and their particles; antibiotics or anti-viral medicines are used if it does not improve in two weeks.

Lung Cancer

134. What is lung cancer?

Lung cancer is a respiratory disease which is more common in men than in women.

135. What are the most common causes of lung cancer?

The most common causes of lung cancer are smoking, industrial carcinogens and air pollution.

136. What are the symptoms of lung cancer?

Symptoms of lung cancer are cough with sputum, breathlessness and pleural chest pain.

137. What are the main causes of any cancer?

The main causes of any cancer are carcinogens (such as those in tobacco smoke), ionising radiation and viral infection.

138. What is the main contributor to lung cancer?

Smoking is the main contributor to lung cancer. Cigarette smoke contains over 69 known carcinogens (cancer-causing chemicals).

139. Does lung cancer show symptoms in its early stages?

No. In its early stages, lung cancer does not show any symptoms. However, in the advanced stage, when it has fully spread, it is very painful and incurable and becomes fatal.

140. How is lung cancer treated?

The patient of lung cancer is given one or more treatments, including surgery, chemotherapy and radiation therapy.

141. Which respiratory disease is more common in men than in women?

Lung cancer is more common in men than in women.

B. Short Answers

163. Why do plants not need a specialised respiratory system?

In plants, gaseous exchange takes place directly through the general surface (epidermis and cuticle), the stomata of leaves and young stems, and the lenticels of woody stems, by diffusion. Since gases are exchanged directly between the cells and the air through these surfaces, plants do not need a specialised respiratory system.

164. Differentiate between the terms “respiration” and “breathing”.

Respiration: an energy-releasing process at the cellular level in which food molecules (e.g. glucose) are oxidised to carbon dioxide and water.

Breathing: the physical process in which animals move air into and out of their body to get oxygen from air and to release carbon dioxide in it.

165. How are the surface area of leaves and lungs important for gaseous exchange?

A large surface area allows more gas to diffuse in and out at the same time.

Leaves have a broad surface with many stomata through which carbon dioxide and oxygen are exchanged with the air.

In the lungs, the alveoli are thin-walled and covered with a network of blood capillaries, and together they provide a large surface for the exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide.

166. What is the function of the cartilage present in the walls of the trachea and bronchi?

The C-shaped cartilaginous rings in the walls of the trachea (16 to 20 rings) and the smaller rings in the bronchi prevent them from collapsing and keep the passage of air open.

167. What is a diaphragm and what is its role in breathing?

The diaphragm is a dome-shaped sheet of skeletal muscle (a thick muscular structure) below the lungs; it separates the chest cavity from the abdominal cavity.

During inspiration it contracts and becomes flat, which increases the volume of the chest cavity so air rushes in. During expiration it relaxes and becomes dome-shaped again, which decreases the volume and expels the air.

168. What are carcinogens? Name any two carcinogens present in tobacco.

Carcinogens are cancer-causing chemicals. Two carcinogens present in tobacco are benzene (causes blood cancer, leukaemia) and tar (contains many carcinogens). Formaldehyde and radioactive compounds are also carcinogens found in cigarettes.

169. Compare the composition of inhaled and exhaled air.

Oxygen: inhaled air 21%, exhaled air 16%. Carbon dioxide: inhaled air 0.04%, exhaled air 4%. Water vapours: variable in inhaled air, saturated in exhaled air. Temperature: variable in inhaled air, 38 °C in exhaled air.

C. Detailed Answers

170. The gaseous exchange in plants is important for photosynthesis and respiration. Explain the types of gases exchanged and its mechanism.

Gases exchanged: oxygen (O₂) and carbon dioxide (CO₂).

Mechanism: plants exchange gases directly with the environment by diffusion through their general surface (the epidermis, which has a cuticle at some parts), through the stomata (small pores in the epidermis of leaves and young stems) and through the lenticels (special pores in the bark of woody stems).

During the day: all cells respire and the green parts also photosynthesise. Leaves use the carbon dioxide produced in respiration and also take carbon dioxide from the environment for photosynthesis; they use the oxygen produced in photosynthesis for respiration. So during the daytime leaves take in carbon dioxide and release oxygen.

During the night: there is no photosynthesis but all cells respire, so the cells take in oxygen from the environment and release carbon dioxide.

171. Describe the mechanism of breathing in human beings.

Breathing has two phases: inspiration (taking in of air) and expiration (letting out of air), which take place continuously one after the other. Air moves in when the pressure in the lungs is lower than the pressure outside the body, and moves out when the pressure in the lungs is greater than the atmospheric pressure.

Inspiration: the muscles of the diaphragm contract, so it becomes flat instead of dome-shaped, and the intercostal muscles between the ribs contract, so the ribcage expands. The volume of the chest cavity increases and the pressure on the lungs decreases, so atmospheric air rushes into the lungs through the air passageway.

Expiration: the muscles of the ribs and the diaphragm relax. The ribcage goes down to its original position and the diaphragm becomes dome-shaped. The volume of the chest cavity decreases, which increases the pressure on the lungs and expels the air out.

172. Draw a diagram of the air passage in the human body and describe the role of different parts in the flow of air through it.

Diagram (draw and label as in Fig. 10.4 / 10.5): nostril, nasal cavity, pharynx, epiglottis, larynx, trachea, bronchus, bronchioles, alveoli, right lung, left lung, diaphragm.

Path of air: Nostrils → Nasal cavities → Pharynx → Larynx → Trachea → Bronchi → Bronchioles → Alveoli.

Nostrils: two openings that let air from outside into the nasal cavities.

Nasal cavities: air is warmed, moistened and freed from dust by the hairs and the mucous membrane.

Pharynx: muscular passage lined by a mucous membrane; its tonsils and adenoids trap and filter microorganisms.

Larynx (voice box): air passes through it from the pharynx; the glottis leading into the trachea is guarded by the epiglottis, which keeps food out.

Trachea (wind pipe): its ciliated, mucus-secreting inner surface filters, moistens and warms the air; the 16 to 20 C-shaped cartilaginous rings keep it open.

Bronchi: two branches of the trachea, one entering each lung; they divide into smaller bronchi.

Bronchioles: smaller bronchi of 1 mm or less with no cartilage; each ends in a duct opening in a cluster of alveoli.

Alveoli: thin-walled pouches covered with blood capillaries, where gaseous exchange occurs.

173. What are the causes of respiratory disorders such as bronchitis, pneumonia and lung cancer?

Bronchitis: caused by viruses, bacteria and their particles that irritate the bronchial tubes, so that the lining of the bronchi or bronchioles becomes inflamed or infected.

Pneumonia: caused by bacteria, viruses or fungi that infect the alveoli; it spreads when an infected person coughs or sneezes and others breathe in the small droplets.

Lung cancer: the most common causes are smoking, industrial carcinogens and air pollution. Smoking is the main contributor — cigarette smoke contains over 69 known carcinogens (cancer-causing chemicals), e.g. tar, benzene, formaldehyde, radioactive compounds and metals. The main causes of any cancer are carcinogens, ionising radiation and viral infection.

Asthma (also a respiratory disorder): the most common reason is sensitivity to allergens such as pollens, cold, smoke or other chemicals in the air, which make the walls of the bronchi and bronchioles swell.
