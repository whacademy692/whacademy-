Part 1

Understanding the Theorem

The hypotenuse, the legs, and the core formula.

1. What is Pythagoras' Theorem? State it as an equation.
• State the Pythagorean theorem• What is the formula for the Pythagorean theorem?
In any right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse is equal to the sum of the squares of the other two sides. If the legs are \(a\) and \(b\) and the hypotenuse is \(c\):

\(a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2}\)
It only applies when the triangle has a \(90^\circ\) angle.
📝 The theorem is named after Pythagoras, an ancient Greek mathematician — though the relationship was known earlier in other cultures too.
2. How do I identify the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle?
• Which side is the hypotenuse?• Is the hypotenuse always the longest side?
The hypotenuse is the side directly opposite the right angle, and it is always the longest side of the triangle. For example, in \(\triangle ABC\) with \(\angle C = 90^\circ\), the hypotenuse is AB (opposite C), and AC, BC are the two legs (also called catheti).
⛔ Common Mistake: don't assume the hypotenuse is whichever side is written last in a problem — always check which side is opposite the right angle.
3. How do I find the hypotenuse if I know both legs?
• How to calculate the hypotenuse from two sides?
Square each leg, add the two results, then take the square root:

\(c = \sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\)
Worked example: In \(\triangle ABC\), \(AC = 5\) cm, \(BC = 12\) cm and \(\angle C = 90^\circ\). Find AB.
\(AB^{2} = 5^{2}+12^{2} = 25+144 = 169\), so \(AB = \sqrt{169} = 13\) cm.
4. How do I find a missing leg if I know the hypotenuse and the other leg?
• How to find a side when the hypotenuse is given?
Subtract the square of the known leg from the square of the hypotenuse, then take the square root:

\(a = \sqrt{c^{2}-b^{2}}\)
Worked example: The hypotenuse is 13 m and one leg is 12 m. Find the other leg.
\(\sqrt{13^{2}-12^{2}} = \sqrt{169-144} = \sqrt{25} = 5\) m.
⛔ Common Mistake: when finding a leg you SUBTRACT; when finding the hypotenuse you ADD. Mixing these up is the single most common error in this chapter.
Part 2

Applying the Theorem to Real Life

Ladders, cables, screens, rectangles and algebra.

1. How is the theorem used for a ladder leaning against a wall?
• Ladder-against-a-wall Pythagoras problem
The ladder is the hypotenuse, the height it reaches up the wall is one leg, and its distance from the wall's base is the other leg.

Worked example: A ladder's top is 2.4 m above the ground, and its foot is 0.5 m from the wall. Find the ladder's length.
\(\sqrt{2.4^{2}+0.5^{2}} = \sqrt{5.76+0.25} = \sqrt{6.01} \approx 2.45\) m.
2. How do I find the diagonal of a rectangle using the theorem?
• Diagonal of a rectangle formula
A rectangle's diagonal splits it into two right triangles, where the length and breadth are the legs and the diagonal is the hypotenuse:

\(\text{diagonal}^{2} = \text{length}^{2}+\text{breadth}^{2}\)
Worked example: A rectangular field has length 12 m and breadth 9 m. Find its diagonal.
\(\sqrt{12^{2}+9^{2}} = \sqrt{144+81} = \sqrt{225} = 15\) m.
3. How can algebra be combined with the theorem in word problems?
• Rectangle with algebraic sides, find x
Write the sides and diagonal as algebraic expressions, substitute into \(a^{2}+b^{2}=c^{2}\), then solve the resulting equation.

Worked example: A rectangle ABCD has sides \((x+7)\) m and \(x\) m, and diagonal BD \(=(x+9)\) m. Find \(x\).
\((x+7)^{2}+x^{2}=(x+9)^{2} \Rightarrow x^{2}-4x-32=0 \Rightarrow (x-8)(x+4)=0\), so \(x=8\) (rejecting the negative root).
📝 Always reject a negative solution for a length — a side cannot have negative length.
4. How do I find the height of an isosceles triangle using the theorem?
• Isosceles triangle height formula
The height from the apex bisects the base at a right angle, creating a right triangle whose legs are the height and half the base, with one equal side as the hypotenuse.

Worked example: An isosceles triangle has equal sides of 13 cm and a base of 10 cm. Find its height.
Half-base \(=5\) cm, so height \(=\sqrt{13^{2}-5^{2}}=\sqrt{169-25}=\sqrt{144}=12\) cm.
Part 3

The Converse of the Theorem

Testing whether a triangle is right-angled, from its sides alone.

1. What is the converse of the theorem, and how is it used?
• How to test if a triangle is right-angled from its sides?
If a triangle's three sides \(a, b, c\) (with \(c\) the longest) satisfy \(a^{2}+b^{2}=c^{2}\), the triangle must be right-angled, with the right angle opposite \(c\). If \(a^{2}+b^{2} \neq c^{2}\), it is not right-angled — no angle measurement is needed.
📝 Always square the longest side separately, then compare it to the sum of the squares of the other two.
2. Is a triangle with sides 7 cm, 24 cm and 25 cm right-angled?
• Check if 7, 24, 25 is a Pythagorean triple
Yes. \(7^{2}+24^{2} = 49+576 = 625 = 25^{2}\), so the converse confirms this triangle is right-angled, with the right angle opposite the 25 cm side.
⛔ Common Mistake: don't just check if the numbers "look close" — the equality a² + b² = c² must hold exactly.
3. What are Pythagorean triples, and how are new ones made from old ones?
• List of Pythagorean triples• Scaling a Pythagorean triple
A Pythagorean triple is a set of three whole numbers \(a,b,c\) with \(a^{2}+b^{2}=c^{2}\):

Smallest
\((3, 4, 5)\)
Common
\((5, 12, 13)\)
Common
\((8, 15, 17)\)
Multiplying every number in a triple by the same positive number always gives another valid triple — for example, doubling \((3,4,5)\) gives \((6,8,10)\).
