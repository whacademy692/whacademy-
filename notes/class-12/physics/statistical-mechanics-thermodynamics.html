Chapter Overview

Statistical mechanics is the branch of physics that applies statistical methods to systems made of a very large number of particles. It links the microscopic world (speed and kinetic energy of individual molecules) with the macroscopic world (pressure, volume, temperature and entropy) that we measure in the laboratory. In this chapter you will see how the random motion of gas molecules and their collisions with the walls of a container produce pressure; you will derive P = mN⟨v²⟩/3V, relate temperature to the average translational kinetic energy (⟨KE⟩ = 3kT/2), and calculate the root-mean-square speed of gas molecules. You will then see where the ideal gas model fails and how Van der Waals corrected it for real gases, why gravity matters inside stars, and how extreme conditions create unusual states of matter: degenerate matter (white dwarfs and neutron stars), Bose-Einstein condensates, superfluids and superconductors.

Part 1 PHY-01PHY-02

Pressure Exerted by Gas Molecules

Section 16.1 — collisions, momentum transfer and the derivation of P = mN⟨v²⟩/3V.

The molecules of a gas are in continuous random motion inside a container. They collide with one another and with the walls. Because the collisions with the walls are elastic, each molecule bounces back and transfers momentum to the wall. By Newton's second law, the rate of transfer of momentum (Δp/Δt) to the wall is equal to the force F on the wall, and the force per unit area (A) is the pressure.

Pressure P = F/A = (Δp/Δt) × (1/A)

Hence the pressure depends on (i) the number of molecules that hit each side of the box in one second and (ii) the force with which a molecule collides with the wall.

Note: (For Your Information) Pressure is affected by volume. When the volume of the container decreases, molecules in the smaller space hit the walls more frequently, giving a higher pressure. When the volume increases, there is more space, collisions are less frequent and the pressure is lower.

Setting up the model

Consider an ideal gas of N identical molecules, each of mass m, moving rapidly and randomly in a cubic box of side l. Take a single molecule moving towards face-1 with x-component of velocity vx1 (Figure 16.1: gas molecules colliding with the walls of the container; Face-1 and Face-2 are opposite faces perpendicular to the x-axis).

Derivation of the pressure of an ideal gas

Step 1: Initial momentum along x = m vx1. After a perfectly elastic collision with face-1 the momentum is reversed: final momentum = −m vx1. Change in momentum of the molecule:

\[\Delta p = -mv_{x1}-mv_{x1} = -2mv_{x1}\](16.1)

By conservation of momentum, the momentum imparted to the wall is 2 m vx1.

Step 2: The molecule travels to face-2, rebounds and returns, covering a distance 2l before hitting face-1 again. The time taken:

\[\Delta t = \dfrac{2l}{v_{x1}}\](16.2)

Step 3: Rate of change of momentum of the molecule along the x-axis:

\[\dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{-2mv_{x1}}{2l/v_{x1}} = -\dfrac{mv_{x1}^{2}}{l}\](16.3)

Step 4: Rate of change of momentum = force. The force on the molecule by the wall is −m vx1²/l; by Newton's third law the force exerted by the molecule on the wall is equal and opposite:

\[F_{x1} = \dfrac{mv_{x1}^{2}}{l}\]

Step 5: Similarly for the other molecules Fx2 = m vx2²/l, …, FxN = m vxN²/l. Total force along x:

\[F_x = F_{x1}+F_{x2}+\dots+F_{xN} = \dfrac{m}{l}\left(v_{x1}^{2}+v_{x2}^{2}+\dots+v_{xN}^{2}\right)\]

Multiplying and dividing by N, and writing ⟨vx²⟩ = (vx1² + vx2² + … + vxN²)/N (the mean-square velocity along x):

\[F_x = \dfrac{mN}{l}\langle v_x^{2}\rangle\](16.4, 16.5)

Step 6: The mean-square velocity is the sum of the mean-square components:

\[\langle v^{2}\rangle = \langle v_x^{2}\rangle+\langle v_y^{2}\rangle+\langle v_z^{2}\rangle\](16.6)

Because of the random motion of a very large number of molecules, no direction is preferred: ⟨vx²⟩ = ⟨vy²⟩ = ⟨vz²⟩. So ⟨v²⟩ = 3⟨vx²⟩ :

\[\langle v_x^{2}\rangle = \dfrac{\langle v^{2}\rangle}{3}\](16.7)

Step 7: Putting this in Fx and generalising (the force per unit area is the same on all faces of the cube):

\[F = \dfrac{mN}{l}\times\dfrac{\langle v^{2}\rangle}{3}\](16.8)

Step 8: By Pascal's law the pressure is transmitted equally on all faces. Pressure = force/area, with area l²:

\[P = \dfrac{F}{l^{2}}\](16.9)
\[P = \dfrac{mN}{3l^{3}}\langle v^{2}\rangle\]

Since l³ = V (volume of the gas):

P = (mN)/(3V) ⟨v²⟩ (16.10)
This is the expression for the pressure of an ideal gas.
Common mistake: Do not confuse N (number of molecules) with n (number of moles), or m (mass of ONE molecule) with M (molar mass). In P = mN⟨v²⟩/3V, mN is the total mass of the gas.

Exam Questions — Pressure Exerted By Gas Molecules

1. Define the pressure of a gas.
• What is meant by the pressure exerted by a gas?

The pressure of a gas is the force exerted by its molecules per unit area of the walls of the container, produced by their collisions with the walls: P = F/A.

2. How do gas molecules exert pressure on the walls of a container?
• Explain how the motion of gas molecules produces pressure.
  • Gas molecules move randomly and collide continuously with the walls. In every elastic collision the molecule's momentum changes and an equal momentum is transferred to the wall.
  • By Newton's second law, the rate of transfer of momentum (Δp/Δt) is the force on the wall, and force per unit area is pressure: P = (Δp/Δt)(1/A).
3. On what two factors does the pressure of a gas depend?
• What determines the pressure that a gas exerts on the walls of a box?

(i) The number of molecules hitting each side of the box in one second, and (ii) the force with which a molecule collides with the wall.

4. What type of collisions do gas molecules make with the walls of the container?

Perfectly elastic collisions — the molecule rebounds with the same speed and only the direction of its momentum is reversed.

5. What is the change in momentum of a gas molecule when it collides with face-1 of a cubical container?
• Find the change in momentum of a molecule of mass m and x-velocity vx1 after an elastic collision with the wall.

Initial momentum = m vx1, final momentum = −m vx1. Δp = −m vx1 − m vx1 = −2 m vx1. By conservation of momentum the momentum imparted to the wall is 2 m vx1.

6. What is the time between two successive collisions of a molecule with the same face of a cubical box of side l?

Between two collisions with face-1 the molecule covers a distance 2l, so Δt = 2l / vx1.

7. Write the rate of change of momentum of a gas molecule along the x-axis.
• What is the force exerted by one gas molecule on the wall of a cubical container of side l?

Δp/Δt = (−2 m vx1)/(2l / vx1) = −m vx1²/l. By Newton's third law, the force exerted by the molecule on the wall is Fx1 = m vx1²/l.

8. Which laws of physics are used in deriving the pressure of an ideal gas?

Newton's second law (force = rate of change of momentum), Newton's third law (equal and opposite forces), the law of conservation of momentum, and Pascal's law (pressure transmitted equally on all faces).

9. What is the total force exerted by N gas molecules on the wall along the x-axis?

Fx = (m/l)(vx1² + vx2² + … + vxN²) = (mN/l)⟨vx²⟩, where ⟨vx²⟩ is the mean-square velocity of all molecules along the x-direction.

10. Define mean-square velocity (speed) of gas molecules.

It is the average of the squares of the velocities of all the molecules: ⟨v²⟩ = (v₁² + v₂² + … + vN²)/N.

11. How is the mean-square velocity related to its x, y and z components?

⟨v²⟩ = ⟨vx²⟩ + ⟨vy²⟩ + ⟨vz²⟩.

12. Why is ⟨vx²⟩ = ⟨vy²⟩ = ⟨vz²⟩ for gas molecules?
• Show that ⟨vx²⟩ = ⟨v²⟩/3.

Because of the random motion of a very large number of molecules, no direction is preferred, so the three components are equal. Then ⟨v²⟩ = 3⟨vx²⟩, i.e. ⟨vx²⟩ = ⟨v²⟩/3.

13. Why is the pressure of a gas the same on all faces of a cubical container?

According to Pascal's law the pressure of a gas is transmitted equally on all faces of the container; also ⟨vx²⟩ = ⟨vy²⟩ = ⟨vz²⟩, so the force per unit area is equal on every face.

14. Write the expression for the pressure of an ideal gas.
• State the kinetic-theory expression for the pressure of a gas in terms of the mean-square speed.

P = mN⟨v²⟩/(3V), where m = mass of one molecule, N = number of molecules, V = volume and ⟨v²⟩ = mean-square speed.

15. How does the pressure of a gas change when its volume is decreased at constant temperature? Explain.
• Why does the pressure of a gas increase when it is compressed?

On decreasing the volume, the molecules collide with the walls more frequently, so the pressure increases. On increasing the volume there is more space, collisions are less frequent and the pressure decreases (P = mN⟨v²⟩/3V, so P ∝ 1/V at constant temperature).

Pressure, Density, Temperature and Kinetic Energy

Section 16.1 (contd.) — P = (2/3)N₀⟨½mv²⟩, P = ⅓ρ⟨v²⟩, PV = NkT and ⟨KE⟩ = 3kT/2.

Pressure in terms of average translational kinetic energy

Multiply and divide the right-hand side of Eq. (16.10) by 2:

\[P = \dfrac{2}{3}\times\dfrac{N}{V}\left\langle\dfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}\right\rangle\](16.11)

Putting N/V = N₀ (number of molecules per unit volume):

\[P = \dfrac{2}{3}N_0\left\langle\dfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}\right\rangle\]

Since (2/3)N₀ is constant, P ∝ ⟨K.E⟩.

The pressure exerted by gas molecules on the walls of the container is directly proportional to the average translational kinetic energy of the gas molecules.

Pressure in terms of density

Since mN/V = ρ (density), Eq. (16.10) gives:

\[P = \dfrac{1}{3}\rho\langle v^{2}\rangle\](16.12)

Temperature in terms of average translational kinetic energy

The ideal gas law is PV = nRT (16.13), where n = number of moles, V = volume, T = absolute temperature and R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ is the universal gas constant. The number of moles is n = N/NA, where NA = 6.022×10²³ per mole is the Avogadro number. So:

\[PV = N\left(\dfrac{R}{N_A}\right)T\]

R/NA = k is the Boltzmann constant, k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J K⁻¹. Hence:

\[PV = NkT\](16.14)

Eq. (16.11) can be written as PV = (2N/3)⟨½mv²⟩ (16.15). Comparing (16.14) and (16.15):

\[NkT = \dfrac{2N}{3}\left\langle\dfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}\right\rangle\]
\[T = \dfrac{2}{3k}\left\langle\dfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}\right\rangle\](16.16)
T ∝ ⟨K.E⟩
The absolute temperature of an ideal gas is directly proportional to the average translational kinetic energy of its molecules.
Average translational KE of one molecule: ⟨½mv²⟩ = (3/2) k T
Note: (For Your Information) Values of the Boltzmann constant per molecule in different units: k = 1.38×10⁻¹⁶ erg K⁻¹ = 1.38×10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ = 3.3×10⁻²⁴ cal K⁻¹.
Common mistake: Temperature in all gas formulas must be the ABSOLUTE temperature in kelvin: T(K) = t(°C) + 273. Using °C is the most common numerical mistake.
Tip: Constants to remember: R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J K⁻¹, NA = 6.022×10²³ mol⁻¹ and k = R/NA.

Part 3 PHY-03

Root Mean Square Speed

Section 16.2 — v_rms, average speed and the effect of temperature and molar mass.

According to the kinetic theory, every molecule moves with a different velocity. If v₁, v₂, v₃, …, vN are the speeds of N molecules, the mean-square speed is found by adding the squares of the speeds and dividing by N:

\[\langle v^{2}\rangle = \dfrac{v_1^{2}+v_2^{2}+v_3^{2}+\dots+v_N^{2}}{N}\]

Taking the square root, and writing √⟨v²⟩ = vr.m.s :

vr.m.s = √[(v₁² + v₂² + v₃² + … + vN²)/N] (16.17)
The square root of the mean-square speed of gas molecules is called the root mean square (rms) speed.

Average speed: the arithmetic mean of the speeds of the molecules of a gas at a given temperature.

\[v_{av} = \dfrac{v_1+v_2+v_3+\dots+v_N}{N}\](16.18)

Another expression for rms speed (from pressure)

Start from Eq. (16.10): PV = (mN/3)⟨v²⟩ (16.19). We also know PV = NkT (16.20). Comparing:

\[\dfrac{mN}{3}\langle v^{2}\rangle = NkT\]
\[\langle v^{2}\rangle = \dfrac{3kT}{m}\](16.21)
vr.m.s = √(3kT/m) (16.22)
vr.m.s ∝ √T — the rms speed is directly proportional to the square root of the absolute temperature.

Putting k = R/NA gives vrms = √(3RT/mNA). With mNA = M (molar mass of the gas):

vr.m.s = √(3RT/M) (16.23)
At constant temperature, vr.m.s ∝ 1/√M

At constant temperature the rms speed is inversely proportional to the square root of the molar mass. A gas of smaller molar mass has a comparatively higher speed; so the rms speed of hydrogen molecules is four times that of oxygen molecules at the same temperature (√(32/2) = 4).

From Eq. (16.12), P = (1/3)ρ⟨v²⟩, so ⟨v²⟩ = 3P/ρ, and taking the square root:

vr.m.s = √(3P/ρ) (16.24)
From (16.22), (16.23), (16.24): vr.m.s = √(3kT/m) = √(3RT/M) = √(3P/ρ) (16.25)

Part 4 PHY-05

Non-Ideal Gases and the Van der Waals Equation

Section 16.3 — ideal gas model, statistical mechanics and corrections for real gases.

Behaviour of ideal gases

In the ideal gas model the size of gas molecules is negligible, there are no interactions between molecules, and the collisions of molecules with each other and with the walls are perfectly elastic. A gas that obeys the ideal gas law (PV = nRT) is called an ideal gas. The law governs macroscopic properties such as temperature, pressure, volume, quantity of gas and entropy. No truly ideal gas exists in nature, but gases approach the ideal state at low pressure and high temperature.

The temperature of an ideal gas is T = (m/3k)⟨v²⟩. This shows that temperature (a macroscopic property) is defined by the motion of individual particles (a microscopic quantity). Such connections between microscopic and macroscopic quantities gave rise to a branch of physics called statistical mechanics.

Statistical mechanics connects the microscopic details of a system (motion, energy and interaction of individual particles) with the macroscopic observables we measure (temperature, pressure, volume and entropy). It provides the mathematical foundation for thermodynamics, which is otherwise a phenomenological theory.

Behaviour of non-ideal (real) gases

The ideal gas law works well at large volumes, high temperatures and low pressure, but it fails at high pressure, high density and low temperature (when molecules move slowly and interact with each other). It also fails when strong gravitational forces act, as in stars. To account for these deviations, modifications were made to the ideal gas law.

Van der Waals equation

Van der Waals modified PV = nRT so that it can be used for real gases over wide ranges of temperature and pressure. He assumed that gas molecules are hard spheres with definite volume and that two molecules interact with each other, especially at low temperatures when they move slowly. He made two corrections.

(i) Volume correction: the molecules have finite size, so some part of the volume V is occupied by the molecules and the space available for their motion is slightly less than V. Roughly, b is the volume of one mole of gas molecules, so the effective volume becomes (V − b) (Figure 16.2: ideal gas, real gas and excluded volume b).

(ii) Pressure correction: inside the container the attractions between molecules cancel each other, but molecules near the surface and walls feel a net inward force (Figure 16.3). The effective pressure of the real gas is (P + a/V²).

One mole: (P + a/V²)(V − b) = RT (16.26)
n moles: (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT (16.27)

Here a and b are empirical constants whose values are different for different gases. The equation describes real gases but can be used for ideal gases as well. At high temperature or very low pressure the volume V is very large, so the terms a/V² and b can be neglected and the Van der Waals equation reduces to PV = nRT.

Tip: Remember the two signs: pressure is CORRECTED UPWARD (P + an²/V²) because attraction reduces the measured pressure; volume is CORRECTED DOWNWARD (V − nb) because molecules occupy space.

Gravitational Effects and Hydrostatic Equilibrium

Section 16.4 — why gravity matters in stars.

In the ideal gas model at laboratory scale, gravity is much weaker than other factors such as the kinetic energy of the gas molecules and the pressure of the gas, so it makes sense to ignore gravity. But on a large scale, e.g. in a star, gravity plays an important role in stability, and accounting for it needs much more complex models than the ideal gas model.

In the core of a star, heat produced by fusion, radiation pressure and degeneracy pressure (a quantum effect) generate a net outward pressure that is counterbalanced by gravity. This state is known as hydrostatic equilibrium (Figure 16.4: gas pressure acts outward, gravity acts inward) and it keeps the star stable.

Part 6 PHY-06

Degenerate Matter, White Dwarfs and Neutron Stars

Sections 16.5 – 16.5.2 — the Pauli exclusion principle and stellar remnants.

16.5 BEHAVIOUR OF MATTER UNDER EXTREME PHYSICAL CONDITIONS

In the universe there are many cases where matter has evolved into extreme physical conditions. These conditions — extremely high or extremely low pressure, materials of high density, or materials under high gravitational force — can alter the behaviour of matter and lead to unusual states such as degenerate matter and to phenomena such as Bose-Einstein condensation, superconductivity and superfluidity.

16.5.1 DEGENERATE MATTER

Extremely high pressure can force particles to interact so closely that quantum mechanical effects, particularly the Pauli Exclusion Principle, become significant.

Pauli Exclusion Principle: Two electrons cannot occupy the same quantum state.

Degenerate matter forms when gravity squeezes atoms so tightly under extremely high pressure in a star that they change from their normal state. Normally atoms consist of a nucleus with electrons revolving around it. Under extremely high pressure, electrons are pressed tightly against the nucleus in high-energy states (as the Pauli principle requires) and cannot move freely. They then resist further compression; this resistance is called degeneracy pressure, and it counteracts gravity and other compressive forces (Figure 16.5). Degenerate matter is therefore matter packed so tightly that its electrons are forced into a tightly packed configuration. It is typically formed in the cores of white dwarfs and neutron stars, where gravity compresses the material to incredibly high densities.

White dwarfs

White dwarfs are formed from the remnants of low- to intermediate-mass stars. When a star has used up all of its nuclear fusion fuel, it removes its outer layers and contracts to form a white dwarf. In a white dwarf the high gravitational force is balanced by electron degeneracy pressure, which arises from the Pauli exclusion principle (Figure 16.6): gravity pulls material together while electrons are forced into high-energy states, forming an equilibrium state.

16.5.2 NEUTRON STARS

Neutron stars are formed from the remnants of more massive stars. Matter is further compressed by gravity, so electrons and protons combine to form neutrons. Neutron degeneracy pressure arises due to the Pauli exclusion principle and balances gravity, preventing further compression (Figure 16.7). This stable state of matter is a neutron star. Although a neutron star is only about 20 km in diameter, it is about 1.5 times more massive than the Sun, with overall densities of the order of 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³. Newly formed "hot neutron stars" can have a surface temperature of several million degrees Celsius.

Note: (For Your Information) The temperature inside a newly formed neutron star is around 10¹¹ to 10¹² K. However, the huge number of neutrinos it emits carries away so much energy that the temperature of an isolated neutron star falls within a few years to around 10⁶ K.

Sections 16.5.3 – 16.5.5 — matter at extremely low temperature.

16.5.3 BOSE-EINSTEIN CONDENSATION

Bose-Einstein condensation (BEC) is a state of matter that forms at extremely low temperatures, close to absolute zero, when the kinetic energies of particles decrease significantly. At low temperature, bosons (e.g. photons, W bosons, Z bosons) can condense into the same state, known as a Bose-Einstein condensate. This is different from degenerate matter, where two fermions (e.g. electrons) cannot exist in the same state. A BEC exhibits unique properties such as superfluidity and superconductivity. (BEC was predicted by Bose and Einstein in the 1920s.)

16.5.4 SUPERFLUIDITY

A notable property of BEC is superfluidity, where the condensate shows zero viscosity and flows without resistance. It is observed when certain fluids are cooled to extremely low temperatures, close to absolute zero. It gives special qualities, such as the capacity to pass through incredibly small spaces at a steady speed without the aid of outside forces, and the ability to form persistent vortices. In fluid dynamics, a vortex is a region in a fluid in which the flow revolves around an axis line, which may be straight or curved.

16.5.5 SUPERCONDUCTIVITY

In certain conditions, BEC leads to superconductivity, where electrical resistance drops to zero and current flows without resistance. This occurs when two electrons form boson-like entities called Cooper pairs, which can occupy the same state and move through the material without scattering. Below the superconducting transition temperature, paired electrons form a condensate (a macroscopically occupied single quantum state that flows without resistance).

Superconductivity is the property of certain materials to conduct current without energy loss when cooled below a critical temperature (transition temperature). Superconductors are substances whose resistivity becomes zero at very low specific temperatures.

Critical temperature (Tc): the low temperature at and below which the resistivity ρ of a substance becomes zero. Also called the superconducting transition temperature (Figure 16.8: resistivity against temperature — resistivity of a normal metal falls gradually, while that of a superconductor drops abruptly to zero at Tc).

Superconductivity was first discovered in 1911 by the Dutch physicist Heike Kamerlingh Onnes when he observed the sudden disappearance of electrical resistance in mercury at an extremely low temperature (4.2 K). In 1988 some new ceramics (thallium, calcium, barium, copper and oxygen) showed superconductivity even at a critical temperature of 125 K. A lanthanum superhydride (LaH₁₀) compound at a pressure of around 170 gigapascals shows superconductivity with a critical temperature of 250 K, the highest critical temperature confirmed so far in a superconducting material.

112. What are some potential applications of superconductors in transportation?

Magnetic levitation (Maglev) trains, which float above the track using powerful superconducting electromagnets; efficient power transmission and small powerful electric motors also help transport systems.

113. Provide an example of a high-temperature superconductor.

A ceramic containing thallium, calcium, barium, copper and oxygen (Tc = 125 K, discovered 1988); lanthanum superhydride LaH₁₀ (Tc = 250 K at about 170 GPa) is the highest confirmed so far.

C. Comprehensive Questions

114. What is the pressure of gas? How do gas molecules exert pressure on the walls of a container?
  • Pressure of a gas is the force exerted by its molecules per unit area of the walls of the container: P = F/A.
  • Gas molecules are in continuous random motion and collide elastically with the walls. In each collision the molecule's momentum changes (Δp = −2mvx for a molecule striking a wall perpendicular to x) and the same momentum is transferred to the wall. By Newton's second law, the rate of transfer of momentum (Δp/Δt) is the force on the wall; the force per unit area gives the pressure, P = (Δp/Δt)(1/A).
  • The pressure depends on the number of molecules hitting the wall per second and on the force of each collision.
115. Derive an expression for the pressure of an ideal gas and show that pressure is directly proportional to the average translational kinetic energy of gas molecules.
  • Take N molecules of mass m in a cubic box of side l. For one molecule with x-velocity vx1, the change in momentum on elastic collision with face-1 is Δp = −2mvx1 (so 2mvx1 is given to the wall).
  • The time between two collisions with the same face is Δt = 2l/vx1, so the force on the wall is Fx1 = (2mvx1)/(2l/vx1) = mvx1²/l.
  • For N molecules: Fx = (m/l)(vx1² + vx2² + … + vxN²) = (mN/l)⟨vx²⟩.
  • Because the motion is random, ⟨vx²⟩ = ⟨vy²⟩ = ⟨vz²⟩ = ⟨v²⟩/3, so F = (mN/l)(⟨v²⟩/3).
  • Pressure P = F/l² = mN⟨v²⟩/(3l³) = mN⟨v²⟩/(3V) (as l³ = V).
  • Multiplying and dividing by 2: P = (2/3)(N/V)⟨½mv²⟩ = (2/3)N₀⟨½mv²⟩. Since (2/3)N₀ is constant, P ∝ ⟨K.E⟩, i.e. pressure is directly proportional to the average translational kinetic energy of the molecules.
116. Describe the root mean square speed of the ideal gas molecule and derive its expressions.
  • The rms speed is the square root of the mean-square speed: vrms = √⟨v²⟩ = √[(v₁² + v₂² + … + vN²)/N].
  • From P = mN⟨v²⟩/3V we get PV = (mN/3)⟨v²⟩, and PV = NkT. Equating: ⟨v²⟩ = 3kT/m, so vrms = √(3kT/m); thus vrms ∝ √T.
  • With k = R/NA and mNA = M: vrms = √(3RT/M); at constant T, vrms ∝ 1/√M.
  • From P = (1/3)ρ⟨v²⟩: vrms = √(3P/ρ).
  • Hence vrms = √(3kT/m) = √(3RT/M) = √(3P/ρ).
117. Derive an expression for the average translational kinetic energy of the ideal gas molecule.
  • The pressure of an ideal gas is P = mN⟨v²⟩/(3V), so PV = (2N/3)⟨½mv²⟩.
  • The ideal gas law in terms of molecules is PV = NkT.
  • Equating the two: NkT = (2N/3)⟨½mv²⟩, so T = (2/3k)⟨½mv²⟩.
  • Therefore the average translational kinetic energy of one molecule is ⟨½mv²⟩ = (3/2)kT, which depends only on the absolute temperature.
118. Describe the modification of the ideal gas model to discuss the behaviour of non-ideal gases using the Van der Waals equation.
  • The ideal gas law PV = nRT works at high temperature and low pressure but fails at high pressure, high density and low temperature. Van der Waals assumed that molecules are hard spheres of definite volume that attract one another, and made two corrections.
  • Volume correction: molecules occupy space, so the volume available for motion is V − nb (b ≈ volume of one mole of molecules).
  • Pressure correction: molecules near the walls feel a net inward pull because of attraction, so the effective pressure is P + an²/V² (a, b are empirical constants, different for different gases).
  • Van der Waals equation: (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT (for one mole (P + a/V²)(V − b) = RT).
  • At high temperature or very low pressure V is very large, a/V² and b become negligible and the equation reduces to PV = nRT.
119. Describe the behaviour of matter under extreme physical conditions. What is degenerate matter, and how are white dwarfs and neutron stars formed?
  • Extreme conditions (very high or very low pressure, very high density, strong gravity) lead to unusual states such as degenerate matter, BEC, superfluidity and superconductivity.
  • Degenerate matter: under extreme pressure from gravity, electrons are pressed tightly against the nucleus in high-energy states (Pauli exclusion principle: two electrons cannot occupy the same quantum state) and resist further compression. This resistance is degeneracy pressure, which balances gravity.
  • White dwarf: the remnant of a low- to intermediate-mass star that has used up its fusion fuel, shed its outer layers and contracted; gravity is balanced by electron degeneracy pressure.
  • Neutron star: the remnant of a more massive star; gravity compresses matter so that electrons and protons combine to form neutrons; neutron degeneracy pressure balances gravity. It is about 20 km across, about 1.5 solar masses and has a density of about 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³.
120. Discuss superfluidity and superconductivity on the basis of Bose-Einstein condensation.
  • Bose-Einstein condensation: at extremely low temperatures, close to absolute zero, bosons condense into the same quantum state, giving a BEC with unique properties.
  • Superfluidity: the condensate has zero viscosity and flows without resistance; it can pass through incredibly small spaces at a steady speed and can form persistent vortices.
  • Superconductivity: when two electrons form boson-like Cooper pairs, they can occupy the same state and move through the material without scattering. Below the critical temperature Tc the resistance drops to zero and current flows without energy loss.
  • Examples and uses: Hg (4.2 K), Al (1.18 K), Sn (3.72 K), Pb (7.2 K); ceramics at 125 K; LaH₁₀ at 250 K under ≈170 GPa. Uses: powerful electromagnets, particle accelerators, Maglev trains, small powerful motors, fast computer chips and efficient power lines.

D. Numerical Problems

121. The mass of a helium atom is 6.64×10⁻²⁷ kg. Calculate the root mean square speed of a helium atom in a gas at a temperature of 15 °C. (Ans: 1.34×10³ m s⁻¹)
  • T = 15 + 273 = 288 K, m = 6.64×10⁻²⁷ kg, k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J K⁻¹
  • vrms = √(3kT/m) = √(3 × 1.38×10⁻²³ × 288 / 6.64×10⁻²⁷) = √(1.796×10⁶)
  • vrms = 1.34×10³ m s⁻¹
122. At which temperature will the root mean square speed of the oxygen molecules become equal to the escape velocity of the Earth (11.2 km s⁻¹)? (Mass of one molecule of oxygen is 5.3×10⁻²⁶ kg and Boltzmann constant k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J K⁻¹.) (Ans: 1.6×10⁵ K)
  • vrms = √(3kT/m) ⇒ T = m v²/(3k), with v = 11.2×10³ m s⁻¹
  • T = (5.3×10⁻²⁶ × (11.2×10³)²)/(3 × 1.38×10⁻²³) = (6.65×10⁻¹⁸)/(4.14×10⁻²³)
  • T = 1.6×10⁵ K
123. The mass of a molecule of a gas is 6.4×10⁻²⁷ kg. Calculate the root mean square speed of the gas molecule and the kinetic energy per molecule at temperature 400 K. (Ans: 1.6×10³ m s⁻¹, 8.3×10⁻²¹ J)
  • vrms = √(3kT/m) = √(3 × 1.38×10⁻²³ × 400 / 6.4×10⁻²⁷) = √(2.59×10⁶) = 1.6×10³ m s⁻¹
  • K.E per molecule = (3/2)kT = 1.5 × 1.38×10⁻²³ × 400 = 8.3×10⁻²¹ J
124. At which temperature does the root mean square speed of gas molecules become double that at 27 °C (pressure of gas is kept constant)? (Ans: 927 °C)
  • T₁ = 27 + 273 = 300 K. v ∝ √T, so v₂/v₁ = √(T₂/T₁) = 2 ⇒ T₂ = 4T₁
  • T₂ = 4 × 300 = 1200 K = 1200 − 273 = 927 °C
125. Determine the root mean square speed of argon atoms at temperature 40 °C. The molar mass of argon is 39.95 g mol⁻¹. (Ans: 442 m s⁻¹)
  • T = 40 + 273 = 313 K, M = 39.95×10⁻³ kg mol⁻¹, R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹
  • vrms = √(3RT/M) = √(3 × 8.314 × 313 / 39.95×10⁻³) = √(1.954×10⁵)
  • vrms = 442 m s⁻¹
126. Calculate the number of gas molecules in a cubic metre of gas at standard temperature and pressure (STP). (Ans: 2.68×10²⁵ molecules)
  • At STP: P = 1.01×10⁵ Pa, T = 273 K, V = 1 m³. From PV = NkT:
  • N = PV/(kT) = (1.01×10⁵ × 1)/(1.38×10⁻²³ × 273) = 2.68×10²⁵ molecules
127. A vessel A contains hydrogen and another vessel B, whose volume is twice that of vessel A, contains the same mass of oxygen at the same temperature. Find (i) the ratio of the root mean square speeds of hydrogen and oxygen gases, (ii) the ratio of pressures of gases in vessels A and B. Molecular masses of hydrogen and oxygen are 2 and 32 respectively. (Ans: 4:1 and 32:1)
  • (i) vrms ∝ 1/√M: vH/vO = √(MO/MH) = √(32/2) = 4, i.e. 4:1.
  • (ii) Let the mass of each gas be m and VA = V, VB = 2V. Moles: nH = m/2, nO = m/32. From P = nRT/V:
  • PA = (m/2)RT/V, PB = (m/32)RT/(2V)
  • PA/PB = (1/2)/(1/64) = 32, i.e. 32:1.
