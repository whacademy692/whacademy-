Chapter Overview

A child on a swing, the pendulum of a wall clock, a vibrating guitar string and the beating of the heart all repeat their motion again and again about a fixed position. This repeating to-and-fro motion is called oscillation, and its simplest and most important form is Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM). In this chapter you will learn the language of oscillations (displacement, amplitude, time period, frequency, angular frequency and phase), see why SHM is the projection of uniform circular motion, study the mass-spring system and the simple pendulum, and follow the conversion between kinetic and potential energy. You will then compare free, forced and damped oscillations, learn how resonance occurs, where it is useful (radio, MRI, microwave oven, guitar, Rubens tube, Chladni plates, acoustic levitation) and where it must be avoided (bridges and airplane wings, as in the Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse).

Part 1 PHY-01

Oscillations

Section 3.1 — Describe simple examples of free oscillations and define oscillatory motion

A child on a swing, the pendulum of a clock and children bouncing on a trampoline all move back and forth between two points. Such systems oscillate.

Key points
  • Oscillatory (vibratory) motion: when a body moves to and fro about its mean position.
  • One oscillation (vibration): the complete round trip of the body about its mean position, i.e. mean position → extreme position A → back through the mean position → extreme position B → back to the mean position.
  • Examples: motion of the Earth during an earthquake, wings of birds in flight, a guitar string producing music, vibration of atoms in a crystal, beating of the heart, and a mass attached to a stretched or compressed spring after release.
NoteOcean waves, sound waves (particles of the medium oscillate) and light waves (electromagnetic radiation) all show oscillatory behaviour.

Questions & Answers — Oscillations

1. Define oscillatory motion.
• What is meant by vibratory motion?

When a body moves to and fro about its mean position, the motion is called vibratory or oscillatory motion.

2. Define one oscillation.
• What is one vibration of an oscillating body?

The complete round trip of an oscillating or vibrating body about its mean position is called one oscillation or vibration.

3. Describe one oscillation of a body between extreme positions A and B.

The body moves from the mean position O to extreme position A, returns through O to extreme position B, and then comes back to O.

4. Name any four examples of oscillating systems.
• Give some examples of oscillatory motion in our surroundings.

A child on a swing, the pendulum of a clock, a bouncing trampoline, a vibrating guitar string, atoms in a crystal, the beating heart.

5. What is the mean (equilibrium) position?

The position about which the body oscillates and where it would rest if no net force acted on it (e.g. a spring neither stretched nor compressed, x = 0).

6. Give two examples of free oscillation.

An ideal simple pendulum slightly displaced from its mean position, and a plucked guitar string.

7. Name three types of waves that show oscillatory behaviour.

Ocean (pond) waves, sound waves and light (electromagnetic) waves.

Part 2 PHY-02

Terms Related to Oscillations

Section 3.2 — Use displacement, amplitude, period, frequency and angular frequency; express period in terms of f and ω

Key points
  • Displacement (x): distance of the vibrating body from the equilibrium position on either side at any instant. SI unit: metre (m).
  • Amplitude (x₀ or A): maximum displacement from the mean position. SI unit: metre (m).
  • Time period (T): time taken to complete one oscillation. SI unit: second (s).
  • Frequency (f): number of oscillations (n) per unit time (t). SI unit: hertz (Hz).
  • Angular frequency (ω): angular displacement per unit time.
\(f=\dfrac{n}{t}\)\(f=\dfrac{1}{T}\)
\(\omega=2\pi f=\dfrac{2\pi}{T}\)\(T=\dfrac{2\pi}{\omega}\)

One hertz is one oscillation per second (1 Hz = 1 s⁻¹). Angular frequency is measured in rad s⁻¹.

Questions & Answers — Terms Related to Oscillations

8. Define displacement. Give its SI unit.

The distance of the vibrating body from the equilibrium position on either side at any instant of time. Its SI unit is the metre (m).

9. Define amplitude.
• What is meant by amplitude of a vibrating body?

The maximum displacement of a vibrating body from its mean position is called amplitude. Its SI unit is the metre (m).

10. Define time period. Write its SI unit.

The time taken to complete one oscillation or vibration is called time period (T). Its SI unit is the second (s).

11. Define frequency.
• What is meant by frequency of oscillation?

The number of vibrations or oscillations (n) per unit time (t) is called frequency, f = n/t. Its SI unit is hertz (Hz).

12. Define hertz.

One hertz is one oscillation per second.

13. What is the relation between frequency and time period?
• Write the relation connecting f and T.

f = 1/T. Frequency is the reciprocal of time period.

14. Define angular frequency.
• What is meant by angular frequency of an oscillating body?

Angular displacement per unit time is called angular frequency (ω).

15. How is angular frequency related to frequency and time period?

ω = 2πf = 2π/T.

16. Express time period in terms of angular frequency.

T = 2π/ω.

17. Write the SI unit of angular frequency.

radian per second (rad s⁻¹).

18. A pendulum makes 30 vibrations in 15 s. Find its frequency and time period.

f = n/t = 30/15 = 2 Hz; T = 1/f = 0.5 s.

19. Differentiate between displacement and amplitude.

Displacement is the distance from the mean position at any instant (it changes with time); amplitude is the maximum displacement (it is the largest value of displacement).

Part 3 PHY-03

Simple Harmonic Motion

Section 3.3 — Explain that SHM occurs when acceleration is proportional to displacement from a fixed point and opposite in direction; use a = −ω²x

Simple harmonic motion is a type of motion in which a system oscillates back and forth about a mean (equilibrium) position and is characterised by a restoring force.

SHM: oscillatory motion in which the acceleration of a particle is directly proportional to its displacement from the mean position and is always directed towards the mean position.
\(a\propto -x\)
Key points
  • Mass-spring system: a mass m on a frictionless horizontal surface attached to a spring. At x = 0 the spring is neither stretched nor compressed (F = 0).
  • If the mass is displaced by x, the spring exerts a restoring force opposite to the displacement: F = −kx (Hooke's law). k = spring constant, SI unit N m⁻¹.
  • On release the mass moves towards the mean position, cannot stop there because of inertia, compresses the spring, comes to rest on the other side at the same distance, and is pushed back. Hence it oscillates.

Using Newton's second law F = ma together with F = −kx:

ma = −kx   ⇒   a = −(k/m) x
Since k/m = ω² is constant, a = −ω²x, i.e. a ∝ −x, so the mass-spring system executes SHM.
\(\omega=\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}}\)
\(T=2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}}\)
\(f=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}}\)
NoteThe time period of a mass-spring system depends on m and k only; it does not depend on the amplitude. The atoms of a solid behave as if joined by tiny springs whose spring constant depends on the bonding between the atoms.
Worked Example 1 — Mass on a spring scale

A fish is hung on a spring scale. (a) What is the spring constant if the spring stretches 8.0 cm for a 10.0 kg load? (b) What is the mass of the fish that stretches the spring 5.5 cm?

Solution

(a) x = 8.0 cm = 0.08 m, m = 10.0 kg.  F = W = mg = kx
k = mg/x = (10 × 9.8)/0.08 = 1225 N m⁻¹
(b) x = 5.5 cm = 0.055 m.  m = kx/g = (1225 × 0.055)/9.8 = 6.875 kg
Practice Problem 1

An object of mass 500 g is suspended from a spring which is stretched by 9.8 cm. Calculate the spring constant.

Show solution
m = 500 g = 0.5 kg, x = 9.8 cm = 0.098 m
k = mg/x = (0.5 × 9.8)/0.098 = 50 N m⁻¹

Questions & Answers — Simple Harmonic Motion

20. Define simple harmonic motion.
• What is SHM?

SHM is the oscillatory motion in which the acceleration of a particle is directly proportional to its displacement from the mean position and is always directed towards the mean position.

21. Write the mathematical condition for SHM.

a ∝ −x (equivalently a = −ω²x).

22. What does the negative sign in a ∝ −x indicate?

It shows that the acceleration is always directed opposite to the displacement, i.e. towards the mean position.

23. Give an example of SHM.

The motion of a mass attached to an elastic spring on a frictionless surface (also a simple pendulum with small swings).

24. State Hooke's law.
• What is the restoring force of a spring according to Hooke's law?

The restoring force exerted by a spring is directly proportional to the displacement from the mean position and opposite to it: F = −kx.

25. What is a restoring force?

The force that acts opposite to the displacement of a body and tries to bring it back to the mean position (e.g. the force of a stretched spring).

26. What does k represent in F = −kx? Write its SI unit.

k is the spring constant (stiffness of the spring). Its SI unit is N m⁻¹.

27. Why does the mass of a mass-spring system not stop at the mean position?

Because of inertia, the moving mass cannot stop at the mean position and goes ahead.

28. Why does an oscillating mass on a spring accelerate?

Its direction of motion changes continuously, so its velocity keeps changing; hence it accelerates.

29. What is the restoring force at the mean position?

Zero, because the spring is neither stretched nor compressed there (x = 0).

30. Write the expression for the acceleration of a mass-spring system.

a = −(k/m) x.

31. Write the expression for the angular frequency of a mass-spring system.

ω = √(k/m).

32. Write the formula for the time period of a mass-spring system.
• How does the time period of a mass attached to a spring depend on mass and spring constant?

T = 2π√(m/k). T increases with m and decreases with k; it is independent of amplitude.

33. Write the formula for the frequency of a mass-spring system.

f = (1/2π)√(k/m).

34. How do atoms in a solid behave like springs?

Atoms oscillate as if joined to neighbours by tiny springs; the spring constant depends on the type of bonding between the atoms.

Part 4 PHY-04

Uniform Circular Motion and SHM

Section 3.4 — Use x = x₀ cos ωt and v = ±ω√(x₀² − x²) to solve problems

Consider a point P moving on a circle of radius x₀ with constant angular velocity ω. Let Q be the projection of P on the x-axis. As P goes round, Q oscillates along the diameter between −x₀ and +x₀. One revolution of P takes the same time as one oscillation of Q, so the equations for displacement, velocity and acceleration of P also hold for Q.

Key points
  • Displacement: at time t the angle is θ = ωt. From triangle OPQ, cos θ = x/x₀.
\(x=x_0\cos\theta=x_0\cos\omega t\)
Key points
  • Velocity: v_P = x₀ω along the tangent. The velocity of Q is its horizontal component, v = v_P sin θ.
v = x₀ω √(1 − cos²θ)   ⇒   v = ω√(x₀² − x²)
\(v=\omega\sqrt{x_0^{2}-x^{2}}\)
v is maximum (= ±x₀ω) at the mean position and zero at the extreme positions.
Key points
  • Acceleration: P has centripetal acceleration a_P = x₀ω² towards O. The acceleration of Q is its horizontal component: a = −a_P cos θ = −x₀ω² cos θ.
\(a=-\omega^{2}x\)
a is maximum (= x₀ω²) at the extreme positions and zero at the mean position.

Since a = −ω²x, the motion of Q is SHM. When a body moves in a circle, its projection undergoes simple harmonic motion on the diameter of the circle.

Worked Example 2 — Angular frequency, velocity and acceleration

A body of mass 800 g attached to a spring vibrates with amplitude 30 cm. The restoring force is 60 N when the displacement is 0.30 m. (a) Find its angular frequency. (b) Calculate the magnitude of its velocity and acceleration at x = 12 cm.

Solution

m = 0.8 kg, x₀ = 0.30 m, F = 60 N, x = 0.12 m
(a) k = F/x₀ = 60/0.30 = 200 N m⁻¹
ω = √(k/m) = √(200/0.8) = √250 = 15.81 rad s⁻¹
(b) v = ω√(x₀² − x²) = 15.81 × √(0.3² − 0.12²) = 15.81 × 0.275 = 4.35 m s⁻¹
a = ω²x = (15.81)² × 0.12 = 250 × 0.12 = 30 m s⁻²
Practice Problem 2

The time period of a mass attached at the end of a spring is 0.40 s. Find the magnitude of the acceleration when the displacement is 4 cm.

Show solution
ω = 2π/T = 2π/0.40 = 15.7 rad s⁻¹
a = ω²x = (15.7)² × 0.04 = 9.87 ≈ 9.9 m s⁻²

Questions & Answers — Uniform Circular Motion and SHM

35. How is simple harmonic motion related to uniform circular motion?

When a body moves in a circle with uniform speed, its projection on a diameter of the circle executes SHM.

36. Why can the equations of P (circular motion) be used for Q (SHM)?

The time period of one revolution of P equals the time period of one oscillation of Q, so the angular speed of P is the same as the linear speed of Q along the diameter.

37. Write the expression for the instantaneous displacement of a body executing SHM.

x = x₀ cos ωt, where x₀ is the amplitude and ω the angular frequency.

38. What is the maximum value of displacement in SHM?

The amplitude x₀ (displacement varies between −x₀ and +x₀).

39. Write the expression for velocity of a body executing SHM in terms of displacement.

v = ω√(x₀² − x²).

40. Write the expression for velocity in terms of time in SHM.

v = x₀ω sin ωt.

41. At which position is the velocity of a particle in SHM maximum and what is its value?

At the mean position (x = 0); v_max = ±x₀ω.

42. What is the velocity of a particle in SHM at the extreme positions?

Zero, since x = ±x₀ gives v = ω√(x₀² − x₀²) = 0.

43. What is the acceleration of point P moving on the circle and what is its direction?

a_P = x₀ω² (centripetal), directed inward towards the centre O.

44. Write the expression for the acceleration of a body executing SHM.

a = −ω²x (or a = −x₀ω² cos ωt).

45. Where is the acceleration maximum and where is it zero in SHM?

Maximum (x₀ω²) at the extreme positions; zero at the mean position.

46. Why is there a negative sign in a = −ω²x?

Because the acceleration is directed towards the mean position, opposite to the displacement.

47. What conclusion is drawn about the projection of a body moving in a circle?

The projection of a body moving in a circle undergoes simple harmonic motion on the diameter of the circle.

48. Which equation describes SHM? Why?

a = −ω²x, because the acceleration is proportional to the displacement and opposite to it.

Part 5 PHY-05

Phase

Section 3.5 — Use the terms phase and phase difference; interpret x = x₀ cos(ωt + φ)

The displacement of a body in SHM is x = x₀ cos θ = x₀ cos ωt. Here θ = ωt is the phase of the motion.

Phase: the angle θ = ωt which specifies the displacement as well as the direction of a point executing SHM.
General form: x = x₀ cos(ωt + φ), φ = initial phase
Key points
  • φ = 0°: x = x₀ cos ωt. At t = 0, T/2, T the point is at an extreme position; at T/4 and 3T/4 it is at the mean position.
  • φ = 90°: x = x₀ cos(ωt + 90°). At t = 0, T/2, T the point is at the mean position; at T/4 and 3T/4 it is at an extreme position. This curve leads the φ = 0° curve by 90°.
  • φ = 180°: x = x₀ cos(ωt + 180°). The curve leads the φ = 0° curve by 180°.

Phase difference 180°: the two systems oscillate out of phase. Phase difference 0° or 360°: they oscillate in phase.

Questions & Answers — Phase

49. Define phase of motion.
• What is meant by phase in SHM?

The angle θ = ωt which specifies the displacement as well as the direction of a point executing SHM is called the phase of the motion.

50. What is initial phase?

The term φ in x = x₀ cos(ωt + φ); it gives the starting (initial) phase of the oscillation at t = 0.

51. Write the general equation for displacement in SHM.

x = x₀ cos(ωt + φ).

52. When are two oscillating systems said to be in phase?

When the phase difference between them is 0° or 360°.

53. When are two oscillating systems said to be out of phase?

When the phase difference between them is 180°.

54. For φ = 0°, where is the oscillating point at t = 0?

x = x₀ cos 0 = x₀, i.e. at the extreme position.

55. For φ = 90°, where is the oscillating point at t = 0 and at t = T/4?

At t = 0 it is at the mean position (x = 0); at t = T/4 it is at an extreme position.

56. For φ = 0°, where is the point at t = T/4 and 3T/4?

At the mean position (θ = π/2 and 3π/2).

57. By what angle does the curve of φ = 90° lead the curve of φ = 0°?

By 90°, because their phase difference is 90°.

58. What is the phase difference between velocity and displacement in SHM?

π/2 (90°).

59. What is the phase difference between acceleration and displacement in SHM?

π (180°).

Part 6 PHY-06

Graphical Representations of Displacement, Velocity and Acceleration

Section 3.6 — Analyse graphs of displacement, velocity and acceleration for SHM

(i) Displacement:  x = x₀ cos ωt   (maximum value x₀)
(ii) Velocity:  v = x₀ω sin ωt   (maximum ±x₀ω at mean position, zero at extremes)
(iii) Acceleration:  a = −ω²x = −x₀ω² cos ωt   (maximum x₀ω² at extremes, zero at mean position)
Key points
  • Phase difference between velocity and displacement = π/2.
  • Phase difference between acceleration and displacement = π (acceleration leads velocity by π/2).

Questions & Answers — Graphical Representations of Displacement, Velocity and Acceleration

60. What is the shape of the displacement-time graph of SHM?

A cosine (sinusoidal) curve, x = x₀ cos ωt, oscillating between +x₀ and −x₀.

61. What are the maximum values of displacement, velocity and acceleration in SHM?

x₀, ωx₀ and ω²x₀ respectively.

62. What is the maximum value of velocity and where does it occur?

±x₀ω at the mean position.

63. When is the acceleration of a particle in SHM maximum?

At the extreme positions, where a = x₀ω² (directed towards the mean position).

64. If the velocity is maximum, what is the acceleration?

Zero, because velocity is maximum at the mean position where displacement and acceleration are zero.

65. What is the phase difference between acceleration and velocity in SHM?

π/2: acceleration leads velocity by π/2 (90°).

66. What does the graph show about the displacement and acceleration curves?

They are exactly opposite (phase difference π): when x is maximum positive, a is maximum negative.

Part 7 PHY-07

Simple Pendulum

Section 3.7 — Show that a simple pendulum executes SHM and use T = 2π√(l/g)

An ideal simple pendulum is a small but heavy bob of mass m suspended by a light, inextensible string of length l, whose other end is attached to a fixed frictionless support P.

Key points
  • When the bob is displaced slightly, two forces act: tension T of the string and the weight mg downward.
  • Weight components: mg cos θ along the string (cancelled by tension) and mg sin θ along the tangent to the arc.
  • The net force mg sin θ is the restoring force: F = −mg sin θ.
  • For small angles (less than 10° or 0.2 rad), sin θ ≈ θ. So F = −mgθ and, since θ = x/l, F = −mg(x/l).

Comparing with F = ma:

ma = −mg(x/l)   ⇒   a = −(g/l) x
a = −ω²x with ω = √(g/l), hence the pendulum executes SHM.
\(T=2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{l}{g}}\)
T depends on the length l and on g; it is independent of the mass of the bob.
NoteTable: for θ = 10° the value is θ = 0.1746 rad and sin θ = 0.1737 – almost equal, which justifies sin θ ≈ θ for small angles.
Worked Example 3 — Length of a seconds-type pendulum

A simple pendulum completes one vibration in one second. Calculate its length when g = 9.8 m s⁻².

Solution

T = 1 s, g = 9.8 m s⁻²
T = 2π√(l/g)   ⇒   1 = 2 × 3.14 × √(l/9.8)
l = gT²/(4π²) = 9.8/(4 × 3.14²) = 0.248 m = 24.8 cm
Practice Problem 3

Find the time periods of a simple pendulum of length 1 m placed on Earth and on the Moon. g on the Moon is 1/6th of its value on Earth.

Show solution
Earth: T = 2π√(1/9.8) = 2.01 s
Moon: g = 9.8/6 = 1.63 m s⁻²,  T = 2π√(1/1.63) = 4.92 s  (= √6 × 2.01 s)

Questions & Answers — Simple Pendulum

67. What is a simple pendulum?

A small heavy bob of mass m suspended by a light, inextensible string of length l from a fixed frictionless support.

68. Name the forces acting on the bob of a simple pendulum.

The tension T in the string and the weight mg acting vertically downward.

69. Into which components is the weight of the bob resolved?

mg cos θ along the string and mg sin θ along the tangent to the arc.

70. Which force provides the restoring force in a simple pendulum?

The tangential component of the weight, mg sin θ, directed towards the mean position (F = −mg sin θ).

71. Why does tension not take part in the oscillation?

The tension in the string exactly cancels the component mg cos θ along the string.

72. What approximation is made for small angles in a simple pendulum?

sin θ ≈ θ (in radians) for θ less than 10° (0.2 rad), so F = −mgθ.

73. Show that a simple pendulum executes SHM.

F = −mg(x/l) = ma gives a = −(g/l)x, i.e. a ∝ −x, which is the condition for SHM.

74. Write the formula for the angular frequency of a simple pendulum.

ω = √(g/l).

75. Write the expression for the time period of a simple pendulum.
• On what factors does the time period of a simple pendulum depend?

T = 2π√(l/g). It depends on the length l and acceleration due to gravity g, and is independent of the mass of the bob.

76. What happens to the time period of a pendulum when it is taken to the Moon where g is 1/6 of that on Earth?

T increases by a factor √6 ≈ 2.45 because T ∝ 1/√g.

Part 8 PHY-08

Energy Conservation in SHM

Section 3.8 — Analyse the interchange of kinetic and potential energy; apply total energy = ½mω²x₀²

The energy of a body executing SHM remains conserved. Consider a mass-spring system.

Key points
  • Potential energy: stretching the spring by x needs work W = F_av x. Force rises uniformly from 0 to kx, so F_av = ½kx, giving W = ½kx². This is stored as elastic potential energy.
\(\text{P.E}=\tfrac{1}{2}kx^{2}\)
Key points
  • At the extreme position (x = x₀) the block is at rest: K.E = 0 and total energy is entirely potential: T.E = P.E_max = ½kx₀².
  • At displacement x: K.E = ½mv² = ½k(x₀² − x²) (using v = ω√(x₀² − x²) and ω² = k/m).
  • At the mean position (x = 0) the P.E is zero and the speed is maximum: v₀ = √(k/m) x₀, so T.E = K.E_max = ½mv_max² = ½kx₀².
\(\text{K.E}=\tfrac{1}{2}k\left(x_0^{2}-x^{2}\right)\)
\(\text{T.E}=\text{K.E}+\text{P.E}=\tfrac{1}{2}k\left(x_0^{2}-x^{2}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}kx^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}kx_0^{2}\)
Since k = mω²:\(\text{T.E}=\tfrac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}x_0^{2}\)

The total energy of the mass-spring system is constant and proportional to the square of the amplitude. Energy oscillates between K.E and P.E but their sum stays constant. This is valid for all bodies executing SHM.

Worked Example 4 — Energy of a mass-spring system

A 0.025 kg mass attached to a spring is displaced 0.10 m to the right of its mean position and released. The time period is 1.57 s. Calculate (a) angular speed, (b) total energy, (c) maximum acceleration.

Solution

m = 0.025 kg, x₀ = 0.10 m, T = 1.57 s
(a) ω = 2π/T = 2(3.14)/1.57 = 4 rad s⁻¹
(b) T.E = ½mω²x₀² = ½(0.025)(4)²(0.1)² = 2 × 10⁻³ J
(c) a_max = x₀ω² = 0.1 × 4² = 1.6 m s⁻²
Practice Problem 4

Find the amplitude, frequency and time period of an object oscillating at the end of a spring if its position is x = 0.25 cos(π/8)t. Also find the displacement after 2.0 s.

Show solution
Compare with x = x₀ cos ωt:  x₀ = 0.25 m,  ω = π/8 rad s⁻¹
f = ω/2π = 1/16 = 0.0625 Hz,   T = 1/f = 16 s
x(2.0 s) = 0.25 cos(π/8 × 2) = 0.25 cos(π/4) = 0.177 m ≈ 0.18 m

Questions & Answers — Energy Conservation in SHM

77. Is the energy of a body executing SHM conserved?

Yes. The energy oscillates between K.E and P.E but their sum, the total energy, remains constant.

78. Write the expression for the potential energy of a mass-spring system at displacement x.

P.E = ½kx².

79. Why is the average force ½kx while a spring is stretched through x?

The applied force increases uniformly from 0 to kx, so F_av = (0 + kx)/2 = ½kx; work done W = F_av × x = ½kx².

80. What is the kinetic energy of the block at the extreme position?

Zero, because the block is momentarily at rest there.

81. What is the potential energy at the mean position?

Zero, because x = 0.

82. At which position is the total energy entirely potential and at which entirely kinetic?

Entirely potential at the extreme positions (x = ±x₀); entirely kinetic at the mean position (x = 0).

83. Write the expression for kinetic energy at displacement x in SHM.

K.E = ½mv² = ½k(x₀² − x²).

84. Write the expression for the maximum speed of a mass-spring system.

v₀ = √(k/m) x₀ (= ωx₀), attained at the mean position.

It is proportional to the square of the amplitude (and to k); it does not change with time.

T.E = ½kx₀² and k = mω², therefore T.E = ½mω²x₀².

P.E is an upward parabola and K.E an inverted parabola (maximum at x = 0); T.E is a horizontal straight line at ½kx₀².

Part 9 PHY-09 PHY-10

Free, Forced and Damped Oscillations

Section 3.9 — Describe practical examples of free and forced oscillations; Describe damping; use the terms light, critical and heavy damping; sketch their displacement-time graphs; justify critical damping in a car suspension

Key points
  • Free oscillations: a body oscillates under a restoring force without any external force acting on it. Every oscillator has a natural frequency (e.g. an ideal pendulum depends on its length; a guitar string on its length). Free oscillations have constant amplitude and period; ideally they do not undergo damping.
  • Forced oscillations: an external force acts on the oscillator at a driven frequency, not necessarily its natural frequency. Examples: pushing a swing periodically; a clock pendulum driven by a small motor.
  • Damping: effect of resistive forces (friction, air resistance, internal forces in springs) which dissipate energy (as heat) from a vibrating object. Amplitude decreases from one oscillation to the next and the motion eventually stops – damped oscillations.
  • Light damping: amplitude decreases gradually with time (e.g. a playground swing coming to rest).
  • Heavy damping: a very large resistive force; the oscillator returns slowly to the equilibrium position without completing a single oscillation (e.g. a mass-spring in thick, viscous liquid).
  • Critical damping: the oscillator returns to equilibrium in the shortest time without oscillating (e.g. shock absorbers in a car).

A car passing over a bump would otherwise bounce violently. Shock absorbers (fluid-filled tubes) are fitted parallel to the springs between the wheels and the frame; they turn the simple harmonic motion of the spring into damped motion by absorbing the spring's energy within a single oscillation, giving a smooth, comfortable ride.

A body is said to execute free oscillations when it oscillates under the influence of a restoring force without any external force acting on it.

Part 10 PHY-11

Resonance

Section 3.10 — State that resonance is maximum amplitude when a system is forced at its natural frequency; justify the effect of damping on the sharpness of resonance

A driving force is required to start the oscillations of any oscillator by supplying initial energy. When its frequency is raised from zero the oscillator vibrates with small amplitude, and the amplitude grows as the driving frequency approaches the natural frequency.

Resonance: the phenomenon in which the amplitude of vibration of an oscillator attains its maximum value when the driving frequency becomes equal to the natural frequency of the oscillator.
The natural frequency is called the resonance frequency.
Key points
  • At resonance the efficiency of energy transfer from the driving force to the oscillator is maximum.
  • Demonstration: five pendulums A, B, C, D, E hang from one horizontal rod; A and B have equal length l, C and D equal length L. If E is made the same length as A and B and set vibrating, A and B start vibrating (C and D stay at rest). If E is made equal to C and D, then C and D vibrate while A and B stay at rest.
  • Effect of damping: damping reduces the maximum amplitude at resonance and broadens the resonance curve. Small damping gives a sharper peak and larger amplitude; heavy damping gives a lower, broader peak.

Resonance is the phenomenon in which the amplitude of vibration of an oscillator attains its maximum value when the driving frequency becomes equal to the natural frequency of the oscillator.

The natural frequency of the oscillator at which resonance occurs.

It supplies the initial energy needed for the motion.

The amplitude increases; the closer the driving frequency is to the natural frequency, the greater the energy transfer and the amplitude.

Maximum; the driving force transfers energy to the oscillator most efficiently at the resonance frequency.

They have the same length, hence the same natural frequency as E, so resonance occurs.

Their lengths (and natural frequencies) differ from that of E, so there is no resonance.

Damping reduces the maximum amplitude at resonance frequency and broadens the resonance curve.

Smaller damping gives a sharper resonance peak with larger amplitude; heavier damping gives a lower and broader peak.

Amplitude is maximum at the natural frequency f₀; the peak is tall and sharp for small damping and low and broad for heavy damping.

To minimise oscillations and ensure a smooth ride over bumps and jumps.

Part 11 PHY-12

Applications of Resonance and Standing Waves

Section 3.11 — Justify circumstances where resonance is useful or must be avoided; identify standing waves and resonance in Rubens tubes, Chladni plates and acoustic levitation

Key points
  • Resonance is useful in: tuning of a radio (electrical resonance), Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), cooking in a microwave oven and amplifying sound in a guitar.
  • Radio: turning the knob changes the natural frequency of the receiver circuit until it equals the station's transmission frequency; resonance gives maximum energy absorption from that station only.
  • MRI: hydrogen nuclei are made to oscillate by strong radio waves (about 100 MHz); at resonance maximum energy is absorbed and the pattern of absorption produces computer-enhanced images.
  • Microwave oven: microwaves of frequency close to the natural frequency of water or fat molecules make them oscillate and absorb maximum energy, so the food heats up; plastic or glass containers have no water molecules, so they do not heat.
  • Guitar: vibration of the strings is transmitted to the hollow wooden box, producing resonance and amplifying the sound.
  • Resonance must be avoided in: bridges (soldiers break step; Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed in 1940 when strong wind drove deck oscillations to large amplitude) and airplane wings (wing frequency must not match gust frequencies).
NoteA singer can shatter a glass by loudly singing a note matching its natural frequency: the glass vibrates with large amplitude and breaks.
Key points
  • Rubens tube: a metal pipe with holes drilled along the top, sealed at both ends. One end is attached to a speaker or frequency generator, the other to a supply of flammable gas. Gas leaks from the perforations and burns as flames. A standing sound wave inside creates high- and low-pressure points: flames are high where pressure is high and low where pressure is low. It was invented by Heinrich Rubens in 1905.
  • Acoustic levitation: suspending matter in air against gravity using acoustic pressure of high-intensity sound waves; a standing wave with nodes traps the particle, giving a container-less environment for experiments.
  • Chladni plate: a flat metal sheet on a central stalk. When it vibrates in a mode, nodes and antinodes form symmetrical patterns; sprinkled sand moves away from antinodes and collects at the nodes.
NoteTwo successive high flames in a Rubens tube are half a wavelength (λ/2) apart; the textbook states the wavelength can be found with a ruler by measuring the distance between the low and high flames.

2. If the amplitude of vibration of a body executing SHM is doubled, what happens to the maximum kinetic energy?
Answer: Maximum K.E = total energy = ½kx₀² ∝ x₀². Doubling the amplitude makes the maximum kinetic energy four times.

Answer: The rhythmic marching can have a frequency equal to the natural frequency of the bridge. Resonance would then increase the amplitude of vibration and could collapse the bridge, so soldiers break step.

Answer: No in both cases. Resonance occurs only when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency of the oscillator; at ½f and 2f the energy transfer is not maximum, so the amplitude stays small.
5. A pendulum clock is taken to a city where g is slightly greater. Will you lengthen or shorten the pendulum to keep correct time? Explain.
Answer: T = 2π√(l/g). Greater g makes T smaller (the clock runs fast). To keep T the same, l must increase in proportion to g, so the pendulum must be lengthened.
6. Two mass-spring systems have equal spring constants and the mass of one is twice the other. Which has the greater period?
Answer: T = 2π√(m/k). With equal k, the system with double mass has the greater period, √2 times larger.
7. Give some applications in which resonance plays an important role.
Answer: Tuning of a radio, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), cooking in a microwave oven and amplification of sound in a guitar (also Rubens tubes, Chladni plates, acoustic levitation).
8. A simple pendulum set into vibration and left untouched eventually stops. Why?
Answer: Air resistance (and friction at the support) acts as a damping force; the pendulum does work against it, so its energy and amplitude decrease until it stops.

Answer: The angular displacement must be small (less than about 10° or 0.2 rad) so that sin θ ≈ θ, giving a = −(g/l)x; the string must be light and inextensible, the support frictionless and damping negligible.

14. Differentiate free and forced oscillations.
Answer: Free oscillations: the body oscillates under its restoring force alone at its natural frequency, with constant amplitude (ideal case), e.g. an ideal pendulum. Solved Exercise — Comprehensive Questions

1. Define and explain phase in simple harmonic motion.

Definition: the angle θ = ωt which specifies the displacement as well as the direction of a point executing SHM is called the phase of the motion.

Explanation: with x = x₀ cos ωt, a given θ fixes both the position and direction of motion. In general x = x₀ cos(ωt + φ), where φ is the initial phase (state of motion at t = 0).

φ = 0°: point starts at the extreme position. φ = 90°: starts at the mean position; its curve leads the φ = 0° curve by 90°. φ = 180°: starts at the opposite extreme; the curve leads by 180°.

Phase difference 180° means the systems oscillate out of phase; 0° or 360° means in phase.

2. Derive expressions for instantaneous displacement, velocity and acceleration of the projection of a particle moving in a circle.

Let P move on a circle of radius x₀ at constant angular velocity ω; Q is its projection on the x-axis. At time t, θ = ωt.

Displacement: in triangle OPQ, cos θ = x/x₀ ⇒ x = x₀ cos ωt.

Velocity: v_P = x₀ω along the tangent. Q's velocity is its horizontal component: v = v_P sin θ = x₀ω √(1 − cos²θ) = ω√(x₀² − x²).

Acceleration: a_P = x₀ω² towards O (centripetal). Q's acceleration is its horizontal component: a = −a_P cos θ = −x₀ω² cos θ = −ω²x (negative sign: directed towards O).

3. Show that the motion of a mass attached to a spring executes SHM.

Restoring force of the spring (Hooke's law): F = −kx. Newton's second law: F = ma.

So ma = −kx ⇒ a = −(k/m)x. Since k/m = ω² is constant, a = −ω²x ∝ −x: acceleration is proportional to displacement and directed towards the mean position, which is the definition of SHM. Here ω = √(k/m) and T = 2π√(m/k).

4. Analyse the interchange between kinetic and potential energy during SHM.

At the extreme position x = x₀: v = 0, so K.E = 0 and P.E = ½kx₀² = T.E.

As the block moves towards the mean position its displacement decreases (P.E falls) and its speed increases (K.E rises): K.E = ½k(x₀² − x²), P.E = ½kx².

At the mean position x = 0: P.E = 0 and K.E = ½mv_max² = ½kx₀² = T.E.

At every instant K.E + P.E = ½kx₀² = ½mω²x₀², constant; the energy only oscillates between K.E and P.E.

5. What is resonance? Give three of its applications in our daily life.

Resonance is the phenomenon in which the amplitude of vibration of an oscillator attains its maximum value when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency of the oscillator.

Applications: (1) tuning a radio – the circuit's natural frequency is matched to the station frequency; (2) microwave oven – microwaves match the natural frequency of water molecules so food heats; (3) MRI – hydrogen nuclei absorb maximum energy from radio waves of resonant frequency (also guitar sound amplification).

6. Justify some circumstances in which resonance should be avoided.

Bridges: rhythmic marching or strong wind can drive the bridge at its natural frequency; the amplitude grows until it collapses (Tacoma Narrows Bridge, 1940). Soldiers therefore break step.

Airplane wings: if wind gusts have a frequency equal to the natural structural frequency of the wing, the amplified vibrations eventually destroy it; wings are designed so their natural frequency does not match external frequencies.

7. Derive equations for kinetic and potential energy of a body of mass m executing SHM.

Potential energy: stretching the spring through x needs work W = F_av x. F rises from 0 to kx, so F_av = ½kx and W = ½kx². This is stored as P.E: P.E = ½kx².

Kinetic energy: v = ω√(x₀² − x²) and ω² = k/m, so K.E = ½mv² = ½m(k/m)(x₀² − x²) = ½k(x₀² − x²).

Total energy: T.E = K.E + P.E = ½k(x₀² − x²) + ½kx² = ½kx₀² = ½mω²x₀² (k = mω²).

8. Explain what is meant by damped oscillations.

Resistive forces such as friction and air resistance act on every oscillating system. The oscillator does work against them, so its energy is dissipated (as heat) and the amplitude decreases from one oscillation to the next until it stops. Oscillations whose amplitude decreases under damping forces are damped oscillations; the pendulum that finally stops because of air resistance is an example.

9. Explain the relation between damping and sharpness of resonance.

In the graph of amplitude against driving frequency, the amplitude is maximum at the natural frequency f₀. Small damping gives a tall, sharp peak (large amplitude, narrow range of frequencies). As damping increases, the maximum amplitude decreases and the curve broadens. Heavy damping gives a low, broad, flat peak.

10. Discuss the use of standing waves and resonance in (a) Rubens tubes (b) Chladni plates (c) acoustic levitation.

(a) Rubens tube: a perforated metal pipe filled with flammable gas and driven by a speaker/frequency generator. A standing wave forms at resonance; flames are high at pressure-high points and low at pressure-low points, so the standing wave can be seen and its wavelength measured with a ruler.

(b) Chladni plates: a vibrating metal plate forms nodes and antinodes at resonance; sand sprinkled on it leaves the antinodes and gathers at the nodes, showing complicated modes of vibration.

(c) Acoustic levitation: high-intensity sound waves form a standing wave whose nodes can trap a particle, suspending matter in air against gravity; it provides a container-less environment for experiments.

11. Analyse graphical representations of the variations of displacement, velocity and acceleration for SHM.

x = x₀ cos ωt varies between +x₀ and −x₀. v = x₀ω sin ωt varies between +ωx₀ and −ωx₀; it is maximum at the mean position and zero at extremes. a = −ω²x₀ cos ωt varies between ±ω²x₀; it is maximum at extremes and zero at the mean position.

Phase difference between velocity and displacement is π/2; between acceleration and displacement is π. All three graphs are sinusoidal with the same time period T.

12. Explain the terms light, critical and heavy damping with the help of examples.

Light damping: amplitude decreases gradually and the object oscillates several times before stopping – e.g. a playground swing.

Critical damping: the object returns to the equilibrium position in the shortest time without oscillating – e.g. shock absorbers in a car.

Heavy damping: a very large resistive force; the oscillator moves slowly back to equilibrium without completing one oscillation – e.g. a mass-spring system in a thick viscous liquid.

Solved Exercise — Numerical Problems

1. The amplitude of a mass attached to a spring is 2.48 m, while the maximum speed of its mass is 4.36 m s⁻¹. What is the period of the motion?

Solution

x₀ = 2.48 m, v_max = 4.36 m s⁻¹
v_max = ωx₀ ⇒ ω = 4.36/2.48 = 1.758 rad s⁻¹
T = 2π/ω = 2π/1.758 = 3.57 s
Answer: 3.57 s
2. A particle moves with displacement x = 3.0 cos(2t) (metre, second). (a) Find the amplitude, frequency, angular frequency and period. (b) Find the time at which the particle reaches the midpoint (x = 0) and the turning point.

Solution

(a) Compare with x = x₀ cos ωt: x₀ = 3.0 m, ω = 2.0 rad s⁻¹
f = ω/2π = 2/(2π) = 0.318 Hz,   T = 1/f = 3.14 s
(b) x = 0: cos 2t = 0 ⇒ 2t = π/2 ⇒ t = 0.785 s
Turning point (x = −3.0 m): cos 2t = −1 ⇒ 2t = π ⇒ t = 1.57 s
Answer: (a) 3.0 m, 0.318 Hz, 2.0 rad s⁻¹, 3.14 s (b) 0.785 s, 1.57 s
3. A particle executes SHM between x = −0.20 m and +0.20 m with period 1.2 s. At t = 0 it is at +0.20 m with zero velocity. (a) Frequency and angular frequency? (b) Amplitude? (c) When does it reach x = 0? x = 0.10 m? (d) Speed at x = 0? at x = 0.10 m?

Solution

(a) f = 1/T = 1/1.2 = 0.83 Hz;   ω = 2πf = 2π/1.2 = 5.2 rad s⁻¹
(b) x₀ = 0.20 m
(c) x = x₀ cos ωt.  x = 0: ωt = π/2 ⇒ t = T/4 = 0.30 s
     x = 0.10: cos ωt = 0.5 ⇒ ωt = π/3 ⇒ t = T/6 = 0.20 s
(d) v = ω√(x₀² − x²).  At x = 0: v = 5.24 × 0.20 = 1.05 m s⁻¹
     At x = 0.10: v = 5.24 × √(0.04 − 0.01) = 5.24 × 0.173 = 0.91 m s⁻¹
Answer: (a) 0.83 Hz, 5.2 rad s⁻¹ (b) 0.20 m (c) 0.30 s, 0.20 s (d) 1.05 m s⁻¹, 0.91 m s⁻¹
4. A mass of 8.0 kg attached to a spring oscillates with amplitude 0.25 m and frequency 0.60 Hz. What is the energy of the motion?

Solution

ω = 2πf = 2π(0.60) = 3.77 rad s⁻¹
T.E = ½mω²x₀² = ½(8.0)(3.77)²(0.25)² = ½(8.0)(14.2)(0.0625) = 3.55 ≈ 3.6 J
Answer: 3.6 J
5. Calculate the period and frequency of a 3.5 m long pendulum (a) at Karachi, g = 9.832 m s⁻²; (b) at K-2, g = 9.782 m s⁻².

Solution

(a) T = 2π√(l/g) = 2(3.14)√(3.5/9.832) = 6.28 × 0.5967 = 3.747 s;   f = 1/T = 0.2669 Hz
(b) T = 6.28 × √(3.5/9.782) = 6.28 × 0.5982 = 3.757 s;   f = 1/T = 0.2662 Hz
Answer: (a) 3.747 s, 0.2669 Hz (b) 3.757 s, 0.2662 Hz
6. A piston in a car engine executes SHM with amplitude 0.41 m. The engine runs at angular frequency 2400 rpm (251 rad s⁻¹). What is the maximum speed of the piston?

Solution

v_max = ωx₀ = 251 × 0.41 = 102.9 ≈ 103 m s⁻¹
Answer: 103 m s⁻¹
7. A ball connected to a spring executes SHM. At t = 0 its displacement is 0.50 m and its acceleration is −0.72 m s⁻². The phase constant is 0.84 rad. What is the ball's displacement at t = 3.4 s?

Solution

x = x₀ cos(ωt + φ),  φ = 0.84 rad
At t = 0: x₀ cos 0.84 = 0.50 ⇒ x₀ = 0.50/0.668 = 0.749 m
a = −ω²x ⇒ ω² = 0.72/0.50 = 1.44 ⇒ ω = 1.2 rad s⁻¹
At t = 3.4 s: x = 0.749 cos(1.2 × 3.4 + 0.84) = 0.749 cos(4.92) = 0.749 × 0.206 = 0.154 ≈ 0.15 m
Answer: 0.15 m
Key Vocabulary

Chapter 3 Vocabulary

Important terms you must know: learn these definitions for exam questions.

TermDefinition
OscillationOne complete round trip of a body about its mean position.
DisplacementThe distance of a vibrating body from the equilibrium position at any instant.
AmplitudeThe maximum displacement of a vibrating body from its mean position.
Time periodThe time taken to complete one oscillation.
FrequencyThe number of oscillations completed per unit time; its SI unit is the hertz.
Angular frequencyThe angular displacement per unit time, ω = 2πf, in rad s⁻¹.
Simple harmonic motionOscillatory motion in which the acceleration is proportional to the displacement and directed towards the mean position.
Restoring forceThe force that always acts towards the equilibrium position of an oscillating body.
Hooke's lawThe restoring force of a spring is proportional to its displacement: F = −kx.
PhaseThe angle ωt that specifies the displacement and direction of motion of a point in SHM.
Free oscillationOscillation under a restoring force with no external force acting on the body.
Forced oscillationOscillation of a body driven by an external periodic force at a driven frequency.
DampingThe effect of resistive forces that remove energy from a vibrating object, so its amplitude decreases.
ResonanceThe condition in which the amplitude of an oscillator is maximum because the driving frequency equals its natural frequency.
