19.1

Electric Potential Energy and Electric Potential

PHY-01

Electric potential energy

Electricity is a form of energy that powers our devices. Electric circuits usually use electric energy and transfer it to other forms such as heat, light or motion. The stored energy of an electric circuit is called electric potential energy. Batteries are typically a few volts, the outlets in your home produce 220 volts, and power lines can be as high as hundreds of thousands of volts. Energy and voltage are two different quantities: a small motorcycle battery and a much larger car battery have the same voltage, yet they store different amounts of energy.

Consider a point charge q0 placed between two oppositely charged parallel plates (Fig. 19.1: the upper plate is positive, the lower plate negative, and the field E points from positive to negative plate). The force on the charge is:

F=q0E

If the charge is free to move in the field, it moves from the positive plate to the negative plate and gains kinetic energy. If the charge is moved against the field, energy is required.

Electric potential energy is the energy needed to move a charge against an electric field.

More energy is needed to move a charge further in the field, and more energy is needed to move it through a stronger field. So electric potential energy depends on two factors: (i) its own electric charge and (ii) its relative position with respect to other electrically charged objects.

The change in potential energy ΔU of charge q0 is equal to the work done by the force in carrying the charge from point B to point A against the electric field:

ΔU=WBA
UA−UB=WBA(19.1)

Here UA and UB are the potential energies at points A and B, and WBA is the work done in carrying a positive charge q0 from B to A without disturbing its equilibrium. The unit of electric potential energy is the joule (J).

Electric potential

Electric potential expresses the effect of the electric field of a source in terms of the location within the field. A test charge with twice the quantity of charge would possess twice the potential energy at a given location, yet the electric potential at that location is the same for every test charge. A positive test charge is at a high electric potential when held close to a positive source charge and at a lower electric potential when held further away. Hence electric potential is purely a property of the location in the field.

Electric potential = potential energy per unit charge
V=Uq

Example: if the potential at a location is 12 J/C, a 1 C or 2 C object is at the same potential; a 2 C object has 24 J of potential energy there and a 0.5 C object has 6 J.

Potential difference and the volt

Potential difference is the work done in moving a unit positive charge from one point to another, keeping the charge in electrostatic equilibrium.

If VA and VB are the potentials at points A and B, the potential difference is:

ΔV=VA−VB=WABq0=ΔUq0(19.2)
ΔU=q0ΔV(19.3)

The unit of electric potential is joule per coulomb (J/C) or volt (V). The potential difference between two points is one volt if one joule of work is done in moving one coulomb of charge from one point to the other.

1 volt = 1 joule/1 coulomb

Multiples and sub-multiples of the volt: 1 mV = 10−3 V, 1 μV = 10−6 V, 1 kV = 103 V, 1 MV = 106 V, 1 GV = 109 V.

19.2

Electric Potential Due To A Point Charge

PHY-02 | PHY-03

Potential difference in a constant field and potential gradient

The potential difference in a constant electric field E is:

ΔV=−EΔr
E=−ΔVΔr

This equation is true only for a constant electric field. The quantity ΔV/Δr is the potential gradient: it represents the rate of change of potential with displacement. Thus the electric field at a point is equal to the negative of the potential gradient at that point. The unit of E is therefore volt per metre (V/m).

Note: The negative sign means that the electric field points in the direction in which the potential decreases.

Derivation of potential due to a point charge

For most cases the electric field is not uniform, so we derive an expression for potential in a non-uniform field. Let an isolated charge +Q be placed at the origin (Fig. 19.2). A test charge q is moved from a far point N to a point 1 against the electric field E by applying a force F. Because E varies with the inverse square of the distance, we divide the large distance into small steps of length Δr so that E stays constant in each step. Here r1, r2, …, rN are the distances of points 1, 2, …, N from Q.

Step 1: Work in the first step from N to N-1:

ΔWN→N−1=FΔrcos⁡ θ=qEΔrcos⁡ θ

Step 2: E and Δr are in opposite directions, so θ = 180°:

ΔWN→N−1=qEΔrcos⁡ 180∘=−qEΔr

Step 3: Put E = (1/(4π ε0)) (Q/r2) and Δr = rN-1 - rN:

ΔWN→N−1=q(14πε0)(Qr2)(rN−rN−1)

Step 4: rN and rN-1 are very close, so r2 = rN-1 rN:

ΔWN→N−1=Qq4πε0(1rN−1−1rN)

Step 5: Similarly for the other steps, down to ΔW2→1 = (Qq)/(4π ε0) (1/r1 - 1/r2). Adding all steps, the middle terms cancel:

ΔWN→1=Qq4πε0(1r1−1rN)

Step 6: Bring the charge from infinity: rN = ∞, so 1/rN = 0. Replace r1 by r to get the potential energy U at distance r from Q:

U=Qq4πε0r(19.4)

Step 7: The electric potential is V = U/q, hence:

V=Q4πε0r(19.5)

The electric potential at any point in an electric field is equal to the work done in bringing a unit positive charge from infinity to that point, keeping it in equilibrium. Equation (19.5) shows that the potential is the same at all points that are equidistant from an isolated source charge Q.

Note: SLO extension (not derived in the textbook): the capacitance of an isolated spherical conductor of radius R in vacuum is C = 4π ε0 R, obtained from C = Q/V with V = Q/(4π ε0 R).
Tip: V is inversely proportional to r, so potential falls as you move away from the charge. For two point charges Q and q, the potential energy is U = qV = Qq/(4π ε0 r).

19.3

Capacitors

PHY-04

What is a capacitor?

Capacitors are a common component in most electronic devices and are used to store energy. Typically, commercial capacitors have two identical parallel conducting plates separated by a distance d (Fig. 19.3); this is called a parallel plate capacitor. The medium between the plates is air or a sheet of some insulator and is known as the dielectric. Capacitors are mainly made of ceramic, glass or plastic, depending upon purpose and size; a computer chip contains a number of capacitors.

When the battery terminals are connected to an initially uncharged capacitor, equal amounts of positive and negative charge, +Q and -Q, are separated onto its two plates. The capacitor remains neutral overall, but we say it stores a charge Q. When a charge is transferred to plate A, electrostatic induction induces charge Q of opposite sign on the inner surface of plate B. Mutual attraction keeps the charges bound on the inner surfaces of the two plates, so the charge remains stored even after the battery is removed.

Capacitance of a capacitor

Experimentally, the charge stored on a capacitor is directly proportional to the voltage applied across it:

Q∝V
Q=CV
C=QV(19.6)

C is a constant of proportionality called the capacitance of the capacitor.

The capability of a capacitor to store charge is called its capacitance.

The unit of capacitance is the farad (F), named after Michael Faraday (1791–1867), an English scientist who contributed to electromagnetism and electrochemistry. Since capacitance is charge per unit voltage, 1 farad = 1 coulomb per volt. If a capacitor stores a charge of 1 coulomb (a very large amount of charge) with a potential difference of 1 volt between its plates, its capacitance is 1 farad. One farad is a very large capacitance; typical capacitors range from picofarad (1 pF = 10−12 F) to millifarad (1 mF = 10−3 F).

Capacitance of a parallel plate capacitor

Let each plate have area A and let the plate separation be d. The plates carry +Q and -Q. If the positive plate is at potential V1 and the negative plate at V2, the field between the plates is:

E=−ΔVΔr=−V2−V1d=V1−V2d
E=Vd(V1−V2=V)(i)

The surface charge density on a plate is σ = Q/A. By Gauss's law the field between the plates is:

E=σε0(ii)
E=QAε0(iii)

Combining (i) and (iii):

QAε0=Vd
QV=Aε0d

For air or vacuum between the plates, Q/V = Cvac, so:

Cvac=Aε0d(19.7 a)

When an insulating material (dielectric) is inserted between the plates, the capacitance is enhanced by a factor εr:

Cmed=Aε0εrd(19.7 b)
εr=CmedCvac(19.8)

The ratio of the capacitance of a capacitor with a dielectric medium between its plates to that of the same capacitor when the space is evacuated is called the relative permittivity εr of the material. From Cmed = (A ε0 εr)/d, the capacitance depends on: (1) the area of the plates, (2) the distance between the plates, and (3) the medium (dielectric) between the plates.

Tip: To increase C: increase the plate area A, decrease the separation d, or insert a dielectric of high εr. C does not depend on Q or V; those only fix how much charge is stored.
Common Mistake: A charged capacitor stores energy and a charge Q on each plate, but its net charge is zero because the plates carry +Q and -Q.

19.4

Combinations of Capacitors

PHY-05

Several capacitors can be connected together for a variety of applications. Multiple connections behave as a single equivalent capacitor, whose total capacitance depends on the individual capacitors and on how they are connected. Capacitors can be arranged in two simple and common types of connections: series and parallel combinations.

19.4.1 Series combination of capacitors

When capacitors are connected plate to plate, i.e. the right plate of one capacitor is connected to the left plate of the next, the combination is called a series combination (Fig. 19.4 shows three capacitors C1, C2, C3 connected between points A and B). If V is the potential difference of the battery and V1, V2, V3 are the potential differences across C1, C2, C3:

V=V1+V2+V3

The battery supplies the same charge Q to each capacitor. Due to electrostatic induction the charge on each capacitor is the same and equal to the charge supplied by the battery: Q = Q1 = Q2 = Q3. Using V = Q/C:

V=Q1C1+Q2C2+Q3C3
V=Q(1C1+1C2+1C3)

Energy Stored In A Capacitor and Uses

PHY-06 | PHY-08

A capacitor stores energy in the form of an electric field. The energy stored is electrical potential energy and is related to the charge Q and the voltage V on the capacitor. The capacitor starts with zero voltage and gradually comes up to its full voltage as it is charged. The first charge placed on the capacitor experiences ΔV = 0 because the uncharged capacitor has zero voltage; the final charge experiences ΔV = V because the capacitor now has its full voltage. Since Q is directly proportional to V, the graph of potential difference against charge is a straight line through the origin (Fig. 19.6: voltage on the vertical axis, charge on the horizontal axis, line OA, with B on the charge axis below A).

The electric potential energy E stored in the capacitor equals the area under the potential–charge graph, i.e. the area of the right-angled triangle OAB:

E=area of right-angled triangle OAB=12×Base  ×Height  

The base is Q and the height is V:

E=12QV(19.11 a)

Substituting Q = C V:

E=12CV2(19.11 b)

Substituting V = Q/C:

E=Q22C(19.11 c)

Equations (19.11 a, b, c) are valid for calculating the energy stored in a capacitor. (The textbook prints these as 9.11; the correct numbering is 19.11.) This energy is directly related to the amount of charge moved onto the plates and the voltage across them.

Note: For your information: a battery stores electrical energy through chemical reactions and releases it slowly. Unlike a battery, a capacitor temporarily stores electrical energy by distributing charged particles on its plates to create a potential difference. A capacitor takes a shorter time than a battery to charge up and can release all its energy very quickly.

Uses of capacitors

Capacitors are very important circuit elements in electronics. Applications in daily life:

  • Filtering unwanted frequencies in radio and TV sets.
  • Camera flashes (flash guns): store and quickly release a large amount of electrical energy.
  • Refrigerators: capacitors are usually used to keep the compressor running.
  • Electric fans: a capacitor is used to increase the speed of the fan.
  • Rectification circuits: a capacitor acts as a filter to reduce ripple voltage.

Charging of a capacitor

Consider an RC (resistor–capacitor) circuit (Fig. 19.7 a). When the switch is set at point 1, a battery of e.m.f. ε starts charging the capacitor through the resistor R. The charge builds up gradually on the plates to a maximum value Q0. While charging, electrons flow onto one plate and move off the other plate until the potential difference across the capacitor equals the e.m.f. of the battery.

The rate of flow of charge is not linear. At the start the current is at its highest value but gradually decreases to zero. The voltage on the capacitor is initially zero and rises rapidly at first, since the initial current is a maximum. As charge builds up on the plates, it repels more of the charge that is arriving, so the current drops. Charging stops when the potential difference across the plates equals the e.m.f. of the battery.

Maximum charge on capacitor = capacitance × e.m.f. of battery
Q0=Cε

The charging process is not linear but exponential:

Q=Q0(1−e−tRC)(19.12)

Here e = 2.7182 is a constant. The charging graph (Fig. 19.7 b) shows Q = 0 at t = 0, rising gradually to its maximum value Q0.

Time constant

Q=Q0(1−e−1)=Q0(1−12.718)=0.632Q0
QQ0=0.632=63.2 %
τ=RC

The smaller the resistance or the capacitance, the smaller the time constant and the faster the charging and discharging of the capacitor, and vice versa.

When a capacitor is discharging, the current is highest at the start and gradually decreases to 0. Bioelectricity

PHY-09 | PHY-10 | PHY-11

Generation of bioelectricity

Bioelectricity refers to the generation or action of electric currents or voltages in biological processes. Electric activity in living tissue is a cellular phenomenon that depends on the cell membrane. The cell membrane acts like a capacitor that stores energy as electrically charged ions on opposite sides of the membrane.

In most solutions, ions of a given charge are accompanied by ions of opposite charge, so the solution has no net charge. If two solutions of different concentrations are separated by a membrane that allows one kind of ion to pass but blocks the other, equal and opposite net charges are produced in the two solutions. This concentration imbalance gives rise to an electric potential difference between the solutions.

In living cells there are two types of solutions: those inside and outside the cell. The cell membrane (Fig. 19.9) is semipermeable: it allows certain ions to pass through while blocking others. In particular, nerve-cell and muscle-cell membranes are slightly permeable to positive potassium ions (K⁺), which diffuse outward, leaving a net negative charge in the cell. Ion channels allow specific inorganic ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ or Cl⁻) to diffuse rapidly down their electrochemical gradients across the lipid bilayer.

Cells control the flow of specific charged elements across the membrane with proteins that sit on the cell surface and create an opening for certain ions to pass through. These proteins are called ion channels. An ion channel spans the membrane and makes a hydrophilic tunnel across it, allowing its target molecules to pass through by diffusion. Channels are very selective and accept only one type of molecule (or a few closely related molecules).

When a cell is stimulated, it allows positive charges to enter the cell through open ion channels. The inside of the cell then becomes more positively charged, which triggers further electrical currents that turn into electrical pulses called action potentials. The bodies of many organisms use certain patterns of action potentials to initiate the correct movements, thoughts and behaviours. The bioelectric potential across a cell membrane is typically about 50 millivolts and is known as the resting potential. Bioelectric phenomena include fast signalling in nerves and the triggering of physical processes in muscles or glands.

Electrogenic and electroreceptive animals

There are several species of aquatic life, such as Electrophorus electricus, that can naturally generate external electric shocks through internal biological mechanisms that act as batteries. Such species are called electrogenic animals. Electric eels are probably the best known electrogenic animals; some types of bacteria, yeast and fish are also electrogenic. There are also animals that can detect electricity, called electroreceptive animals: echidnas, platypuses, bees, spiders, dolphins, sharks and rays. Electroreception is the ability to detect weak, naturally occurring electrostatic fields in the environment. Most electrogenic animals are also electroreceptive, but many electroreceptive animals are not electrogenic.

Note: For your information: electric eels are known for their ability to stun their prey by generating electricity, delivering shocks of up to 860 volts.

Q148If you wish to store a large amount of energy in a capacitor bank, would you connect capacitors in series or parallel? Explain.

Q156Define and explain the term electric potential. Derive an expression for electric potential at a field point due to a source charge.
Ans:

Electric potential at a point is the potential energy per unit charge, V = U/q; equivalently, the work done in bringing a unit positive charge from infinity to that point keeping it in equilibrium. It depends only on the location in the field, not on the test charge.

Derivation: place +Q at the origin and move a test charge q from a far point N to point 1 against the field. Divide the path into small steps Δr so that E is constant in each step.

Step  N→N−1:    ΔW=qEΔrcos⁡ 180∘=−qEΔr
With  E=(14πε0)(Qr2),Δr=rN−1−rN  and  r2=rN−1rN:
ΔWN→N−1=Qq4πε0(1rN−1−1rN)

Adding all the steps, the intermediate terms cancel:

ΔWN→1=Qq4πε0(1r1−1rN)

From infinity, 1/rN = 0; replacing r1 by r gives the potential energy:

U=Qq4πε0r

Dividing by q (V = U/q) gives the potential at distance r from Q:

V=Q4πε0r
Q157What is meant by potential gradient? Explain.
Ans:

The potential gradient is the rate of change of potential with displacement, ΔV/Δr.

In a constant electric field the potential difference between two points a distance Δr apart (along the field) is:

ΔV=−EΔr

Hence the electric field at a point is equal to the negative of the potential gradient at that point:

E=−ΔVΔr

Its unit is V/m. The negative sign shows that the field points in the direction of decreasing potential. For example, between the plates of a parallel plate capacitor, E = V/d.

Q158By using a graph, derive an expression for the energy stored in a capacitor.
Ans:

The capacitor starts with zero voltage and reaches full voltage V as it is charged to Q. Since Q ∝ V, the graph of potential difference against charge is a straight line through the origin (line OA, with B on the charge axis).

The energy stored equals the area under the graph, the area of the right-angled triangle OAB:

E=12×Base  ×Height  

The base is Q and the height is V, so:

E=12QV

Substituting Q = C V and V = Q/C:

E=12CV2  and  E=Q22C
Q159Derive the expression for the equivalent capacitance for a parallel combination of capacitors.
Ans:

Let C1, C2, C3 be connected between the same two points A and B across a battery. The charge supplied is shared: Q = Q1 + Q2 + Q3.

The battery supplies the same voltage V to each capacitor, so Q1 = C1 V, Q2 = C2 V, Q3 = C3 V:

Q=C1V+C2V+C3V=V(C1+C2+C3)
QV=C1+C2+C3

Putting Q/V = Ce:

Ce=C1+C2+C3

For n capacitors, Ce = C1 + C2 + … + Cn, which is larger than any individual capacitance.

Q160Derive the formula for the combined capacitance of capacitors in series.
Ans:

Let C1, C2, C3 be connected plate to plate across a battery of potential V:

V=V1+V2+V3

By electrostatic induction the charge on each capacitor is the same: Q = Q1 = Q2 = Q3. Using V = Q/C:

V=Q(1C1+1C2+1C3)
VQ=1C1+1C2+1C3

Putting V/Q = 1/Ce:

1Ce=1C1+1C2+1C3

For n capacitors, 1/Ce = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + … + 1/Cn, so Ce is less than any individual capacitance.

Q161Explain the process of charging and discharging of a capacitor.
Ans:

Charging: in an RC circuit, with the switch at point 1, a battery of e.m.f. ε charges the capacitor through resistor R. Electrons flow onto one plate and off the other until the potential difference across the capacitor equals the e.m.f. The current is highest at the start and falls to zero, because the charge already on the plates repels the arriving charge. The voltage is zero at first and rises rapidly. The charge grows exponentially:

Q=Q0(1−e−tRC),Q0=Cε

After one time constant τ = RC the charge is 0.632 Q0 (63.2 %).

Discharging: with the switch at point 2, the charge -Q on the negative plate flows through the resistor and neutralises +Q on the positive plate. The current is highest at the start and decreases to zero, when the capacitor is fully discharged. The charge decays exponentially:

Q=Q0e−tRC

After t = RC the charge left is 0.367 Q0 (36.7 %). A smaller time constant means faster charging and discharging. The same forms, x = x0 (1 - e−t/RC) and x = x0 e−t/RC, apply to potential difference and current.

Q162How is bioelectricity generated in animals? Explain.
Ans:

Bioelectricity is the generation or action of electric currents or voltages in biological processes. It is a cellular phenomenon that depends on the cell membrane, which acts like a capacitor storing energy as charged ions on its two sides.

The semipermeable membrane separates solutions of different ion concentrations inside and outside the cell. Nerve and muscle membranes are slightly permeable to K⁺, which diffuse outward leaving the inside net negative; this gives a potential difference (resting potential, about 50 mV).

Ion channels are proteins that sit on the cell surface and form selective hydrophilic tunnels for ions such as Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ to diffuse through.

When the cell is stimulated, positive charges enter through open ion channels and the inside becomes more positive. This triggers further electrical currents that turn into electrical pulses called action potentials. Organisms use patterns of action potentials for fast signalling in nerves and for movements, thoughts, behaviours and the triggering of muscles and glands.

Vocabulary

Electric potential energyThe energy that is needed to move a charge against an electric field.
Electric potentialThe potential energy per unit charge at a point in an electric field.
Potential differenceThe work done in moving a unit positive charge from one point to another, keeping the charge in electrostatic equilibrium.
VoltThe potential difference between two points when one joule of work is done in moving one coulomb of charge from one point to the other.
Potential gradientThe rate of change of potential with displacement.
CapacitorAn electronic device used to store electric energy (charge).
DielectricThe insulating medium (air or a sheet of some insulator) between the plates of a capacitor.
CapacitanceThe capability of a capacitor to store charge; the ratio of the charge on a plate to the potential difference across the capacitor.
FaradThe capacitance of a capacitor that stores a charge of 1 coulomb with a potential difference of 1 volt between its plates.
Relative permittivityThe ratio of the capacitance of a capacitor with a dielectric between the plates to the capacitance of the same capacitor when the space is evacuated.
Series combinationCapacitors connected plate to plate, the right plate of one capacitor joined to the left plate of the next.
Parallel combinationCapacitors connected between the same two points.
Equivalent capacitanceThe capacitance of the single capacitor that behaves in the same way as a whole combination of capacitors.
RC circuitA circuit containing a resistor and a capacitor, used to charge or discharge the capacitor through the resistor.
Time constantThe time during which 63.2 % of the maximum charge is deposited on the plates of the capacitor in an RC circuit; it equals RC.
Surface charge densityThe charge per unit area of a capacitor plate, equal to Q/A.
BioelectricityThe generation or action of electric currents or voltages in biological processes.
Semipermeable membraneA membrane, such as the cell membrane, that allows certain ions to pass through while blocking others.
Ion channelA protein on the cell surface that creates an opening for certain ions to pass through the cell membrane.
Action potentialAn electrical pulse produced when the inside of a stimulated cell becomes more positively charged and triggers further electrical currents.
Resting potentialThe bioelectric potential across a cell membrane, typically about 50 millivolts.
Electrogenic animalsAnimals that can naturally generate external electric shocks through internal biological mechanisms that act as batteries.
ElectroreceptionThe ability to detect weak, naturally occurring electrostatic fields in the environment.
