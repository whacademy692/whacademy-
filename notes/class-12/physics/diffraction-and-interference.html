Diffraction and Interference

Interference and diffraction are two fundamental wave properties that arise from the wave nature of light, sound and other waves. Interference occurs when two or more waves in the same medium superimpose, strengthening (constructive) or cancelling (destructive) each other. Diffraction is the bending and spreading of a wave around the edges of an obstacle or through a narrow slit. This chapter studies two-source (double-slit) interference using water waves, sound, light and microwaves; the conditions needed to see interference fringes; fringe positions and spacing (Δy=λLd\Delta y = \dfrac{\lambda L}{d}); the diffraction grating and the equation d sin⁡θ=mλd\,\sin \theta = m\lambda; and finally the electron double-slit experiment with two interpretations of quantum mechanics — the Copenhagen and Many-Worlds interpretations.

Interference And Diffraction — Basic Ideas

PHY-01

Interference

Interference occurs when two or more waves travelling through the same medium interact and superimpose on each other. Depending on how the waves are aligned, they either strengthen each other (constructive interference) or cancel each other (destructive interference). It is a fundamental property of all types of waves.

Constructive interference

Crests (peaks) of waves overlap and reinforce each other, producing a resultant wave of higher intensity — brighter light or louder sound.

Destructive interference

The crest of one wave coincides with the trough (valley) of another, so they cancel partially or completely — darkness or quieter sound.

Superposition principle

The resultant wave at any point in space is the sum of the individual waves present at that point. The waves add up constructively or destructively depending on their relative phases (alignment).

Diffraction

Diffraction is the bending of a wave around the edges of an obstacle or through a narrow slit. Unlike reflection (a wave bouncing off a surface), diffraction makes the wave spread out into regions beyond the geometrical shadow of the obstacle. Smaller slit width compared with the wavelength gives a more pronounced diffraction effect.

Useful terms

Wavelength (λ): distance between two consecutive crests (or troughs). Frequency (f): number of wave cycles passing a point per unit time (Hz). Wave front: a surface joining points of the wave that are in the same phase. Coherent sources: waves of the same frequency and a constant phase relationship.

Everyday examples of interference: beats between slightly off-tune instruments and colourful bands in soap bubbles. Diffraction is used in X-ray crystallography (structure of materials) and in diffraction gratings (separating light into its colours).

Conditions For Observing Interference Fringes

PHY-02

To observe interference fringes in a two-source set-up, the following conditions must be met:

1. Coherent sources

The two sources must have a constant phase relationship and emit waves of the same frequency and wavelength.

2. Narrow slits

The slits must be narrow and close together compared with the wavelength of the waves, so that waves from each slit spread out, overlap and interfere.

3. Monochromatic waves

The waves should ideally have a single wavelength to produce a clear interference pattern.

4. Stable environment

The set-up must be free from vibrations and air currents, which could disturb the wave patterns and hide the fringes.

Tip: Memory trick for the four conditions: C-N-M-S → Coherent, Narrow, Monochromatic, Stable.

4.2

Interference Of Water Waves

PHY-01

Interference can be demonstrated in a ripple tank using two point sources. Two coherent sources are created by generating ripples in the water from two separate point sources. The tank is illuminated from above (lamp) to enhance visibility, and the pattern is seen as a shadow on white paper under the tank. Where the waves meet they interfere: regions of constructive interference (waves reinforce and become larger) alternate with regions of destructive interference (waves cancel), giving a pattern of alternating crests and troughs on the water surface.

In phase → constructive

If two waves arrive in phase (crests or troughs arrive at exactly the same time), they interfere constructively and the resultant has crests much higher and troughs much deeper than the individual waves.

Anti-phase → destructive

If they arrive in anti-phase (phase difference π radians or 180°), the peaks of one wave arrive with the troughs of the other, so they interfere destructively and the resultant has a smaller amplitude.

Path difference

A phase difference may be produced by letting the two sets of waves travel different distances. This difference in distance of travel is called the path difference.

4.3

Interference Of Sound Waves

PHY-01

Two loudspeakers connected to the same signal generator and amplifier produce notes of the same frequency (coherent sound) and are placed facing a screen. Sound waves are longitudinal and consist of compressions and rarefactions. The waves from both speakers overlap, creating regions of constructive and destructive interference. The pattern is detected by a microphone (detector) that measures the intensity of sound at various points on the screen.

Constructive interference (louder sound)

At points such as P₁, P₄, P₅, P₆ and P₉, compressions (or rarefactions) from the two speakers align, so the sound appears louder.

Destructive interference (quieter sound)

At points such as P₂, P₃, P₇ and P₈, a compression aligns with a rarefaction and vice versa, giving a quieter sound.

Note: This principle of destructive interference of sound is used in noise-cancelling headphones.

Interference Of Light Waves — Young's Double-Slit Experiment

PHY-01

In a double-slit experiment a laser beam is typically used as the coherent light source. It is directed at a barrier with two narrow slits, which act as two coherent sources. A screen behind the slits shows the interference pattern: alternating bright and dark fringes (vertical lines spread out horizontally). Without diffraction and interference the light would simply make two lines on the screen.

Thomas Young's experiment (1801)

The English physicist Thomas Young performed the experiment to demonstrate the wave nature of light. He first passed sunlight (a mixture of wavelengths) through a single slit to make it partially coherent, then through a pair of closely spaced slits (S₁ and S₂). The light diffracts from each narrow slit, producing semicircular waves that overlap and interfere on the screen, giving bright (constructive) and dark (destructive) fringes.

Sunlight made the effect easy to observe, but because each wavelength forms its own pattern, monochromatic (single-wavelength) light is used to see a clear pattern. The double-slit pattern for water waves is nearly identical to that for light. Young's experiment confirmed that light shows wave-like behaviour.

Note: In the early 1800s the nature of light was still debated: Christian Huygens proposed a wave-like behaviour, while Isaac Newton offered an alternative explanation for colour and observable effects.

Path Difference, Maxima And Minima

PHY-04

The wave from slit S₂ travels slightly farther than that from S₁ to reach a point P on the screen. Let d = separation of the slits, L = distance from the slits to the screen, y = distance from the central maximum (O) to point P, and θ = angle of the line to P. From triangle ABC, sin⁡θ=BCAB\sin \theta = \dfrac{BC}{AB}, so BC=AB sin⁡θBC = AB\,\sin \theta. The path difference is therefore:

Path difference d sin⁡θd\,\sin \theta

Constructive interference (bright fringes / maxima)

Occurs when the path difference is an integer multiple of the wavelength:

d sin⁡θ=mλd\,\sin \theta = m\lambda
m=0, ±1, ±2, ±3, …m = 0 ,\ \pm 1 ,\ \pm 2 ,\ \pm 3 ,\ \ldots

m is the order of the maxima. The central maximum is 0th order (m=0); m=±1(m = 0) ;\ m = \pm 1 gives the two 1st-order bright fringes, one above and one below O, and so on.

Destructive interference (dark fringes / minima)

Occurs when the path difference is an odd multiple of half the wavelength:

d sin⁡θ=(m+12)λd\,\sin \theta = (m + \tfrac12 )\lambda
m=0, ±1, ±2, ±3, …m = 0 ,\ \pm 1 ,\ \pm 2 ,\ \pm 3 ,\ \ldots

In this book the first minimum occurs at m=0m = 0 (1st-order minimum).

Common Mistake: For dark fringes use (m+12)λ(m + \tfrac12 )\lambda, not mλ. Since m starts from 0 here, the 3rd minimum has m=2m = 2 and the 1st minimum has m=0m = 0.

Positions Of Fringes And Fringe Spacing

PHY-03

Let P be the position of the mth-order bright fringe: d sin⁡θ=mλd\,\sin \theta = m\lambda. If θ is very small, sin⁡θ≈tan⁡θ\sin \theta \approx \tan \theta, so d tan⁡θ=mλd\,\tan \theta = m\lambda. Since tan⁡θ=yL\tan \theta = \dfrac{y}{L}, we get d(y/L)=mλd(y/L) = m\lambda.

Bright fringe:y=mλLdy = \dfrac{m \lambda L}{d}
Dark fringe:y=(m+12)λLdy = \dfrac{(m + \tfrac12 ) \lambda L}{d}

These equations give the positions of maxima and minima on the screen from known values of d, L and λ. The central maximum (m=0m = 0) is always at y=0y = 0.

Fringe spacing (Δy)

The distance between two consecutive bright (or dark) fringes is the fringe spacing.

Bright: Δy=y(m+1)−y(m)=(m+1)λLd−mλLd\Delta y = y(m+1) - y(m) = \dfrac{(m+1)\lambda L}{d} - \dfrac{m\lambda L}{d}

Δy=λLd\Delta y = \dfrac{\lambda L}{d}

For dark fringes: Δy=(m+1+12)λLd−(m+12)λLd=λLd\Delta y = \dfrac{(m+1+\tfrac12 )\lambda L}{d} - \dfrac{(m+\tfrac12 )\lambda L}{d} = \dfrac{\lambda L}{d}. So the fringe spacing between bright fringes and between dark fringes is the same.

Dependence of fringe spacing

Δy∝λ, Δy∝L, Δy∝1d\Delta y \propto \lambda ,\ \Delta y \propto L ,\ \Delta y \propto \dfrac{1}{d}. Closer slits (smaller d) spread the fringes farther apart.

Common Mistake: Convert units before substituting: nm → 10−910^{-9} m, µm → 10−610^{-6} m, mm → 10−310^{-3} m. Use θ in degrees on the calculator when finding sin⁡θ\sin \theta.

4.7

Interference Of Microwaves

PHY-01

Microwaves can also be used for two-source interference experiments. These involve two coherent microwave sources, i.e. microwaves of the same frequency and phase. When they overlap they create an interference pattern of alternating regions of constructive and destructive interference.

Experimental set-up

A microwave transmitter emits microwaves towards metal plates with two slits (openings), which act as new sources of waves. The waves from these slits overlap and interfere. A microwave receiver (detector), placed at variable distances and angles from the slits, is moved through the various points of interference and measures the intensity of the resulting microwaves. At points of constructive interference the detector records a higher intensity; at points of destructive interference the intensity is much lower or even zero. The pattern can be shown on a screen or a recording device connected to the detector.

Use of the data

The distance between points of maximum or minimum intensity is related to the wavelength and the geometry of the set-up, so the wavelength of the microwaves can be calculated.

Importance

Such experiments demonstrate the wave nature of microwaves. Understanding microwave interference is crucial in designing microwave communication systems so as to avoid signal loss due to destructive interference.

4.8

Diffraction Grating

PHY-05

A diffraction grating is an optical device consisting of a transparent material (glass or plastic) with a large number of equally spaced parallel slits or grooves etched or ruled over it. The slits are closely spaced and act as individual sources of secondary waves when illuminated by incident light.

Grating element (grating constant)

The distance between two adjacent slits is called the grating element, represented by d:

d=unit length of gratingtotal number of linesd=\dfrac{\text{unit length of grating}}{\text{total number of lines}}

The grating element is typically of the order of the wavelength of light or smaller. The number of slits per unit length, N, determines the resolving power of the grating.

Diffraction of light through a grating

When monochromatic light illuminates a grating, light waves from the slits interfere constructively and destructively, creating a pattern of bright and dark fringes. The angles of the bright maxima depend on the wavelength of light and the spacing between the slits:

d sin⁡θ=mλd\,\sin \theta = m\lambda

By measuring the angle of diffraction θ for a particular order m of the bright fringe and knowing the spacing d, the wavelength of light can be determined: λ=d sin⁡θm\lambda = \dfrac{d\,\sin \theta}{m}. This makes the grating a valuable tool in spectroscopy and other scientific applications.

Double slit versus grating

Maxima are produced at the same angles in both, but those for a grating are narrower and hence sharper. The maxima become narrower and the regions between them darker as the number of slits is increased.

Note: For a single slit of width a, the m-th diffraction minimum occurs at a sin⁡θ=mλ(m=1, 2, 3, …)a\,\sin \theta = m\lambda (m = 1 ,\ 2 ,\ 3 ,\ \ldots ). This form is used in the exercise questions on single-slit diffraction.
Common Mistake: Do not confuse the grating element d with the number of lines per unit length N. d=1Nd = \dfrac{1}{N}. E.g. 5000 lines per cm means d=15000 cm=2×10−4 cmd = \dfrac{1}{5000} \,\text{cm} = 2 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{cm}.

4.10

Electron Double-Slit Experiment

PHY-06

The electron double-slit experiment is a classic physics experiment that demonstrates the principles of wave-particle duality and the nature of reality at the quantum level.

Experimental set-up

Electron gun: electrons are emitted from the electron gun. Double slits: the electrons pass through two parallel slits. Screen: the electrons hit the screen behind the slits and create a visible pattern.

Result

If electrons were classical particles, two distinct patterns corresponding to the two slits would be expected. However, the actual outcome is an interference pattern, similar to what is expected from waves. This suggests that electrons exhibit wave-like behaviour.

Implications

Quantum mechanics: it laid the foundation for quantum mechanics, which describes the behaviour of particles at the atomic and subatomic level. Limits of classical physics: it showed that classical physics is insufficient to describe the behaviour of particles at the quantum level. Philosophical implications: it raises questions about the nature of reality, observation and the role of the observer in shaping reality.

The experiment has led to several interpretations of quantum mechanics. The two prominent ones are the Copenhagen interpretation and the Many-Worlds interpretation.

4.11

Copenhagen Interpretation

PHY-06

The Copenhagen interpretation (CI) was formulated by Niels Bohr and Werner Heisenberg. It states that a quantum system, like an electron before measurement, does not possess a definite position or momentum. Instead it exists in a superposition of all possible states simultaneously.

How CI explains the electron double-slit experiment

  • Before detection, the electron is in a superposition of states, passing through both slits simultaneously (wave-like behaviour).
  • The act of measurement (detecting the electron on the screen) forces the wave function to collapse into a definite position, explaining why a single electron hits at a specific point (particle-like behaviour).
  • The interference pattern arises from the probability distribution of where the electron might be detected: regions of higher probability density are bright fringes; regions of lower probability density are dark fringes.

Criticisms

  • The "collapse of the wave function" on measurement seems like an arbitrary break from the deterministic nature of physics.
  • It does not explain what happens to the "other possibilities" in the superposition before measurement.

The Copenhagen interpretation remains widely held and influential, but its implications and limitations are still debated among physicists and philosophers.

Answer: The act of measurement forces the wave function to collapse into one definite position, so a single electron is detected at a specific point.

4.12

Many-Worlds Interpretation

PHY-06

The many-worlds interpretation, proposed by Hugh Everett III in 1957, presents a radically different view of quantum mechanics. It proposes that every interaction in the quantum realm splits the universe into multiple realities (worlds), one for each possible outcome. Each branch of reality carries forward one possibility from the superposition.

How MWI explains the electron double-slit experiment

  • The electron initially exists in a superposition, going through both slits in all the newly created worlds.
  • In each world, the electron interacts with the detector screen and leaves its mark (particle-like behaviour) at a specific location.
  • The interference pattern on the screen reflects the combined effect of electrons hitting the screen in all the parallel universes. We experience only one world (the one where the electron interacted with our detector), but the interference pattern signifies the existence of the other possibilities.

Criticisms

  • The concept of multiple universes is difficult to test experimentally.
  • It does not provide a clear explanation of the transition from superposition to a definite state upon measurement.
Note: Both interpretations try to explain the puzzling behaviour of quantum systems at the microscopic level. Neither is universally accepted. In short: Copenhagen emphasises indeterminism, complementarity and the role of measurement; Many-Worlds posits a multitude of parallel universes in which all quantum outcomes coexist.

Answer: No. Neither is universally accepted and physicists still debate their implications; the underlying reality remains an ongoing exploration.

Short Questions

Short 1How is an interference pattern formed by a diffraction grating different from the pattern formed by a double slit?
Answer: Maxima occur at the same angles in both, but those of a grating are much narrower and sharper, and the regions between them are darker. A double slit gives broader fringes.
Short 2A beam of light always spreads out. Why can a beam not be produced with parallel rays to prevent spreading?
Answer: Because of diffraction: whenever light passes through an opening (or past an edge) of finite size it bends and spreads into the region beyond. Hence a perfectly parallel beam cannot be produced.
Short 3In the sunlight, the shadow of a building has fuzzy edges even if the building does not. Is this a refraction effect? Explain.
Answer: No. It is a diffraction effect: light bends around the edges of the building and spreads into the geometrical shadow, so the edges of the shadow are blurred.
Short 4A laser pointer emits a coherent beam of parallel light rays. Does the light from such a source spread out at all? Explain.
Answer: Yes. Even laser light spreads out slightly, because it is diffracted when it passes through the small opening of the pointer; no real beam is perfectly parallel.
Short 5A beam of light passes through a single slit to create a diffraction pattern. How will the spacing of the bands in the pattern change if the width of the slit is increased?
Answer: The bands become closer together (less spreading). Diffraction is more pronounced for a narrow slit, so increasing the width reduces the spread of the pattern.
Short 6Describe a diffraction grating and the interference pattern it produces.
Answer: A diffraction grating is a glass or plastic plate with a very large number of equally spaced parallel slits or grooves. Monochromatic light passing through it gives sharp, narrow bright maxima at angles given by d sin⁡θ=mλd\,\sin \theta = m\lambda, separated by dark regions.
Short 7Suppose a monochromatic light falls on a diffraction grating. What happens to the interference pattern if the same light falls on a grating that has more lines per centimetre?
Answer: The grating element d=1Nd = \dfrac{1}{N} becomes smaller, so sin⁡θ=mλd\sin \theta = \dfrac{m\lambda}{d} increases: the maxima appear at larger angles and are spread farther apart; they also become sharper because more slits take part.
Short 8What is the significance of the equation d sin⁡θ=mλd\,\sin \theta = m\lambda in the context of (a) diffraction gratings (b) Young's double-slit experiment?
Answer: (a) It gives the angles of the bright maxima of the grating, so λ can be found from a measured θ and known d. (b) It is the condition of constructive interference (path difference = mλ) for bright fringes, from which y=mλLdy = \dfrac{m\lambda L}{d} follows.
Short 9What is the effect of the following aspects of a diffraction grating on the resulting interference pattern: (a) number of slits (b) width of the slits?
Answer: (a) More slits make the maxima narrower and sharper and the regions between them darker. (b) Narrower slits diffract light more strongly, spreading it over a wider region of the screen; wider slits spread it less.
Short 10Describe the conditions necessary for sustained interference patterns to be observed in Young's double-slit experiment.
Answer: (1) The sources must be coherent (same frequency/wavelength and constant phase relationship). (2) The slits must be narrow and close together. (3) The light should be monochromatic. (4) The set-up must be stable, free from vibrations and air currents.

Comprehensive Questions

Long 1Design an experiment using a ripple tank to demonstrate the principles of interference with water waves. Describe what you expect to observe when two wave sources are introduced.
Answer:
  • Apparatus: a shallow ripple tank containing water, a vibrator carrying two point sources that touch the water, a lamp above the tank and a sheet of white paper below it.
  • Procedure: switch on the vibrator so that the two point sources produce circular ripples of the same frequency (coherent sources). Illuminate the tank from above.
  • Observation: the circular waves overlap. Along some lines the crests of one wave meet the crests of the other (in phase) — constructive interference, large waves. Along other lines crests meet troughs (anti-phase, phase difference π) — destructive interference, almost calm water. A stable pattern of alternating crests and troughs is seen as bright and dark lines on the white paper.
Long 2Briefly explain why we see a spectrum of colours (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet) in a rainbow. Is it due to reflection, refraction, or interference of light?
Answer: Sunlight is a mixture of wavelengths. When it enters water droplets it is refracted, and each colour (wavelength) is bent by a different amount, so white light is spread into its spectrum. The colours are therefore mainly due to refraction (dispersion) of sunlight by water droplets, not interference.
Long 3Describe two examples of how interference of light waves plays a role in natural phenomena you can observe in everyday life (excluding rainbows).
Answer: (i) Soap bubbles: light waves reflected from the two surfaces of the thin soap film interfere, constructively for some colours and destructively for others, giving colourful bands. (ii) Butterfly wings and opal: rows of reflectors act like diffraction gratings, so different colours are reflected at different angles and a shimmering colour is seen.
Long 4Sometimes, when two tuning forks with slightly different frequencies are struck close together, you hear a wavering sound instead of a clear tone. Explain what causes this "beat" phenomenon and how it is related to the interference of sound waves.
Answer: The two sound waves have slightly different frequencies, so their phase difference at the listener changes continuously. At one instant compressions meet compressions (constructive interference) and the sound is loud; a little later compressions meet rarefactions (destructive interference) and the sound is soft. This repeated loud–soft variation is the beat. The number of beats per second equals the difference of the two frequencies.
Long 5Both CDs and DVDs use lasers to store information, but DVDs hold much more data. How might the principles of diffraction be used in DVD design to achieve this higher storage capacity?
Answer: The tracks (grooves) on a CD/DVD act like a diffraction grating, and diffraction limits how small a spot a laser can be focused to; this limit is smaller for shorter wavelengths. A DVD uses a laser of shorter wavelength and focuses a smaller spot, so its tracks can be narrower and closer together (smaller grating element) and many more tracks fit on the disc, giving a higher storage capacity.
Long 6Describe a diffraction grating and its key features like grating constant and slit width. How does a diffraction grating separate white light into its constituent colours?
Answer: A diffraction grating is a glass or plastic plate with a large number of equally spaced parallel slits or grooves. Its key features are the grating constant (grating element) d — the distance between two adjacent slits, d = unit length / number of lines — and the slit width, which controls how strongly each slit diffracts the light. For white light, d sin⁡θ=mλd\,\sin \theta = m\lambda gives a different angle θ for each wavelength in a given order m; therefore the colours are spread out at different angles into a spectrum (the central m=0m = 0 image remains white).
Long 7Scientists use X-ray diffraction to determine the structure of crystals. Briefly explain how the diffraction pattern of X-rays helps reveal the arrangement of atoms within a crystal lattice.
Answer: The atoms of a crystal are arranged in regular rows and planes whose spacing is of the order of the wavelength of X-rays, so the crystal acts like a diffraction grating. X-rays scattered by the atoms interfere, giving bright spots only in directions where the scattered waves are in phase (constructive interference). The positions (angles) and intensities of these spots depend on the spacing and arrangement of atoms, so analysing the pattern reveals the structure of the crystal lattice.

Vocabulary

InterferenceThe superposition of two or more waves in the same medium, giving a larger or smaller displacement
Constructive interferenceCrests overlap and reinforce, giving a larger resultant (brighter light, louder sound)
Destructive interferenceA crest meets a trough and they cancel, giving a weaker resultant
Superposition principleThe resultant wave at any point is the sum of the individual waves there
DiffractionBending of a wave around an obstacle edge or through a narrow slit into the shadow region
Coherent sourcesSources with the same frequency and a constant phase relationship
WavelengthThe distance between two consecutive crests (or troughs)
FrequencyThe number of wave cycles passing a point per unit time, measured in hertz
Wave frontA surface joining points of a wave that are in the same phase
Slit widthThe width of the opening through which a wave passes in diffraction
Diffraction gratingA plate with a large number of equally spaced parallel slits or grooves
Grating elementThe distance between two adjacent slits of a grating
Diffraction patternThe distribution of intensity after diffraction, usually alternating bright and dark bands
Path differenceThe difference in distance travelled by two waves to reach the same point
In phaseCrests (or troughs) of two waves arrive at exactly the same time
Anti-phaseTwo waves with a phase difference of \pi radians (180°)
Order of a maximumThe integer m in d\sin\theta=m\lambda; the central maximum has order zero
Central maximumThe zero-order bright fringe, located at y=0y = 0
Fringe spacingThe distance between two consecutive bright (or dark) fringes
Monochromatic lightLight of a single wavelength
MaximaBright fringes, produced where waves interfere constructively
MinimaDark fringes, produced where waves interfere destructively
Ripple tankA tank of water with a vibrator and a lamp used to show interference of water waves
CompressionA region of a sound wave where particles are pushed together
RarefactionA region of a sound wave where particles are spread apart
Young's double-slit experimentThe 1801 experiment that demonstrated the wave nature of light
Electron double-slit experimentA classic experiment demonstrating wave-particle duality at the quantum level
Superposition of statesExisting in all possible states at once before measurement, as in the Copenhagen view
Collapse of the wave functionMeasurement forces the electron into one definite position
Copenhagen interpretationInterpretation of Bohr and Heisenberg: no definite position before measurement
Many-worlds interpretationEverett's 1957 view that every interaction splits the universe into many worlds
Resolving power of a gratingIt is determined by the number of slits per unit length
X-ray crystallographyA technique using diffraction of X-rays to understand the structure of materials
SpectroscopyUse of a grating to disperse light into its constituent wavelengths
Noise-cancelling headphonesDevices that use destructive interference of sound
Wave-particle dualityElectrons show wave-like and particle-like behaviour
Probability density (Copenhagen)High probability density gives bright fringes and low gives dark fringes
