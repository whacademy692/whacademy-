NOTES Chapter at a Glance

Quick Notes — Read Before the Q&A

1. Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation
Every object attracts every other object with a force that is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres: F = G m₁m₂/r². The force is always attractive and does not depend on the medium between the masses. It explains the fall of objects, the tides caused by the Moon's pull and the orbiting of planets around the Sun. It was later refined by Einstein's theory of general relativity but remains a cornerstone of physics.
2. The Gravitational Constant G
G is the constant of proportionality in Newton's law. Its value is 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻², so the force between everyday objects such as ships or people is extremely small (of the order of 10⁻⁷ N for 45 kg and 50 kg bodies 1 m apart). Henry Cavendish measured G in 1798, about 100 years after Newton, using a torsion balance. The unit follows from G = F r²/(m₁m₂), giving N m² kg⁻².
3. Action and Reaction in Gravitation
The gravitational forces F₁₂ and F₂₁ that two masses exert on each other are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, F₁₂ = −F₂₁. This shows that Newton's law of gravitation is consistent with Newton's third law of motion. Because F ∝ 1/r², doubling the distance reduces the force to one quarter and tripling it reduces the force to one ninth.
4. Gravitational Field Strength
The gravitational field is the area around a massive object where its gravitational force acts. Gravitational field strength is the gravitational force per unit mass at a point, g = F_g/m, measured in N kg⁻¹. It is a vector quantity pointing in the direction of the gravitational force. Since a_g = F_g/m = g, gravitational field strength and acceleration due to gravity are equivalent.
5. g on the Surface of the Earth and Other Planets
For a body of mass m₀ on the surface of the Earth, equating m₀g with G m₀M/R² gives g = GM/R². The mass of the body cancels, so light and heavy bodies fall at the same rate. With M = 6 × 10²⁴ kg and R = 6.4 × 10⁶ m, g ≈ 9.8 m s⁻². On another planet g = Gm/r², so g and the weight W = mg depend on that planet's mass and radius; on Jupiter g is about 24.77 m s⁻², roughly 2.5 times the Earth's value.
6. Variation of g with Height
At a height h above the surface the distance from the Earth's centre is R + h, so g_h = GM/(R + h)². Using GM = gR², g_h = gR²/(R + h)². As h increases, g decreases: it is about 9.8 m s⁻² at the height of Mount Everest, 8.7 m s⁻² at 400 km and 0.2 m s⁻² at 35,700 km. For small heights, h ≪ R, so (R + h)² ≈ R² and g is nearly constant.
7. Gravitational Field Lines
Gravitational field lines show the direction and strength of a field: closer lines mean a stronger field and lines farther apart mean a weaker one. For a point mass (all its mass treated as concentrated at one point) the lines point radially inward in all directions in a symmetric pattern. Outside a uniform sphere the field is the same as that of a point mass at its centre. Inside a uniform sphere only the mass within the radius matters, so the field strength increases steadily toward the surface.
8. Satellites and Launching
A satellite is any object that orbits a planet because of gravity and keeps a stable path. Natural satellites are moons; artificial satellites (communication, weather and navigation) are placed in orbit on purpose. The first artificial satellite was launched in 1957, and the International Space Station is the largest satellite in orbit and can be seen with the naked eye. If the launch speed is too low the satellite falls back to Earth; if it is too high the satellite moves in an elliptical orbit or escapes Earth's gravity (escape speed). The correct speed gives a circular orbit, which needs the least energy.
9. Orbital Velocity
In a circular orbit gravity supplies the centripetal acceleration, a_c = v²/r = g = GM/r². Therefore the orbital velocity is v = √(GM/r), where r = R + h is measured from the centre of the Earth. The speed does not depend on the satellite's mass, and a satellite moves faster when it is closer to the Earth. Just above the surface v ≈ 7.9 km s⁻¹, the ISS at about 400 km moves at about 7.7 km s⁻¹ and completes about 16 orbits in 24 hours, and the Earth moves around the Sun at about 30 km s⁻¹.
10. Geostationary Satellites and the Hill Sphere
A geostationary satellite has a period of 24 hours, orbits from west to east directly above the equator and so stays above the same point on the Earth. Equating v = 2πr/T with v = √(GM/r) gives r = (GMT²/4π²)^(1/3) = 4.23 × 10⁷ m, a height of about 3.6 × 10⁴ km, with orbital speed about 3.07 km s⁻¹. At least three geostationary satellites are needed for global coverage. A lunar-stationary satellite is impossible because the required orbit (about 88,417 km) lies outside the Moon's Hill sphere (about 60,000 km), the region where the Moon's own gravity dominates over the Earth's.
11. Gravitational Potential Energy and Potential
The formula ΔP.E = mgh is accurate only where g is constant, near the Earth's surface. In general the absolute gravitational potential energy of a mass m at distance r from the centre of the Earth is U = −GMm/r, taking zero potential energy at infinity; it is negative at any finite distance and increases toward zero as the object moves away. Gravitational potential is the potential energy per unit mass, V = U/m = −GM/r, measured in J kg⁻¹. At the Earth's surface V ≈ −62.3 MJ kg⁻¹.
PART 1 Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation

Law, G and the Action-Reaction Pair

1. Define Newton's law of universal gravitation.
• State Newton's law of gravitation.
Every object in the universe attracts every other object with a force which is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres.
2. Write the mathematical form of Newton's law of gravitation.
• Give the formula for the gravitational force between two masses m1 and m2 separated by a distance r.
Fg = G m1m2 / r2, where G is the gravitational constant and r is the distance between the centres of the two masses.
3. What is the gravitational constant 'G'?
• Define the gravitational constant and state its value.
G is the constant of proportionality in Newton's law of gravitation. Its value is 6.67 × 10-11 N m2 kg-2.
4. What is the SI unit of G?
The SI unit of G is N m2 kg-2, obtained from G = Fgr2 / (m1m2).
5. Who measured the value of G and how?
• When and by whom was G first measured?
Henry Cavendish measured G in 1798 using a torsion balance apparatus, about 100 years after Newton's work.
6. Why is Newton's law of gravitation called a universal law?
Because it applies to all objects in the universe, from an apple and the Earth to planets, stars and galaxies, with the same formula everywhere.
7. What is the force of gravity?
• Define gravitational force.
The force of attraction between any two objects in the universe due to their masses is called the force of gravity (Fg).
8. Is gravitational force attractive or repulsive?
Gravitational force is always attractive.
9. Does the gravitational force depend on the medium between the masses?
No. The gravitational force does not depend on the medium between the masses.
10. Why is gravitational force called an inverse-square force?
Because it is inversely proportional to the square of the distance between the masses: Fg ∝ 1/r2.
11. How does the gravitational force change if the distance between two bodies is doubled?
• What happens to Fg when r becomes 2r?
The force decreases by a factor of 4, i.e. it becomes F/4.
12. What happens to the gravitational force when the distance between two bodies is tripled?
The force decreases by a factor of 9, i.e. it becomes F/9.
13. What does the graph of gravitational force versus distance show?
It shows a curve in which the force falls rapidly as distance increases: F at r, F/4 at 2r and F/9 at 3r.
14. What is the relation between the gravitational forces F12 and F21 acting on two masses?
F12 = −F21. The two forces are equal in magnitude but opposite in direction.
15. How is Newton's law of gravitation consistent with Newton's third law of motion?
• Show that gravitational forces form an action-reaction pair.
The force on m2 due to m1 (F21) and the force on m1 due to m2 (F12) are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, F12 = −F21. Hence they act like action and reaction, which is Newton's third law.
16. Why do we not notice the gravitational attraction between ordinary objects like ships and buildings?
Because G is very small, the force is extremely weak (of the order of 10-7 N for ordinary masses) and goes unnoticed unless highly sensitive instruments are used.
17. Which theory later refined and expanded Newton's law of gravitation?
Einstein's theory of general relativity.
18. Name two natural phenomena explained by Newton's law of gravitation.
The tides caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon, and the orbiting of planets around the Sun.
19. Calculate the force of attraction between two bodies of masses 45 kg and 50 kg placed 1 m apart.
Fg = G m1m2/r2 = (6.673 × 10-11 × 45 × 50)/(1)2 = 1.5 × 10-7 N.
20. The mass of the Earth is 6 × 1024 kg and that of the Moon is 7.4 × 1022 kg. The distance between them is 3.84 × 105 km. Calculate the force exerted by the Earth on the Moon. (Assignment 15.1)
Fg = G MEMM/r2, with r = 3.84 × 108 m.
Fg = (6.67 × 10-11 × 6 × 1024 × 7.4 × 1022)/(3.84 × 108)2
Fg ≈ 2.0 × 1020 N.
PART 2 Gravitational Field Strength

Field Strength and g on the Surface

1. Define gravitational field.
• What is a gravitational field?
The area around a massive object (like the Earth or the Sun) where its gravitational force acts is called its gravitational field.
2. Define gravitational field strength.
• What is gravitational field strength?
The gravitational field strength 'g' at a point is the gravitational force acting on a unit mass placed at that point, g = Fg/m.
3. Write the formula and SI unit of gravitational field strength.
g = Fg/m. Its SI unit is N kg-1 (equivalent to m s-2).
4. Is gravitational field strength a scalar or a vector quantity?
It is a vector. Its magnitude is 'g' and its direction is the direction of the gravitational force (towards the source mass).
5. What is the relation between gravitational field strength and acceleration due to gravity?
From Newton's second law a = F/m, so ag = Fg/m = g. Hence gravitational field strength and acceleration due to gravity are equivalent.
6. Derive the formula for g on the surface of the Earth.
• Show that g = GME/RE2.
The force between a stone of mass m0 and the Earth is Fg = G m0ME/RE2.
By Newton's second law, Fg = W = m0g.
Comparing: m0g = G m0ME/RE2, so g = G ME/RE2.
7. What is the value of g on the surface of the Earth? Which values are used to get it?
Using ME = 6 × 1024 kg, RE = 6.4 × 106 m and G = 6.67 × 10-11 N m2 kg-2, g = 9.77 m s-2 ≈ 9.8 m s-2.
8. Does the value of g depend on the mass of the falling body?
• Why do light and heavy bodies fall at the same rate?
No. In g = GME/RE2 the mass m0 of the body cancels out, so light and heavy bodies fall at the same rate.
9. On what factors does the gravitational field strength of the Earth depend?
Only on the mass of the Earth (ME) and the radius of the Earth (RE).
10. Write the general expression for gravitational field strength on the surface of any planet.
g = G m/r2, where m is the mass of the planet and r is its radius.
11. Why does our weight change on another planet?
Because weight W = mg and g on a planet depends upon the planet's mass and radius.
12. Calculate the free-fall acceleration on the surface of Jupiter (m = 1.898 × 1027 kg, r = 7.15 × 107 m).
gJ = G mJ/rJ2 = (6.67 × 10-11 × 1.898 × 1027)/(7.15 × 107)2
gJ = 24.77 m s-2, which is about 2.528 times g on Earth.
13. Find the weight of a 60 kg astronaut on Jupiter and on Earth.
On Jupiter: W = m gJ = 60 × 24.77 = 1486.2 N.
On Earth: W = 588.6 N.
14. The gravitational field strength on the surface of the Moon is 1.6 N kg-1 and its mass is 7.3 × 1022 kg. What is its radius? (Assignment 15.3)
From g = G m/r2, r = √(G m/g)
r = √((6.67 × 10-11 × 7.3 × 1022)/1.6)
r ≈ 1.74 × 106 m.

Variation of g with Altitude

1. Write the expression for the acceleration due to gravity at a height h above the Earth's surface.
gh = G ME/(RE + h)2, which can be written as gh = g RE2/(RE + h)2.
2. Show that gh = gRE2/(RE + h)2.
gh = GME/(RE+h)2, and on the surface g = GME/RE2, i.e. GME = gRE2.
Putting GME = gRE2 in gh gives gh = gRE2/(RE+h)2.
3. How does 'g' vary with altitude?
As we go further away from the centre or surface of the Earth, the value of g decreases.
4. Why is g approximately constant for small changes in height near the Earth's surface?
Because for small h (h ≪ RE), (RE + h)2 is almost equal to RE2, so gh ≈ g. For example, g is still 9.8 m s-2 at the height of Mount Everest (8.8 km).
5. Why is the change in g noticeable only at very long distances?
Because the Earth's radius (6.4 × 106 m) is very large compared to ordinary heights. Noticeable change appears only at large altitudes, e.g. 8.7 m s-2 at 400 km and 0.2 m s-2 at 35,700 km.
6. State the value of g at 400 km (space shuttle orbit) and at 35,700 km (communication satellites).
gh = 8.7 m s-2 at 400 km and about 0.2 m s-2 at 35,700 km (Table 15.1).
7. What is the gravitational field strength at 120 km above the Earth's surface?
gh = gRE2/(RE+h)2 = 9.8 × (6.4 × 106)2/(6.4 × 106 + 0.12 × 106)2
gh = 9.4 m s-2, slightly less than its surface value.
8. What is the value of g at 35,700 km above the Earth's surface, where geostationary satellites orbit? (Assignment 15.2)
gh = gRE2/(RE+h)2 = 9.8 × (6.4 × 106)2/(6.4 × 106 + 35.7 × 106)2
gh ≈ 0.23 m s-2 (about 0.2 m s-2).

Gravitational Field Lines

1. What are gravitational field lines?
Lines used to represent a gravitational field; they indicate the direction and strength of gravity.
2. What does the closeness of gravitational field lines indicate?
Closer lines represent a stronger field, while lines farther apart indicate a weaker field.
3. How do gravitational field lines look for a point mass?
They point radially inward, uniformly in all directions, forming a symmetric pattern.
4. What is a point mass?
A point mass is a body treated as if all its mass is concentrated at a single point in space.
5. What is a uniform sphere?
A sphere that has a consistent (uniform) density throughout.
6. How does the gravitational field of a uniform sphere behave at points outside the sphere?
It acts as if all the mass were concentrated at the centre, like a point mass of the same total mass.
7. Why can the Earth's mass be treated as concentrated at its centre for points outside its surface?
Because the field outside a uniform sphere is the same as that of a point mass at its centre; this simplifies calculations without considering differences in density.
8. How does the gravitational field strength change inside a uniform sphere?
It increases steadily toward the surface, because only the mass within the radius affects the field at any point inside.
9. Why does the field strength reduce rapidly as one moves away from the Earth?
Because of the inverse-square nature of Newton's law of gravitation, g ∝ 1/r2.
PART 3 Satellites and Orbits

Satellites, Launching and Orbital Velocity

1. Define a satellite.
A satellite is any object that orbits a planet due to the force of gravity, maintaining a stable path around it.
2. What is a natural satellite? Give an example.
A natural body orbiting a planet, dwarf planet or minor planet, where the larger body's gravity dominates the system, is a natural satellite. Moons are examples.
3. What are artificial satellites?
Artificial satellites are objects intentionally placed into orbit around the Earth or other celestial bodies.
4. When was the first artificial satellite launched?
In 1957.
5. Name three types of artificial satellites.
Communication satellites, weather satellites and navigation satellites (also used for military operations and scientific research).
6. What is the International Space Station (ISS)?
A space craft capable of supporting a crew, designed to remain in space for an extended period; it is the largest satellite in orbit and can be seen with the naked eye.
7. How does a satellite stay in orbit without an engine?
It is held in place by the gravitational pull of the Earth.
8. How is a satellite launched into orbit?
It is moved to a high altitude and then accelerated to a sufficiently high tangential speed with the help of a spacecraft (e.g. a rocket).
9. What happens if a satellite is launched with too low a speed? What if the speed is too high?
If the speed is too low, the satellite falls back to Earth. If it is too high, it moves in an elliptical orbit or escapes Earth's gravity (escape speed). With the correct speed it moves in a circular orbit.
10. Why are satellites usually put into circular orbits?
Because circular (or nearly circular) orbits require the least energy.
11. Define orbital velocity.
The orbital velocity of a satellite is the velocity required for it to orbit the Earth (or another body) at a specific altitude; in a circular orbit it is a constant tangential speed.
12. Which force provides the centripetal force for a satellite in a circular orbit?
The force of gravity, so the centripetal acceleration ac equals g at that height.
13. Derive the expression for the orbital velocity of a satellite.
Centripetal acceleration ac = vo2/r is supplied by gravity: ac = g, so g = vo2/r and vo = √(gr).
Putting g = GME/r2: vo = √(GME/r).
14. What does 'r' represent in vo = √(GME/r)? Write vo in terms of height h.
r is the distance from the centre of the Earth to the satellite, r = RE + h, so vo = √(GME/(RE + h)).
15. On what factors does orbital velocity depend?
On the mass of the larger body (e.g. Earth) and on the distance from its centre to the satellite; it does not depend on the satellite's mass.
16. Where does a satellite move faster, near the Earth or far from it?
A satellite moves faster when it is close to the gravitating body and slower when it is farther away.
17. What is the orbital velocity of a satellite just above the Earth's surface (r = RE = 6.4 × 106 m)?
vo = √(GME/RE) ≈ 7.9 km s-1.
18. Calculate the orbital velocity of the ISS orbiting at 400 km above the Earth's surface.
vo = √(GME/(RE+h)) = √((6.67 × 10-11 × 6 × 1024)/(6.4 × 106 + 4 × 105))
vo ≈ 7.67 × 103 m s-1 ≈ 7.7 km s-1 (the book rounds it to 7.66 km s-1).
19. How many orbits does the ISS complete in 24 hours?
About 16 orbits, so it experiences 16 sunrises and sunsets per day.
20. Calculate the orbital velocity of the Earth around the Sun (Ms = 1.99 × 1030 kg, r = 1.5 × 1011 m).
vo = √(GMs/r) = √(6.67 × 10-11 × 1.99 × 1030/(1.5 × 1011))
vo ≈ 30 km s-1. So everyone sitting on the Earth is moving at about 30 km s-1 around the Sun.
21. Calculate the orbital speed of a satellite orbiting the Earth at an altitude equal to the Earth's radius. (Assignment 15.4)
r = RE + h = 2RE = 1.28 × 107 m
vo = √(GME/r) = √(6.67 × 10-11 × 6 × 1024/(1.28 × 107))
vo ≈ 5.6 × 103 m s-1 = 5.6 km s-1.
PART 4 Geostationary Satellites

Geostationary Orbit and the Hill Sphere

1. Define a geostationary satellite.
• What is a geosynchronous satellite?
A satellite that takes exactly 24 hours to circle the Earth, so that it remains stationary above a fixed point on Earth, is called a geostationary (or geosynchronous) satellite.
2. What is a geostationary orbit?
The orbit of a geostationary satellite, lying directly above the equator at an altitude where its orbital period matches the Earth's rotation period.
3. State the characteristics of a geostationary satellite.
Its orbital period is 24 hours, it orbits from west to east, it lies directly above the Equator and it remains at the same point above the Earth's surface.
4. Why does a geostationary satellite appear stationary?
Because its orbital period equals the Earth's rotation period (24 h), so it moves with the Earth relative to a fixed point on the surface.
5. Derive the expression for the orbital radius of a geostationary satellite.
Orbital speed vo = √(GME/r) and also vo = 2πr/T.
Equating: 2πr/T = √(GME/r). Squaring and rearranging: r3 = GMET2/(4π2).
Taking the cube root: r = (GMET2/(4π2))1/3.
6. Calculate the orbital radius of a geostationary satellite.
r = (GMET2/(4π2))1/3 = (6.67 × 10-11 × 6 × 1024 × (86400)2/(4 × (3.14)2))1/3
r = 4.23 × 107 m = 4.23 × 104 km (from the centre of the Earth).
7. Calculate the orbital speed of a geostationary satellite.
vo = √(GME/r) = √(6.67 × 10-11 × 6 × 1024/(4.23 × 107))
vo = 3.07 × 103 m s-1 = 3.07 km s-1.
8. Calculate the height above the Earth's surface of a geostationary satellite. (Assignment 15.5)
h = r − RE = 4.23 × 107 − 6.4 × 106 m
h ≈ 3.59 × 107 m ≈ 3.6 × 104 km (about 35,700 km in the book's table).
9. What is the time period of a geostationary satellite?
T = 24 hours = 86400 s.
10. What is the minimum number of geostationary satellites needed for complete global coverage?
Three.
11. Why can a lunar-stationary satellite (stationary relative to the Moon) not keep a stable orbit?
The required orbital radius for a 27.3-day period is about 88,417 km, which lies outside the Moon's Hill sphere (about 60,000 km), so Earth's gravity would destabilise the orbit.
12. What is a Hill sphere?
The region around a celestial body where its own gravitational pull dominates over that of a larger body.
13. Venus has mass 4.867 × 1024 kg and period 243 days. Find the radius of a synchronous satellite for Venus.
T = 243 days = 2.093 × 107 s
r = (GMvT2/(4π2))1/3 = 1.53 × 109 m = 1.53 × 106 km.
14. Why is a synchronous orbit around Venus not feasible even though the formula gives a value?
Because Venus rotates very slowly and has lower mass than the Earth, so its Hill sphere is much smaller; 1,530,000 km lies outside it and the Sun's gravity would make the orbit unstable.
PART 5 Gravitational Potential and Potential Energy

Potential Energy and Potential

1. Why is the formula ΔP.E = mgh not valid far from the Earth's surface?
Because it assumes g is constant; as we move away from the surface gravity weakens, so an expression based on Newton's law of gravitation must be used.
2. What is the zero-reference point for gravitational potential energy?
Infinity: the point 'n' is taken at r = ∞, where the potential energy is zero.
3. Why is gravitational potential energy negative at a finite distance?
Because the zero is chosen at infinity, and potential energy decreases as objects move closer to the zero-reference point (and increases as they move apart).
4. Why is the work W1 in the derivation taken as negative?
Because the displacement is outward while the gravitational force is inward, so θ = 180° and cos 180° = −1, giving W1 = −FavΔr.
5. What approximation is made in finding the average distance rav in the derivation?
Δr is very small, so (Δr)2 is neglected, giving rav2 = REr1.
6. Define gravitational potential energy 'U' (absolute potential energy).
The potential energy possessed by a body in a gravitational field with respect to the reference point of zero potential (at infinity) is called absolute gravitational potential energy.
7. Write the expression for the gravitational potential energy of a mass m at distance r from the centre of the Earth.
U = −GMEm/r.
8. How does U vary with distance and what happens as r → ∞?
U increases as the distance increases (becomes less negative) and goes to zero as r approaches infinity.
9. Define gravitational potential.
The gravitational potential V at a point is the work done per unit mass in bringing a small test mass from infinity to that point; it is the gravitational potential energy per unit mass.
10. Write the formula for gravitational potential in terms of potential energy.
V = U/m.
11. Write the expression for gravitational potential at a point at distance r from the centre of the Earth.
V = −GME/r.
12. What is the SI unit of gravitational potential?
J kg-1 (equivalent to N m kg-1).
13. Does gravitational potential at a point depend on the mass of the object placed there?
No. V = −GME/r does not depend on the mass of the object.
14. Calculate the gravitational potential at the surface of the Earth.
V = −GME/RE = −(6.67 × 10-11 × 6 × 1024)/(6.4 × 106)
V = −62.3 × 106 J kg-1 = −62.3 MJ kg-1.
15. Are the equations of gravitational potential valid only for the Earth?
No. They are derived for the Earth but are equally valid for other gravitating objects.
16. Find the gravitational potential energy of the Earth with respect to the Sun (Ms = 1.99 × 1030 kg, ME = 6 × 1024 kg, r = 1.5 × 1011 m).
U = −GMsME/r = −(6.67 × 10-11 × 1.99 × 1030 × 6 × 1024)/(1.5 × 1011)
U = −5.29 × 1033 J.
17. Find the gravitational potential due to the Sun at the position of the Earth.
V = −GMs/r = −(6.67 × 10-11 × 1.99 × 1030)/(1.5 × 1011)
V = −8.85 × 108 J kg-1 = −885 MJ kg-1.
18. Calculate the gravitational potential at 1000 km, 50,000 km and 100,000 km from the Earth's surface and compare with mgh. (Assignment 15.6)
V = −GME/(RE+h): at 1000 km, V ≈ −54.1 MJ kg-1; at 50,000 km, V ≈ −7.10 MJ kg-1; at 100,000 km, V ≈ −3.76 MJ kg-1.
Change in V from the surface: +8.45, +55.4 and +58.8 MJ kg-1. The formula gh gives 9.8, 490 and 980 MJ kg-1.
So gh agrees only roughly at 1000 km (small h) and fails badly at large heights, because g is not constant there.

Multiple Choice Questions

1. Two identical balls of masses 1 kg each having distance of 1 m between their centres: gravitational force between them is? (A) 667×10-9 N (B) 6.67×10-11 N (C) 667×1011 N (D) 6.67×10-13 N
Correct option: B. F = G m1m2/r2 = 6.67 × 10-11 × 1 × 1/1 = 6.67 × 10-11 N.
2. Gravitational force between two objects is 'F'. If the masses are doubled and the distance between centres is halved, the force is: (A) F (B) 4F (C) F/4 (D) 16F
Correct option: D. New force F′ = G(2m1)(2m2)/(r/2)2 = 4 × 4 × F = 16F.
3. The value of 'g' at a height of 1500 km above the Earth's surface in m s-2 is: (A) 0 (B) 6.4 (C) 9.8 (D) 12.2
Correct option: B. gh = gRE2/(RE+h)2 = 9.8 × (6400)2/(7900)2 ≈ 6.4 m s-2.
4. Orbital velocity to launch a satellite in a circular orbit just above the surface (r = RE = 6.4 × 106 m) is: (A) 0 (B) 1.19 km s-1 (C) 3.07 km s-1 (D) 7.9 km s-1
Correct option: D. vo = √(GME/RE) ≈ 7.9 km s-1.
5. When a satellite is put into a higher circular orbit, its kinetic energy: (A) increases (B) decreases (C) is zero (D) remains the same
Correct option: B. In a higher orbit r is larger, so vo = √(GM/r) is smaller and kinetic energy (½mv2) decreases.
6. The orbital velocity of a geostationary satellite is: (A) 0 (B) 1.19 km s-1 (C) 3.07 km s-1 (D) 7.9 km s-1
Correct option: C. vo = 3.07 km s-1 at r = 4.23 × 107 m.

Correct option: C. Three satellites are needed.
8. When an object moves away from a massive body, its gravitational potential energy: (A) increases (B) decreases (C) remains constant (D) becomes zero
Correct option: A. U = −GMm/r becomes less negative (increases) as r increases.
9. The gravitational potential at an altitude of 35,700 km above the Earth's surface is approximately: (A) −94.7 MJ kg-1 (B) −62.3 MJ kg-1 (C) +947.11 J kg-1 (D) −947.11 J kg-1
Correct option: A (nearest given option). V = −GME/(RE+h) = −(6.67 × 10-11 × 6 × 1024)/(4.21 × 107) ≈ −9.5 × 106 J kg-1.

Short Questions

1. Why can gravitation not account for the formation of molecules?
Gravitational forces between atoms and molecules are extremely weak because their masses are tiny and G is very small (6.67 × 10-11). Molecules are held together by much stronger electric forces.
2. Why don't two books on your desk attract each other gravitationally, despite Newton's law of gravitation?
They do attract, but the masses are small and G is very small, so the force is far too weak (about 10-7 N or less) to be noticed against friction and other forces.
3. Why does an apple fall towards the Earth due to gravity, while the Earth doesn't move towards the apple?
The forces are equal and opposite (action-reaction), but a = F/m. The apple's mass is very small, so its acceleration is large, while the Earth's enormous mass gives it a negligible acceleration.
4. Can gravitational field strength be negative? Explain.
Its magnitude cannot be negative. Gravity is always attractive, so g always points towards the source mass; a negative sign only indicates this inward direction, not a negative magnitude.
5. What factors determine the strength of the gravitational field around a planet?
The mass of the planet and the distance from its centre: g = Gm/r2.
6. If two planets have the same mass but different radii, how would their gravitational field strengths compare?
Since g = Gm/r2, the planet with the smaller radius has the stronger field; g is inversely proportional to the square of the radius.
7. Why do satellites in higher orbits have lower orbital velocities?
Because vo = √(GM/r): as the orbital radius r increases, gravity (and the required centripetal acceleration) becomes weaker, so the orbital velocity decreases.
8. How does the mass of the Earth affect the orbital velocity required for a satellite to stay in orbit?
From vo = √(GME/r), orbital velocity is directly proportional to the square root of the Earth's mass: a larger mass needs a larger orbital velocity at the same r.
9. Is it possible for an object's gravitational potential energy to become negative? What does this mean for the object's motion?
Yes, U = −GMm/r is negative at any finite distance because the zero is at infinity. It means the object is bound to the larger body; energy must be supplied to move it away to infinity, where U = 0.
10. How is the gravitational potential energy of two point masses related to the concept of gravitational potential?
Gravitational potential is potential energy per unit mass, V = U/m. For two point masses, U = −GMm/r, so V = −GM/r and U = mV.

Comprehensive Questions

1. What is the force of gravity? State and explain Newton's law of gravitation. Also show that it is consistent with Newton's third law of motion.
Force of gravity: the force of attraction between any two objects in the universe.
Law: Every object in the universe attracts every other object with a force which is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres.
Explanation: For masses m1 and m2 at distance r, Fg ∝ m1m2 and Fg ∝ 1/r2. Combining, Fg ∝ m1m2/r2. Replacing proportionality with a constant, Fg = G m1m2/r2, where G = 6.67 × 10-11 N m2 kg-2. The force is always attractive and does not depend on the medium.
Consistency with the third law: the force on m2 due to m1 is F21 and the force on m1 due to m2 is F12. They are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, F12 = −F21, so they form an action-reaction pair as in Newton's third law.
2. What are gravitational field and gravitational field strength? Explain. Derive the formula for gravitational acceleration 'g' on the surface of the Earth and discuss the variation of 'g' with altitude.
Gravitational field: the area around a massive object where its gravitational force acts. Gravitational field strength 'g': the gravitational force per unit mass at a point, g = Fg/m (a vector in the direction of the force, equal to the acceleration due to gravity).
Derivation: for a stone of mass m0 on the Earth's surface, Fg = G m0ME/RE2 and Fg = W = m0g. Equating, g = GME/RE2 = 9.8 m s-2. It is independent of m0.
Variation with altitude: at height h, gh = GME/(RE+h)2. Since GME = gRE2, gh = gRE2/(RE+h)2. As h increases, gh decreases (e.g. 9.8 at Everest, 8.7 at 400 km, 0.2 at 35,700 km); the change is noticeable only at very large distances.
3. How do engineers calculate the required velocity for a satellite to be placed into a specific orbit? Describe the steps involved in determining the orbital parameters necessary for a successful satellite launch and deployment.
Step 1: Decide the orbit height h and find the orbital radius r = RE + h.
Step 2: Gravity supplies the centripetal force, so ac = vo2/r = g, giving vo = √(GME/r).
Step 3: Substitute G, ME and r to find the required orbital velocity (e.g. 7.66 km s-1 at 400 km).
Step 4: Move the satellite to the required altitude and accelerate it with a rocket to the required tangential speed.
Step 5: Check the speed: too low and it falls back, too high and it moves in an ellipse or escapes; the correct speed gives a circular orbit, which needs the least energy.
Step 6: For a 24-hour orbit use r = (GMET2/(4π2))1/3, where T is the required period.
4. What are geostationary satellites? Calculate the orbital radius 'r', orbital speed 'vo' and height 'h' above the surface of the Earth for the geostationary orbit.
Geostationary satellites orbit directly above the equator with a period of 24 hours (west to east), so they stay above a fixed point on the Earth.
Radius: vo = 2πr/T = √(GME/r) gives r = (GMET2/(4π2))1/3 = (6.67 × 10-11 × 6 × 1024 × (86400)2/(4 × 3.142))1/3 = 4.23 × 107 m.
Speed: vo = √(GME/r) = 3.07 × 103 m s-1.
Height: h = r − RE = 4.23 × 107 − 6.4 × 106 ≈ 3.59 × 107 m (about 36,000 km).
5. If ME is the mass of the Earth, r the distance of a unit mass from the Earth's centre and G the gravitational constant, prove that gravitational potential V = −GME/r.
Move a mass m from the surface (RE) outward in small steps Δr so that the force is nearly constant in each step. By the work-energy principle, ΔP.E = Wnet = W1 + W2 + … + Wn.
For one step, W1 = FavΔr cos 180° = −FavΔr, with Fav = GMEm/rav2. Since Δr is very small, (Δr)2 is neglected, so rav2 = REr1 and Δr = r1 − RE.
Hence W1 = −GMEm (1/RE − 1/r1). Similarly W2 = −GMEm (1/r1 − 1/r2), and so on up to Wn = −GMEm (1/rn-1 − 1/rn).
Adding all, the middle terms cancel: ΔP.E = −GMEm (1/RE − 1/rn).
Take rn = ∞, so 1/∞ = 0 and the general potential energy is U = −GMEm/r.
Gravitational potential is energy per unit mass: V = U/m = −GME/r. (Proved)

Numerical Problems

1. Find the mass of the Earth by considering a small object placed on its surface such that the distance between their centres is the Earth's radius, 6.4 × 106 m.
g = GME/RE2, so ME = gRE2/G = 9.8 × (6.4 × 106)2/(6.67 × 10-11)
ME ≈ 6.0 × 1024 kg.
(The book's key prints 6.4 × 1024 kg, but the working gives 6 × 1024 kg, the value used throughout the chapter.)
2. Alpha Centauri A (2.19 × 1030 kg) and B (1.80 × 1030 kg) attract each other with a force of 2.24 × 1025 N. What is the distance between the two stars?
F = Gm1m2/r2, so r = √(Gm1m2/F)
r = √(6.67 × 10-11 × 2.19 × 1030 × 1.80 × 1030/(2.24 × 1025))
r ≈ 3.43 × 1012 m.
3. What will be the value of 'g' on an exoplanet whose mass is five times the mass of the Earth and whose radius is twice the radius of the Earth?
g′ = G(5ME)/(2RE)2 = (5/4) × GME/RE2 = 1.25 g
g′ = 1.25 × 9.8 = 12.25 m s-2.
4. The Hubble Space Telescope is in a circular orbit 613 km above the Earth's surface (RE = 6.4 × 106 m, ME = 6 × 1024 kg). (a) What is its speed? (b) What is the period of its orbit?
r = RE + h = 6.4 × 106 + 6.13 × 105 = 7.013 × 106 m
(a) vo = √(GME/r) = √(6.67 × 10-11 × 6 × 1024/(7.013 × 106)) ≈ 7.55 × 103 m s-1 (book: 7550 m s-1)
(b) T = 2πr/vo ≈ 5.8 × 103 s (book: 5807 s; small difference is due to rounding of constants).
5. To launch a satellite around Mars at an altitude of 300 km above its surface: Mars has mass 6.42 × 1023 kg and radius 3.39 × 106 m. Calculate its orbital velocity.
r = 3.39 × 106 + 3 × 105 = 3.69 × 106 m
vo = √(GM/r) = √(6.67 × 10-11 × 6.42 × 1023/(3.69 × 106))
vo ≈ 3.4 × 103 m s-1 = 3.4 km s-1.
6. An asteroid orbits the Sun at a distance of 8.35 × 1011 m. (a) How fast must it travel to maintain its circular orbit? (b) How long will it take to orbit the Sun?
(a) vo = √(GMs/r) = √(6.67 × 10-11 × 1.99 × 1030/(8.35 × 1011)) ≈ 1.26 × 104 m s-1
(b) T = 2πr/vo = 2π × 8.35 × 1011/(1.26 × 104) ≈ 4.2 × 108 s ≈ 13.2 years.
7. Mars has a mass of 6.42 × 1023 kg and a period of 88,642 s. What would be the radius of a stationary satellite for this planet?
r = (GMT2/(4π2))1/3 = (6.67 × 10-11 × 6.42 × 1023 × (88642)2/(4π2))1/3
r ≈ 2.04 × 107 m ≈ 20,400 km (book: 20,428 km).
8. Calculate the potential energy of the Moon (mass 7.35 × 1022 kg) relative to the Earth (6 × 1024 kg) if the distance between their centres is 3.94 × 105 km.
U = −GMEm/r = −(6.67 × 10-11 × 6 × 1024 × 7.35 × 1022)/(3.94 × 108)
U ≈ −7.47 × 1028 J, i.e. about −7.4 × 1028 J (as in the book).
9. A 50 kg weather satellite orbits the Earth at an altitude of 1000 km and a similar 50 kg communication satellite at 37,000 km. Calculate the difference in their gravitational potential energies.
r1 = 6.4 × 106 + 1 × 106 = 7.4 × 106 m and r2 = 6.4 × 106 + 3.7 × 107 = 4.34 × 107 m
ΔU = U2 − U1 = GMEm (1/r1 − 1/r2) = 6.67 × 10-11 × 6 × 1024 × 50 × (1/(7.4 × 106) − 1/(4.34 × 107))
ΔU ≈ 2.2 × 109 J = 2.2 GJ.
10. What is the change in gravitational potential energy of a 64.5 kg astronaut lifted from the Earth's surface into a circular orbit of altitude 4.40 × 102 km?
ΔU = GMEm (1/RE − 1/(RE+h)) with RE+h = 6.84 × 106 m
ΔU = 6.67 × 10-11 × 6 × 1024 × 64.5 × (1/(6.4 × 106) − 1/(6.84 × 106))
ΔU ≈ 2.6 × 108 J.
